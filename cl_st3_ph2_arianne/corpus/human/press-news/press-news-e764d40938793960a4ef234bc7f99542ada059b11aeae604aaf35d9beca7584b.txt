The unprecedented situation we are facing at the moment is still unfolding , and much will depend on what happens next . <h> Benefits need to be sustainable <p> Let 's take a look back to recent history . <p> In 1997 the international Kyoto Protocol was drawn up , which saw many countries commit to reducing greenhouse gas emissions , and its first commitment period began in 2008 . <p> Hitting emissions targets for a short-term period is nowhere near as important as engineering a society and economy that can sustainably and prosperously run off low or zero net emissions into the future . <p> This goal has been embraced across Australia . All of our states and territories have committed to a net zero economy by 2050 , and a nationwide target , supported by many including the Business Council of Australia , was being debated right before COVID-19 effectively bookmarked the issue . <p> While some people have argued that we need to either stop focusing on economic growth or at least decouple growth from fossil fuel emissions , shutting down the economy and grounding people indoors is neither a desirable nor sustainable way to do this . <p> COVID-19 response measures have been extreme and might be considered " draconian " if we were n't facing a public health catastrophe to justify them . <p> Through this and the country 's bushfire crisis that preceded it , Australians have already shown a willingness to buy locally , supporting farmers and neighbourhood suppliers . <p> Along with hand sanitiser and toilet paper , the likes of vegetable seeds , craft materials , sourdough starter , and humble forms of entertainment like puzzles and board games , have experienced a dramatic surge in popularity as people reconnect with forgotten notions of self-sufficiency and simplicity . <p> With many shops closed , people are purchasing pre-loved goods from those in their local community through online marketplaces . <p> Through this , we have already overcome an important barrier to behaviour change . <p> Business looks very different to what it did only six months ago , and business-as-usual is about as stubborn as it comes . <p> We can absolutely take advantage of this shift . <p> The government faces a pivot point where it can choose to move away from fossil fuel dependency . Picture : Getty Images <h> A pivot point for the clean energy transition <p> After having broken down , we get to rebuild , and we have choices in the way that we do this . <p> Some of these will be dictated by the need to get essential services back up and running as soon as possible . But as governments devise economic stimulus packages , they can harness this opportunity to foster the dual benefit of accelerating the transition to a cleaner economy needed to address climate change . <p> Some measures that have been suggested include taking advantage of low oil prices to remove fossil fuel subsidies ; or providing guarantees to private investors wishing to use low interest rates to invest in renewable technologies that need greater investment , like hydrogen . <p> The International Energy Agency , which is preparing a special report to offer guidance to governments , has warned that we could ' compound the tragedy ' if we let the measures we take now hinder the clean energy transition , when they could have advanced it . <p> This is a critical message -- experts have calculated that the damage to the Australian economy from climate change could be $A762 billion by 2050 . <p> The government is now facing an unexpected potential pivot point where it can effectively choose to lock in a degree of fossil fuel dependency into the future or move away from it . <h> A pathway with co-benefits <p> Rather than looking at 2020 as the manifestation of two separate crises , we can look at it from the perspective of experiencing interconnected events . <p> The global response to COVID-19 has already overcome an important barrier to behaviour change . Picture : Getty Images <p> Social , economic and environmental systems are inextricably linked , and major disruptions to these systems rarely occur as isolated events . <p> Climate change could increase the likelihood of more wildlife-human disease transfers in future . The need to prioritise hard and fast short-term action will challenge how well we can think ahead to flow-on , compound effects that threaten the economy and society over a longer timescale . <p> Although trade-offs are inevitable in responding to complex problems , key decision-makers need to look for pathways with co-benefits . <p> The global response to COVID-19 has already exhibited environmental benefits . <p> But the simple answer to whether the pandemic will have long-term positive effects depends on whether we choose to harness critical opportunities to promote individual and collective behaviour change , and to foster rather than sideline the clean energy transition . <h> Privacy Statement <p> The University of Melbourne ( University ) collects , uses , handles and discloses personal information in accordance with the Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014 ( Vic ) ( Act ) and other applicable legislation . <p> This Privacy Statement relates only to the collection of personal information in relation to the Pursuit Website .
