In the coming two decades , however , BRI countries ' GDP average growth rate is expected to be twice that of the OECD , and investments in BRI countries are likely to comprise more than half of total investment worldwide . <p> In the worst-case scenario , the BRI countries could account for over half of global CO2 emissions by 2050 . <p> That possibility -- set out in a forthcoming study that we co-authored -- assumes that BRI economies stick to their current carbon-intensity trajectory , while the rest of the world reduces its emissions in line with the Paris climate agreement . <h> READ MORE <p> In other words , if not managed in a climate-conscious way , development in the BRI countries would undercut efforts to meet the world 's climate goals . <p> This is a risk that needs to be taken seriously . Moreover , these countries hold many of their carbon-intensive assets on public balance sheets , which are less likely to become " stranded assets . " <p> In order to ensure that development in the BRI countries does not undermine the global climate agenda , meaningful steps must be taken to reduce substantially the carbon footprint of new investments in these economies . <p> The window for action is narrow : investment decisions made in the coming few years will determine the carbon intensity of critical infrastructure and major real-estate assets that will operate for decades . <p> By linking policy , finance , and the international community 's expertise and technological resources , it is possible to lay the groundwork for low-carbon development in the BRI economies . To that end , three focused and interconnected actions should be pursued . <p> First , global investors must be convinced to adopt @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Such an effort could include promoting the Green Investment Principles for the Belt and Road , introduced by China 's Green Finance Committee and the City of London last November , in cities like London , Hong Kong , New York , and Singapore -- the world 's principle sources of capital . <p> Requiring investors to disclose the carbon footprint of BRI infrastructure projects , rather than just the risk climate change poses to investments , would also make a difference . <p> Second , China could take more aggressive action to promote low-carbon investments in BRI countries , in line with the government 's high-level commitments to advance sustainable development and its international climate leadership . <p> For example , China could require all BRI investments by Chinese financial institutions and non-financial corporations to adhere visibly to specific green standards . <p> This could be complemented by the creation of a low-carbon compact that includes leading Chinese and international companies carrying out BRI projects and providing green technologies to participating countries . <p> Finally , international organisations should increase their support to BRI countries for greening infrastructure development @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ advance frameworks for green finance policy and increase their capacity for green public procurement . <p> After all , while most BRI infrastructure investments still rely on international financing , over time , larger BRI countries ' financial systems will increasingly shape the carbon intensity of domestic investments . <p> The BRI has the potential to invigorate developing economies and raise incomes for many millions of people . But we need to make sure that the benefits are not offset by unmitigated climate change . <p> Now is the moment for China and the international community to work together to take concrete action to ensure that BRI investments are climate friendly . <p> Ma Jun , a former chief economist of the People 's Bank of China , is Director of the Centre for Finance and Development at Tsinghua University and Chairman of China 's Green Finance Committee . <p> Simon Zadek is Senior Visiting Fellow at the Centre for Finance and Development at Tsinghua University .
