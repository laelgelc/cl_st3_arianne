More units come in from emitters , who get them by buying them from businesses with a surplus - such as forest owners - or from the Government . Forest owners also have to surrender units for deforestation . <p> In the early years of the scheme , units could also be bought from overseas . Some of the overseas units were dubious and very cheap . <p> In the 2008-2012 period , we had 122.9m Kyoto units . <p> The result was that after meeting our obligations - even though our gross emissions had actually increased - we had 123.7m units left over to go towards future obligations . <p> GOING AROUND AGAIN <p> These units may well be crucial in meeting our commitments in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ in 2020 . <p> New Zealand 's aim in this period is to reduce emissions to 5 per cent below 1990 . <p> For the full eight years , our total emissions are projected to be 655.9 Mt CO2-e . That 's 146.1 Mt CO2-e above the target of 509.8 Mt CO2-e . <p> But forestry activities should offset much of the shortfall , with 38 of those units left over from the previous surplus to balance the books . That would leave us 85.7 units in the black . <p> From 2021 , the curve gets steeper . New Zealand is looking to reduce emissions by 30 per cent below 2005 levels by 2030 . That target is equivalent to 11 per cent below 1990 levels by 2030 . <p> How that might be achieved is supposedly being hammered out by the review now under way . MfE has some concerns . <p> The bottom line is that it is worried that unless we do things differently , the Government - and therefore the taxpayer - could be on the hook for a total of $3.75-7.5 billion to pay for international units .
