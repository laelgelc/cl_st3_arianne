<h> Climate change projects are n't working because communities are left out <p> Nigeria recently began a fresh effort to ascertain its climate change obligations under the Paris Agreement - a global action plan to put the world on track by limiting global warming to below 2 ? C. However , for its programmes to succeed transparency is crucial . <p> This means the programmes must manage and publish information on actions and processes so that it 's accessible , timely and accurate . This in turn builds trust and ownership between those that control the finances and with the communities who are directly affected by climate change initiatives . <p> I did a study on community experiences in Nigeria 's Cross River State with an ongoing climate fund project called Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation ( REDD+ ) . It supports national governments in sustainable land uses and better stewardship of forest resources among the people , particularly those who live on the forest edge . If they did n't , communities become suspicious and wo n't engage with the conservation efforts . <h> Safeguarding forests and livelihoods <p> One important initiative of REDD+ is its social safeguard scheme which directly targets forest-edge communities . <p> It provides monetary compensation ( carbon credits ) for restrictions imposed on income they could 've derived from the forest -- this includes forgoing their rights to emit certain amounts of carbon dioxide ( eg. from cutting down trees or burning wood for fuel ) from their livelihood activities which depend heavily on forest resources . <p> This is where issues of transparency occur . REDD+ funds flow from the United Nations to the government . They are then held in a trust on behalf of the forest communities . Disbursement is usually done through a specialised local agency or ministry . In most countries , the local agency is usually the ministry or a special commission in charge of forest . The agency works with representatives of the forest communities known as the Forest Community Association . <p> Initial results suggest communities are n't @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ in terms of carbon credits or the provision of alternative livelihoods . <h> Apprehensive communities <p> The source of apprehension was a lack of inclusion . They were n't actively engaged with REDD+ officials and did n't have access to information . This fuelled the rumour mill within forest communities and led to allegations of bad practices within the REDD+ focal office . <p> Experiences elsewhere show there may be reason to worry . In Kenya , 80% of respondents in an assessment done on the corruption risks in the project expressed concern that funds would be misused by the central government and others involved . <p> So , what can be done to improve transparency ? <p> Firstly , communication around projects should include all those affected . <p> In Nigeria , for example , workshops organised by institutions that manage climate funds tend to invite those who are seen as educated . There 's the assumption that information would trickle down to the communities on the ground - such as forest communities . In addition , it reflects a lack of consideration towards the communities . Most are living in conditions of poverty in rural areas and are being asked to abandon their forest livelihoods without fulfilling promises on the alternatives promised . <p> Without trusting the process or the alternative income that carbon credits provide , communities will continue to plunder forests , rendering the climate interventions ineffective . <p> Recipients of climate finance also have a responsibility to improve their communication . They should be proactive in producing detailed reports on how they are spending funds entrusted to them . This has not been done sufficiently yet .
