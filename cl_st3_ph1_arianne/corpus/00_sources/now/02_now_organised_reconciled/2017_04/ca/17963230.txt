<p> I lost my Cracker Jack wildlife cards sometime in the 1980s , but the images printed on the cards are still vivid in my mind . <p> The small cards came wrapped in clear plastic and featured a holographic image of a species in danger . I can still see the top drawer of my old desk where I marked pencil lines to keep them organized . <p> I started collecting my cards around the time Canada started to systematically identify species most at risk . Formed in 1977 , the Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada ( COSEWIC ) has provided science-based assessments of wildlife most at risk of being lost . <p> That list has almost doubled since I started working at the Nature Conservancy of Canada ( NCC ) in 2002 . Today , there are 748 species that have been assessed as at risk in Canada by COSEWIC . <p> Part of this steep increase has resulted from more species being assessed . Once listed under SARA , there are efforts to develop a plan to recover or manage the species . <p> The increase in assessments and better tools to identify endangered species definitely account for many " new " species at risk . There are many species common 40 years ago that have genuinely declined . Many bat species are now endangered because the introduction of white nose syndrome has devastated colonies across eastern North America . Birds that were once common , including barn swallow , Sprague 's pipit and eastern whip-poor-will , have declined by over 70 per cent since I was kid in the ' 70s . Nine caribou populations are now at risk . <p> The rapid , steady growth in at-risk species worries me . If we focus our conservation efforts on these " hotspots " we simultaneously protect the habitat of many rare species . ? ? <p> The conservancy has been protecting species-at-risk hotspots for years . Most are within landscapes dominated by human activities where we are continuing to lose coastal shorelines , forests , wetlands and prairie grasslands . By focusing conservation efforts on some of the best habitat remnants and restoring areas around them , land protection and management can reduce key threats to at-risk species and start to help their recovery . <p> In time , their status can be improved , and we can start to see species shift into lower-risk categories or , ideally , be removed from the list . <p> By restoring forests , savannahs and wetlands , the amount of habitat available for at-risk species increases . In some cases we need to help restore populations by reintroducing animals back to properties within their historic range . There are threats from invasive diseases , climate change and poaching . <p> But we do know how to recover endangered species , even when it 's complicated . My old Cracker Jack wildlife cards included osprey . Their numbers had dwindled because persistent pesticides such as DDT made their eggs so fragile they often broke when parents sat on the nest . While never officially listed in Canada , their numbers had plummeted by the ' 70s . Today , I 've stopped pointing out osprey nests in light towers at baseball diamonds to my son . <p> The opportunity to recover Canada 's at-risk wildlife can inspire us to see the world that could be . Habitats that have almost been lost can be restored . Species at the edge of extinction can be gently pulled back .
