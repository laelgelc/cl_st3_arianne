<p> In a previous article I expressed the opinion that Scotland could not have a 100 per cent renewables electricity supply " well before 2030 " . Generating sufficient renewable energy in a relatively low population density country is not technically difficult . It is surety of supply , usefulness , acceptability and cost which matter . <p> Grid system operators can only perform their magic of continuously matching supply to fickle demand if they have access to sufficient dispatchable -- controllable -- generation . Assurance of this is given by the " capacity credit " ( or capacity value ) of the generation method . Cc is the fraction of installed capacity available with high probability at peak demand . Solar is completely absent at winter peak , and tidal stream -- though always there -- occurs in peaks and troughs shifting in a fashion not correlated to the demand cycle . So both are discounted for Cc purposes . <p> For the biomass and hydro stored energy systems we could , for the purposes of the present discussion , reasonably assume 100 per cent availability . But from what I have read , the Cc of a mixed off-and-onshore system in a small country like Scotland at this level would be no higher than about 7 per cent ( which is probably optimistic as UK wind is frequently no more than 1 per cent of its installed capacity ) . <p> So , for our assured 3GW peak we would need an installed capacity of at least 43GW -- which is absurd . In addition to the environmental impact , the capacity required ( and cost ) for security of supply would be 11 times the average consumer demand which actually pays for it . <p> We are not of course " electrically insulated " , but part of the UK National Grid with up to 4GW connectivity . So provided the rest of the UK has an adequate mixed supply , we can have a sensible generation level and import/export to maintain grid stability .
