<h> Charity that ends at home makes us poorer <h> Richard Moore <p> There have been few more inspiring sites in my lifetime than thousands of suburban strangers , marching through the suburbs of Brisbane to help their neighbours after the 2011 floods . After Cyclone Debbie , Queenslanders need to come together again , and Australian and their governments need to support those efforts strongly . <p> The repair bill will be in the billions of dollars but , as a Commonwealth , we have the resources . <p> Many Australians tend to think we spend a huge proportion of our national income on foreign aid . We do n't . Currently , we spend about $1 in every $500 . That is not extravagant ; it is an underinvestment in our own security and prosperity . <p> Politicians like George Christensen need to look beyond their own backyard and see how Australian foreign policy directly affects Australians ' prosperity . Unsurprisingly , Tasmania 's top-five export destinations are all in Asia . <p> It seems strange we need to keep saying it , but we do : Asian development is in Australia 's interests , as is investing in sustaining that growth and development . <p> A big part of our problem in getting this right is popular misunderstanding about foreign aid -- too often spread by those who should know better . It is presented as charity to be dialled up or down , diverted or ended , supposedly with no effect on our interests , let alone our reputation . <p> This is utterly wrong . Most aid is in the form of knowledge , expertise and capacity-building . It helps governments , communities and individuals to manage their own affairs . <p> In doing so , it greatly benefits us . Imagine , in the era of smartphones and cheap travel , what would happen if Indonesia were to experience the sort of social unrest it did in the mid 1960s ; or if there were to be a catastrophic failure of fisheries @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the river . <p> What if drug-resistant tuberculosis became more established in Papua New Guinea and the Torres Strait , or a virulent strain of bird or swine flu spread rapidly through ill-equipped countries in South-East Asia ? <p> What happens overseas affects us . It ca n't be shut out . The sensible strategy is to try to reduce the probability and effects of potential threats before they land on our doorstep . <p> Much of our international aid already goes into supporting regional and global cooperation on problems we ca n't fix by ourselves . Problems such as refugee management , transnational crime and , of course , storm-inducing climate change . <p> This is what rich , confident , outward-looking countries need to be doing . Turning inward will slow global growth , increase distrust , and fuel rivalry and conflict . What it wo n't do is make globalisation go away . <p> The economic , social and technological forces driving international integration may be slowed -- and should certainly be better steered -- but they will not be reversed . We need to make common cause with friends and neighbours . <p> Yes , we also need more emphasis on strengthening Australian communities and supporting local action to address disadvantage and disaster . But we do n't need have to stop there -- and if do we will be poorer for it . <p> The government 's forthcoming foreign policy white paper offers a chance to re-establish the overwhelming significance of regional development and to make us a more active partner in it .
