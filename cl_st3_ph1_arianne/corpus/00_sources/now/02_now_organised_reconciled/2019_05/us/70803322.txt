A new study finds that nearly 60 percent of the fish flesh that feeds bigger fishes and other predators on a reef comes from tiny fishes that stick close to crevices and other hiding places . <p> These tiny species , called cryptobenthic fishes , may not look as if they amount to much among all @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Simon Brandl . But new analyses show that these little species are like snack bowls that get quickly replenished . What keeps up the supply of snack-sized fishes is a stay-near-home tendency among many of their larvae , Brandl and colleagues propose online May 23 in Science . <p> Unlike many larger reef fish species , the young of cryptobenthic fishes are more likely to linger close to their parents ' home reef , the researchers found by combing through decades of data on what species of fish larvae get caught where . Many of the larger reef fishes have young that take longer , dangerous journeys in open water . But the cryptobenthic young stand a better chance of making it to adulthood by sticking close to the reef , where they quickly replace parents that get snacked , the team says . <p> It 's easy to overlook these snack-bowl bits of flesh among all the bigger , showy reef fishes . The majority of these little species are smaller , Brandl says . <p> He and colleagues got a sense of the fishes ' numbers in surveys on reefs in Australia , Belize and French Polynesia . The researchers fenced off sample plots , released clove oil and then collected every fish in a plot that succumbed to the oil 's anesthetic effects . <p> With this and other data , the researchers created computer simulations of spawning , larval return and adult replacement .
