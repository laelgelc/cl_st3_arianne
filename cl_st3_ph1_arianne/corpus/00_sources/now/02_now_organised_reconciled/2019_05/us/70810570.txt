( Beth J. Harpaz/AP ) <p> ByMike Gunter Jr . <p> May 27 <p> Bring on summer -- it 's time to plan your own summer escape . So here 's a vacation challenge : Consider three U.S. destinations that might change your mind-set when it comes to climate change policy . Think of this as a timely quest , to go before they 're gone . <p> When it comes to climate change , conflation of fact and fiction muddy our national discourse . More Americans than ever understand the threats our changing climate presents , even as President Trump continues to cast doubt on its science . That said , these same Americans remain @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ a poll earlier this year by the Associated Press and the University of Chicago . <p> According to this poll , almost 70 percent of Americans said they would not pay as little as $10 a month to reduce rising temperatures . Americans are unwilling to pay to address climate change now , even as costs rise considerably the longer we postpone doing so . <p> My last book , " Tales of an Ecotourist , " tackles this challenge by focusing expressly on how the public accesses information . Exploring five amazing global destinations , it highlights key characteristics of climate change that Americans too often misunderstand -- from the scientific and economic , to the social , cultural and political . The work builds on arguments of 18th century French Enlightenment philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau and 20th century American educator John Dewey to trumpet the power of travel . <p> This approach is all the more relevant in today 's increasingly polarized political climate . Thoughtful travel helps citizens identify their common interests , strengthening our democratic society by highlighting what we all hold dear -- regardless of political @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ So the chance to see changes unfolding within our own country helps make climate change more " sticky , " as Canadian journalist and author Malcolm Gladwell would assert . Such firsthand experiences may transform climate change policies into infectious ideas , becoming more tangible , more personal -- and more relevant . Here are a few suggestions : <p> 1 . St. Augustine , Fla. -- The Sunshine State claims the nation 's oldest city ( technically it 's our oldest continuously inhabited European-established settlement ) . This northeastern Florida tourist destination offers picturesque coquina walls at Castillo de San Marcos National Monument , a charming 144-square-block historic district centered around the bustling pedestrian-only St. George Street , 42 miles of nearby beaches and even the " fountain of youth . " <p> Set along the Atlantic Coast , it 's also one of many Florida locales struggling with " sunny day flooding " -- when seawater , as a result of climate change 's effect on sea levels , spills out from storm water drains and onto city streets . It does n't take a Hurricane Matthew striking @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <p> Monthly high tides do the trick , flooding city streets even on sunny days . According to last year 's National Climate Assessment , by 2045 nearly 64,000 homes in Florida will face similar flooding every other week . <p> 2 . Bar Harbor , Maine -- Shooting up the Eastern Seaboard to Maine and Acadia National Park presents a different challenge tied to climate change . As the historic gateway to Acadia , Bar Harbor is more than a worthy summer destination . If your plans revolve around the Fourth of July , consider shifting it to the top of your list . <p> Indeed , National Geographic ranks Bar Harbor as one of the top 10 local destinations to celebrate Independence Day in the United States . Patriotic festivities begin with an outdoor blueberry pancake breakfast and conclude with a traditional fireworks celebration over Frenchman Bay , with American lobster races and lunches in between . <p> Here 's the catch : The prized American lobster is one of several species in the region affected by climate change . As oceans absorb heat from the warming @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ 0.2 degrees Fahrenheit per decade since 1980 . In the Gulf of Maine , sea surface temperatures are rising at nearly twice this global rate because of a weakening Labrador current from the north and strengthening warm Gulf Stream from the south . <p> What does this mean if you 're a lobster ? American lobsters are migrating northeast to cooler waters at the rate of 43 miles a decade , according to a 2013 Princeton University study . Just ask southern New England about the devastating financial implications -- the lobster industry there has already crashed . <p> More directly to your summer vacation purposes , by the middle of this century our American lobster will require renaming , as it heads north to new habitats . Go now , before celebrating Independence Day in Maine is possible only with Canadian lobsters . <p> 3 . Glacier National Park , Montana -- Skipping across the country to majestic Glacier National Park , more evidence of climate change awaits . Between 2030 and 2080 , that number will shrink to zero . Go now before they are gone , too . <p> See it this summer <p> Travel is no silver bullet when it comes to addressing climate change . Beyond taking care to minimize one 's vacation carbon footprint , visiting some places runs the risk of adding to the climate change problem rather than inspiring broader public support for climate solutions . <p> By these measures , some types of travel are harder to justify than others . ( Antarctica 's fragile ecology comes to mind . ) But that does n't mean shunning all opportunities . Personally seeing effects of climate change at some of the nation 's most treasured destinations may help encourage meaningful action . <p> Borrowing from Mark Twain , " vegetating in one little corner of the earth " is not the answer . To better understand climate change , take a trip and see it yourself this summer . Go before it 's gone .
