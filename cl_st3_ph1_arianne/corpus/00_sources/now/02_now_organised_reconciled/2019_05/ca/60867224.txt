The peatlands , which are a type of wetland , are one of the world 's largest carbon banks . <p> Every year , plants such as Sphagnum mosses growing on the surface of the peatlands absorb carbon dioxide through photosynthesis . <p> When the plants die , they form layers of partially decomposing organic matter -- peat . Over thousands of years , these deposits built up and now store a wealth of carbon safely in the soggy ground . <p> The lack of oxygen in waterlogged peat slows the rate of decomposition . This means that carbon dioxide -- a byproduct of decomposition -- is released only at very low levels . <p> Scientists are worried that changes to the water table -- @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ man-made alterations to the delicate landscape , such as new roads -- could cause the peat to dry out , decay faster and release more carbon dioxide . This would turn the peat from environmental friend to enemy . <p> Conversely , too much water in the peatlands could result in an increase in emissions of another potent greenhouse gas -- methane . This gas is produced when microbes in moist environments consume decaying organic matter . The warmer and wetter it gets , the more methane scientists say will be produced . <p> " You 're now altering the security of that wealth in the bank , and you might be turning those small deposits into large withdrawals of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere , " said Nigel Roulet , a geography professor at McGill University and a member of the Far North panel . <p> " We have a moral obligation to ensure that we develop the North in a way that anticipates and mitigates against those kinds of emissions due to land-use change .
