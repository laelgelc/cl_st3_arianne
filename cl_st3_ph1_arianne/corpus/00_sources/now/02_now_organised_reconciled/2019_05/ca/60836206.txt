<h> Saskatchewan 's highest court ruled in favour of carbon pricing -- and it was n't really close on key issues <p> Stewart Elgie and Nathalie Chalifour are professors at the University of Ottawa , Faculty of Common Law , who have published many articles on constitutional law and the environment.They were co-counsel for an intervenor in the Saskatchewan court case , Canada 's Ecofiscal Commission . <p> In its May 3 decision upholding the federal carbon-pricing law , the Saskatchewan Court of Appeal took a classic Canadian federalism approach -- the same one that has been used for decades in areas such as health and social programs . Nonetheless , some commentators -- including some premiers -- have attacked the decision and tried to play down its significance because there were two dissenting judges . <p> But the critics ' arguments do n't hold water , for several reasons . <p> Story continues below advertisement <p> First , there is nothing unusual about a dissenting judgment in important constitutional cases such as this . Since 1988 , the Supreme Court of Canada has upheld three different federal environmental laws that were challenged , with a dissent in each @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the majority and dissenting judges in Saskatchewan actually agreed that the federal government has the constitutional power to price carbon . They also agreed that carbon pricing is effective . Where they differed was about the constitutional basis for the federal power . <p> The majority held that Parliament has jurisdiction to price carbon as a matter of national concern under its residual Peace , Order , and Good Government power -- because " climate change is a global problem " and " greenhouse gas GHG emissions do n't respect provincial boundaries . " They found the carbon pricing law is a " regulatory charge , " not a " tax , " largely because Parliament is returning all the revenues to taxpayers . <p> The dissent did not agree that carbon pricing is a matter of " national concern , " finding ( surprisingly ) that GHG emissions are a form of local air pollution . However , they held that Parliament does have authority to implement a carbon price under its taxation power ( something Saskatchewan had conceded ) . The problem , in their view , was @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ tax , constitutionally speaking ( since Parliament explicitly designed it as a regulatory charge ) . But importantly , the dissent was clear that Parliament could enact a carbon price if it designed the law as a tax . <p> All five judges also concluded , based on extensive evidence , that carbon pricing is effective . According to Chief Justice Robert Richards , there was uncontested evidence that : " A well-designed carbon price is an indispensable part of a strategy for reducing emissions in an efficient way " and that " carbon prices ... around the world have been successful in reducing greenhouse gas emissions . " <p> Writing for the minority , Justice Ralph Ottenbreit concurred that , " There is economic evidence to the effect that levies of this nature , whether a tax or regulatory charge , can successfully effect behavioural changes in a consumer . " <p> Similarly , all five judges agreed about the " existential necessity of mitigating anthropogenic GHG emissions . " And they agreed that both the federal and provincial governments have important roles to play in pricing and regulating @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ constitutional power allows Ottawa to price carbon . <p> Story continues below advertisement <p> Story continues below advertisement <p> The central issue in the case was how to put boundaries on the federal power , to allow it to deal with the national and global dimensions of GHG emissions , while leaving room for provinces to address the significant provincial aspects -- such as the historic flooding now happening in Ontario and Quebec , and the wildfires in British Columbia . <p> The majority judges bounded the federal power by holding that Ottawa can set " minimum national standards of price stringency for GHG emissions . " Provinces can bring in their own climate laws and tailor them to the needs of their economies , as long as they meet the national minimum requirements . <p> This is the classic approach to Canadian federalism that has been used for decades . Both the majority and dissenting judgments enable this same approach for climate action , albeit under different heads of power . <p> And climate action is long overdue .
