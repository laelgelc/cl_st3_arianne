The model showed that rising temperatures and a growing number of weather-related disasters now cause more migration than lack of income or political freedom . <p> Each 10 per cent increase in temperature in an origin country caused an increase of 3 per cent in migration from that country to the 16 destination countries , which included Australia @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Both developed and at-risk countries need more planning and policy to prepare for what is likely to be a growing trend of people wanting to move . <p> Dennis Wesselbaum , economist , Otago Business School <p> The study , published last month in the journal Global and Planetary Change , also found that this migration happens in stages . <p> Dennis Wesselbaum , the lead researcher and an economist at the Otago Business School , explains that migration actually decreased for around five years after a temperature anomaly , before increasing for the next 20 years . <p> " One explanation is that people move to places further away and have to save more money to finance migration cost , or that it takes time to identify the temperature shock , " Wesselbaum told SciDev.Net . <p> Raya Muttarak , senior lecturer of geography and international development at the UK 's University of East Anglia , believes another reason for the apparent delay is that people at first try " in-situ adaptation " . <p> " If you experience climatic shocks in the first year , you try different @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ changing jobs , borrowing money , " said Muttarak , who was not involved in the study .
