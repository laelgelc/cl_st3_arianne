<h> Respecting science and ethics : the case of GMOs <p> Reasoned policymaking on GM crops requires a philosophical , as well as scientific analysis , argues Andreas Christiansen . <p> Photo Credit : Adobe Stock <p> This argument may sound controversial ; after all , should n't GM policymaking be based on science ? <p> However , there is no such thing as " policy based on science " , at least in the sense that no policy is justified by reference to science alone . <p> We always also need to make normative assumptions about what is valuable , just , right , or good . Of course , policies can - and should - be science-based in the sense that scientific findings are respected when considering which policies to implement . <p> The value of philosophical analysis lies in making explicit normative assumptions , and critically asking whether those assumptions - along with the scientific facts - justify certain policy conclusions . <p> None of the best arguments for being against GM crops justify restrictive policies like those in place in the EU . <p> The background is a presumption in favour of GM crops , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ or food security ; the principle that people should be free to do what they want , provided that they do not ( in a broad sense ) harm others or disproportionately harm the environment . <p> Both presumptions can be overridden by other considerations -but , not in this case . <p> Here are three examples of why prominent anti-GM arguments do not work . <p> " A logical and critical analysis of arguments aimed at restricting GM crops shows that plausible normative assumptions , which may also be shaping public opinion , do not justify certain policy conclusions " <p> Risk <p> Let us assume that we should be relatively averse to taking risks , and that ( at least some ) GM crops may have sufficiently adverse effects . <p> Even so , the current process-based authorisation procedure is not justified , only a trait- or product-based one is . But since GM crops will replace other things that are not natural in this definition -- namely highly cultivated crops , which are far from independent of human interventions -- the view can not justify restrictions on GM crops . <p> Organic farming <p> In many countries , including Denmark , it is a widespread view that organic farming is superior to both conventional and GM farming . <p> Let @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ ask : In virtue of what is organic farming superior ? <p> A moderate view would argue that organic farming is good because it is seen as a more sustainable and environmentally friendly approach , while a radical view would propose that organic farming is good because it represents working " with nature " rather than against it . <p> Restricting GM crops based on the value of organic farming now presents us with a dilemma : The moderate view is normatively plausible ( and popular ) ; but it does not work as an anti-GM argument , since many GM crops are compatible with the normative aim of sustainability such as , improved insect resistance or drought tolerance . <p> " Reasoned policymaking on GM crops requires a philosophical , as well as scientific analysis " <p> In the radical view , GM crops are incompatible with the normative aim since the very technique of GM can be considered as not working " with nature " - but that normative aim is much less attractive and popular . <p> I suspect that organics-based arguments for restricting GM crops typically @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the clear incompatibility between GM and the aims of organic farming of the radical view . This is a fallacy and should be avoided . <p> As the examples illustrate , a logical and critical analysis of arguments aimed at restricting GM crops shows that plausible normative assumptions , which may also be shaping public opinion , do not justify certain policy conclusions . <p> Indeed , I believe that no arguments that both represent cogent reasoning and respect scientific facts support these policies . <p> So there are no good reasons not to allow and even promote the use of GM crops under a suitable trait-based risk regulation scheme . <p> And to the extent that GM crops can aid in solving serious problems , such as the effects of climate change and food security in a growing world , it is imperative to do so .
