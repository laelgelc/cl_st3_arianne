<h> Global problem of illegal plastic waste <p> THE Malaysian government should be commended for acting assertively against the illegal importation of plastic waste . The Energy , Technology , Science , Climate Change and Environment Minister was right in pointing out that Malaysia is not a dumping ground for developed nations , and that locals who facilitate such activities are traitors to our national environment and health . <p> The problem of illegal plastic waste can not be overstated . A comprehensive report by Greenpeace last year highlights the magnitude of the problem . <p> The report points out the global pattern of how the so-called recyclables are increasingly being shipped from developed countries to middle- and low-income countries . Malaysia is the top receiver of such plastic waste in South-East Asia , where 754,000 metric tonnes were " imported " in the first seven months of 2018 alone . This amount is pale in comparison to the 3,000 metric tonnes of plastic waste which , as announced by the minister on Tuesday , will be sent back to the originating countries . <p> The vast majority of these plastic waste were not recycled . Local residents , such as those in Klang , suffered from pungent and toxic fumes daily , resulting in respiratory problems especially among children . In Kuala Langat , seepage of polluted water from the illegal dumpsites has contaminated the river . <p> This illegal plastic waste problem brings several other issues to the fore . First , it highlights the hypocrisy of the developed countries that are exporting them . They are loud and strong in advocating against palm oil due to its purported impact on the environment , yet they are complicit in treating developing countries as convenient dumpsites . <p> Second , the illegal plastic waste issue shows that recycling plastic is not just a matter of putting plastic bottles into the correct recycling bin . In this age of globalisation , waste management has become international . Various actors and processes are involved across the globe , and pollution can be exported and imported . <p> This leads to the third point . For example , the Canadian government has been dragging its feet for over five years in taking back its illegally exported waste to the Philippines . It finally agreed to take back its garbage last week after President Rodrigo Duterte threatened to sever diplomatic ties and dump the garbage into Canada 's territorial waters . <p> The recent addition of plastic waste into the Basel Convention , the treaty that restricts transboundary movement of hazardous waste , is a major step forward . However , more needs to be done collectively by the sending and receiving countries to ensure compliance . <p> Lastly , at the individual level , we should proactively reduce our reliance on single-use plastic where possible .
