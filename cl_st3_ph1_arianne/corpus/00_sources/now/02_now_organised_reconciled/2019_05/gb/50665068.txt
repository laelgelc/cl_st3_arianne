<h> Boiling Point : What to do about the gas and other home carbon issues <h> Boiling Point : What to do about the gas and other home carbon issues <p> WHEN it comes to shifting to a zero-carbon society , one of the big things that needs to be had is a public conversation around the way we heat and insulate our homes . It does n't sound glamorous . Talking about it does n't have the allure of the conversation about the latest breed of electric cars -- but we need to do it . We need to talk boilers . <p> Earlier this year the UK Committee on Climate Change report urged a ban on gas boilers and cookers in 2025 , and reported that cuts in greenhouse gas emissions from UK housing had stalled . Heat , meanwhile , accounts for half the energy consumption in Scotland . If we do n't decarbonise heat , we do n't have any chance to decarbonise the whole system . <p> A big question , then , is what does that mean for the gas boiler ? The majority of newbuilds are still being constructed with gas boilers . Yet methane gas , a fossil fuel , has no real place in a zero-carbon world . Its burning produces half the amount of carbon dioxide as coal and , when methane leaks , it is a powerful greenhouse gas itself . <p> This boiler question seems particularly pertinent to me because my own boiler is old , on its last legs , and likely to be up for replacement soon . The first is the kind of energy-efficiency measures , like insulation and double glazing , and this has already been promoted by the Scottish Government for more than 10 years . The second , the shift to zero or low-carbon heating , is a project which , though encouraged through the Renewable Heating Incentive , has only so far penetrated a tiny fraction of households . <p> Progress has been slow . Dr Niall Kerr , a University of Edinburgh researcher in energy policy effectiveness , believes that for Scotland to have a chance of reaching our zero-carbon targets it is going to require an unseen level of effort . <p> " The rate of change that we have achieved with government policy in the past few years is of a certain level of magnitude , " he says . " We need to move from changing a few people , to changing 10 to 20 times as many people every year . Most experts believe that the answer is likely to lie in a variety of different technologies which will each suit different situations . <p> As Dr Zhibin Yu , senior lecturer in systems power and energy at the University of Glasgow , puts it : " No single technology will solve this whole issue -- because we have different users , different scales , different sources . " <p> One of the biggest questions is whether the solution lies in a decarbonised electrical supply or decarbonising the gas network .
