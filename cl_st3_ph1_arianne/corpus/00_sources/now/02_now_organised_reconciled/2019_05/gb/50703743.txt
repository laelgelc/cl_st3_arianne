<h> CLIMATE COLUMN : Work of Extinction Rebellion is far from over <p> This column is written by Andy Williams , an electrical engineer studying for a masters degree in renewable energy technology and a member of Extinction Rebellion , who lives in Chalford . <p> EXTINCTION Rebellion . How did a movement started by a small group of Stroud people become so successful ? <p> XR is less than a year old , and yet it has spread around the world with astonishing speed and after years of hardly mentioning it , the UK parliament has declared a Climate Emergency . <p> A lot of this success must be down to the Stroud-based founders of the movement . Clearly the potent mix of non-violent civil disobedience , a caring culture , deep respect , and a simple set of demands plays a large part . <p> But there is something else . <p> Many of us have been deeply worried about climate change for decades . <p> But MPs kept quiet because they were sure it was a vote loser and the media rarely covered it . The general feeling seemed to be " It ca n't be that bad or someone would be doing something about it " . <p> But last year things started to change . <p> In the space of a few months a Swedish schoolgirl started a lonely climate strike , the IPCC published its urgent 1.5 ? report , and Extinction Rebellion was born . <p> The massive XR actions in April coincided with the Attenborourgh programme , and a European tour by Greta Thunberg . <p> But who were we rebelling against . We were given full use of a Waterloo church and the police seemed to be with us . <p> And then at the Climate Emergency debate nearly all MPs turned out to be fervent supporters of action on climate change . <p> XR Rebellion was a movement whose exact time had come .
