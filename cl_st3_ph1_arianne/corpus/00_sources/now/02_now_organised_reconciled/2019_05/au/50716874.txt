<p> Election results lead to over-reactions in the short term . Both the winners and the losers can be guilty of this . Like any sporting contest , especially in a grand final , the winner on the day can regard itself as invincible and dismiss any talk of weaknesses , while the loser can be full of self-doubt and often engages in a full-scale retreat from all that it stood for and all that it did well . After elections the winner is full of self-congratulations , certain of its mandate and inclined to over-confidence . If the victory is unexpected , as was Scott Morrison 's , the dangers are greater . Unrestrained largesse , including jobs for the boys , can be overdone in the moment of great success . The over-reaction of the election loser , especially after an unexpected loss like Bill Shorten 's , can be even greater despite the election still being closely contested . There are two ways to interpret the national swings in an election campaign and they also apply if the results are broken down to the state level or even to particular types of seats , like mining seats , rural seats or inner-city seats . The first is to take the two-party preferred swing as it stands , in this case 1.33 per cent . On that basis the swing was 1.33 per cent to and from the Coalition and Labor . Looked at that way exhilaration on one side and despair on the other hardly seems warranted . Of course , when the expectation was for a swing the other way delivering a new Labor government led by Shorten , then that does change perceptions by magnifying both joy and disappointment .
