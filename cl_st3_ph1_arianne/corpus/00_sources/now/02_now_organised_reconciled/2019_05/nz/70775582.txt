<h> Kicking a goal for environmental issues in sport <p> Arsenal forward Pierre-Emerick Aubameyang celebrates after scoring his side 's third goal during the Europa League semifinal soccer match , second leg , between Valencia and Arsenal at the Camp de Mestalla stadium in Valencia , Spain , on May 9 . <p> Opinion : As some of you will by now be aware , I 'm a keen follower of English football and the curtain is now coming down on what , for me , has been a dream season . <p> But , before all you Kiwis who are rugby fanatics or merely uninterested in sport generally stop reading , bear with me because this column is not just about " soccer " as you persist in calling it , there 's also an important environmental message . <p> I 'll get the football content out of the way first . This has been a fantastic season for me because my hometown club , Lincoln City , have won the League Two championship and will be promoted to the third tier of English football next season , meeting the likes of former top-tier clubs such as Bolton @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ favourite top-flight team , Liverpool , last week achieved a footballing miracle in beating European giants Barcelona 4-0 at Anfield to overturn a 3-0 deficit in the semi-finals of the Champions League . Then at the weekend they also came within a whisker of winning their first Premier League title . <p> They beat Wolves in their final game of the season this week but it was not enough to deny imperious Manchester City the title , despite collecting enough points to have won the Premier League title in every other season bar last year and this . <p> The remarkable thing about the outcome of last week 's European ties was that results mean that the Champions League and Europa League cup finals will for the first time be contested between four teams for the same country -- England . <p> And this is where we turn to concerns about the environment . <p> The Europa League final between London rivals Arsenal and Chelsea -- teams whose home grounds are just 12 kilometres apart -- is being staged in Baku in Azerbaijan , which means supporters will have to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the match . <p> Such are the travel logistics and the paucity of flights between London to Azerbaijan that the fans will probably face a return journey of anything between four or eight days , depending on whether they fly or travel overland . <p> The stadium in Baku is closer to Mumbai in India than it is to the teams ' home city and it is this fact that is raising concerns among environmentalists . <p> At a time of growing concern about climate change , it 's estimated the carbon footprint -- maybe that should be bootprint -- of getting the fans and the teams to Baku will be thousands of tonnes of carbon dioxide . <p> To a lesser extent the same concerns are raised about the situation surrounding the two Premier League teams contesting the Champions League final -- Liverpool and Tottenham Hotspur . They and their fans will have to travel to Madrid for the final . <p> Co-leader of the UK Green Party Jonathan Bartley summed it up : " With Arsenal and Chelsea each being given just 6000 seats and sitting just a @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ missing an open goal by not hosting the final in London . Packing thousands of fans on to flights is damaging to the environment . The 45,000 fans travelling to both finals will emit about 35,490 tonnes of carbon dioxide . It 's time Uefa ( European football 's ruling body ) did better . " <p> While it 's understandable Uefa want to share around the blue riband matches , it defies common sense that supporters of teams , whose grounds are just a couple of Tube trips apart , have to spend several days , hundreds of pounds and travel thousands of kilometres to watch their favourites play local rivals . <p> This is especially true at a time when the effects of travel on climate change are coming under the microscope . <p> It 's surely not beyond the wit of Uefa to have contingency plans in place to switch venues in the event of just such a scenario as has unfolded this season .
