<h> Using behavioural insights to address COVID-19 <p> MSc International Development and Humanitarian Emergencies alumna , Emma Smith , refers back to her 2018 dissertation to provide behavioural insights to addressing COVID-19 . <p> Two years ago , my MSc dissertation in International Development and Humanitarian Emergencies started with a chilling sentence : " It is only a matter of time before the world sees another globally threatening outbreak . " It goes on to say " experts suggest that the next pathogen is likely to be airborne , and existing health systems are not equipped to contain and combat it . " <p> I had hoped I would n't be so right , so soon . <p> I reread my dissertation and extracted/added to some takeaways that I thought were worth sharing : <p> The rise of global disease outbreaks <p> According to the World Health Organization ( WHO ) , at any given time , there are " dozens of disease outbreaks around the world " . There are a variety of factors that contribute to these outbreaks , such as the increased levels of human migration . When these outbreaks occur , they are referred to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ pandemics . Epidemics and pandemics describe the circulation of an infectious illness within a short time frame . <p> Examples of such emergencies include the Ebola virus outbreak in West Africa between 2013 -- 2016 which had a case fatality rate of over 70% ( World Health Organization , 2014 ) , as well as the 1918 Spanish influenza pandemic which spread rapidly and caused an estimate of 50 million deaths . The latest life-threatening outbreak is COVID-19 . <p> We have reason to believe -- with factors such as growing populations , urbanization , climate change , and new habited areas -- that we will begin to see more frequent and more severe global health emergencies . And our world is not prepared . <p> So what do we do to mitigate , prepare for , respond to , and recover from suffering during these outbreaks ? <p> A crucial -- and often overlooked -- aspect is effective emergency communication at all stages of an outbreak . Clear and targeted messaging in emergencies can save lives as it enables responses such as evacuations or prevention techniques such as critical @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ clear warnings and updates reach their populace -- and pay less attention to how their messages then prompt people to change their behavior . <p> During the avian influenza outbreak in Thailand , public health education campaigns and general media reports about avian influenza appear to have been effective in reaching rural people ( Olsen et al. , 2005:1601 ) . But what the officials quickly realized was that despite widespread knowledge about avian influenza and effective means of prevention , most Thai people did not actually change their ways . <p> Why not ? <p> It 's hard for us to change our behavior . <p> Turns out , human brains are a little peculiar . We have a hard time changing our routines and habits , even when we know something is bad for us . Like how we will eat that chocolate bar , despite knowing what it does to our body . We need help making good choices . <p> This is , I believe where behavioral insights could come in . Behavioral insights are defined as , " insights from psychology , cognitive science , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ actually make choices " ( " Behavioral Insights " , 2018 ) . Emergency communications could incorporate such insights to improve decision-making in health crises . <p> Examples of behavioral insights that could be used to improve communications during COVID-19 : <p> Social norm messaging to help people practice social distancing such as , " You are the only one of your friends who is not working from home " . Social norms signal appropriate habits and are classed as expectations or rules within a group of people ( Dolan et al. , 2010 ) . <p> Priming people to make decisions to care for others by using phrases or words such as " let us unite , we are one , together we stand " . Conceptual priming is a technique and process applied in psychology that engages people in a task or exposes them to stimuli . The prime consists of meanings ( e.g. words ) that activate associated memories . <p> Loss aversion to get people to wash their hands such as , " by not washing your hands , you could lose elderly or vulnerable members @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ concept associated with prospect theory and is encapsulated in the expression " losses loom larger than gains " ( Kahneman &amp; Tversky , 1979 ) . It is thought that the pain of losing is psychologically about twice as powerful as the pleasure of gaining . <p> Default options with messaging like " unless you opt out , you will be placed on a volunteer list to grocery shop for elderly neighbors in this building . " Default options are pre-set courses of action that take effect if nothing is specified by the decision maker ( Thaler &amp; Sunstein , 2008 ) , and setting defaults is an effective nudge when there is inertia or uncertainty in decision making ( Samson , 2014 ) . <p> Social norms , default options , loss aversion , and priming are just some simple examples of mechanisms that could be used in emergency communications to introduce and sustain new habits .
