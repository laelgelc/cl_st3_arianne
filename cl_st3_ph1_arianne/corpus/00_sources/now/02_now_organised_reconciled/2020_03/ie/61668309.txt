<h> Can more tillage , not less , save Irish farming ? <p> By John Spink , Head of the Teagasc Crops , Environment and Land Use Programme , Oak Park , Carlow <p> There has been an upsurge in interest in diet and the relative merits of plant-based versus animal-sourced foods , particularly related to the impact of diet on climate change . <p> Whilst a hectare of crop land undoubtedly produces much lower greenhouse gas ( GHG ) emissions than a hectare of intensive livestock grazing land , the pros and cons of either dietary choice is not that simple . <p> As a country we have a trade deficit of ? 1 billion in fruit and vegetables , which means that a large proportion of that plant-based diet is imported and often from regions of the world increasingly suffering from water shortages to produce the crops . <h> Importing products we can grow <p> Our single biggest fruit import is bananas at 93,000t . Cultivation of these long-term grasslands results in a rapid release of this stored carbon . <h> Scope to increase crop production <p> It may seem therefore that there is little scope to increase crop production from its current 290,000ha . <p> However , a recent analysis of land use by Jesko Zimmermann , Teagasc data technologist , showed that there were over 428,000ha in frequent crop production . <p> Another 434,000ha had been in crop production at least once since the year 2000 , which is a reflection of the movement of the crop area into and out of temporary grassland . <p> As a country , therefore , we have the potential to significantly increase our production of both horticultural and tillage @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <p> Many are surprised to find out that Ireland is highly productive when it comes to crops , with the highest wheat yields and second highest barley yields in the world . <p> This is because we have a very benign climate that is rarely too hot or too cold ; we are far north which gives long summer days and we have a ready supply of water from our high rainfall . <p> It is the latter that is increasingly going to give us a competitive advantage for crop production compared to some of the current global centres of production , which are predicted to suffer more frequent droughts as climate change progresses . <p> There is clearly demand , even currently , just for import replacement , but there is also the possibility of export markets in the future . <p> If we can seize this opportunity , we could see increased production of grain for high-value markets such as beer and whiskey production , apples for consumption or cider production , a wide range of vegetables and protein crops to replace soya.
