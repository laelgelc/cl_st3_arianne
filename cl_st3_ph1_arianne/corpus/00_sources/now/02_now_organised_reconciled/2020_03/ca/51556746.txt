<h> The COVID-19 pandemic and Canada 's food system <p> " It is ... likely that one of COVID-19 's legacies may be to provoke a national conversation on the nature of our food system , the way in which our farmers are dependent on foreign labor , and the way in which consumers rely daily on food produced all over the world . " <p> In the past week , many Canadians were confronted with a scene that was entirely new to them . Empty grocery store shelves evoke the image of a society sliding into the precipice , of wartime rations , and of the zombie apocalypse . But for all the concern this has generated , at least three key points should stand out . <p> The first is that we should not panic . Canada is blessed with a robust infrastructure and sophisticated network of food processors , retailers , and regulators committed to ensuring food continues to arrive in a more or less business as usual fashion . As Galen Weston of Loblaw wrote on March 16 , " we are not running out of food or essential supplies . Canadians are consuming the same amount of food this week as they did three weeks ago . <p> Furthermore , many of the players who run our food supply chain are quite used to dealing with uncertainty and massive fluctuations . For instance , fruit and vegetable produce sellers regularly must contend with highly perishable crops that come in waves of abundance and dearth . Simon Somogyi , the Arrell chair in the Business of Food at the University of Guelph , has put considerable thought in this . He says , " of all the food sectors , produce sellers are the ones most used to dealing with rapid change and problem solving given the unexpected impacts of weather and short shelf life of their product . " So , every expectation is that the shelves in our grocery stores will more or less return to normal in the relatively near future . <p> A second important @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ are at least a few key areas that will be very tricky to navigate . To a large extent , farming in Canada depends on the approximately 60,000 seasonal migrant workers who come to our country to do things such as pruning apple orchards and picking strawberries . These people often fill low paying jobs , which nevertheless demand quite a bit of skill , and there is a chronic labour shortage of this type of worker in our country . Although controversial ( critics worry that many foreign workers are underpaid and exploited ) , our farmers depend on these workers and this is why on March 18 , Agriculture and Agri-Food Minister Marie-Claude Bibeau announced , " we know how much we depend on these foreign workers for the agricultural sector . Today we have reassured the industry that we will find a way to let them come . " <p> This reassurance will undoubtedly help farmers maintain domestic fruit and vegetable supply chains over the next growing season . For instance , in the 1960s , U.S. governments reduced the amount of migrant workers that tomato farmers in California could hire . This precipitated a rapid consolidation of farms as smaller farmers sold out and the new , larger , farmers invested in mechanical harvesting technology , thus reducing their overall labour requirements . <p> A third key implication for our food system is more philosophical . Along with all the short-term hardship , COVID-19 has exposed what many people perceive as a vulnerability in the modern economy . We now know -- in a way we did n't seem to acknowledge even two weeks ago -- that our whole world depends on the seamless high-volume transfer of goods and people around the world . Many commentators , therefore , are starting to question whether or not it is possible , feasible , resilient ( or even desirable ) to assume that we will ever go back to " the way things were . " In terms of food systems , this will likely lead many to call for greater regional self-sufficiency @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of agri-food systems . In some ways , this could spark a reinvestment in Canadian farms , food processors and our rural economies that have been declining for decades . <p> To close , Canadians worried about the security of their food system in light of COVID-19 should n't panic . Our food supply chains are almost certainly robust enough to survive this particular shock . However , and looking into the longer term , it is also likely that one of COVID-19 's legacies may be to provoke a national conversation on the nature of our food system , the way in which our farmers are dependent on foreign labor , and the way in which consumers rely daily on food produced all over the world . In a world that was already required to change due to climate change , this may force us to revaluate some of the basic assumptions that underpin our food .
