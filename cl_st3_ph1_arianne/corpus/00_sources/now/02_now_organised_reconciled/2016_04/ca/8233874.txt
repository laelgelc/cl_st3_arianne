<h> Trevor Hancock : We must say no to old-paradigm solutions <p> The greatest challenge we face in the 21st century is to make the transition to a way of life that enables everyone on Earth to have a good quality of life -- including , of course , good health -- while living within the limits of the one small planet we call home . <p> To do so , we Canadians have to dramatically reduce our ecological footprint , while maintaining a good quality of life . <p> Canada 's ecological footprint in 2007 was the 11th largest in the world , at about seven hectares per person . But the planet 's biocapacity ( its regenerative ability ) was 1.8 hectares per person . So we are using about four planets ' worth of ecosystem goods and services . <p> That means less for other people , who then can not get the resources and development they need to lift them out of poverty . It also means less for other species -- which helps explain why the Earth 's Living Planet Index has declined @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ than half of both the global and the Canadian ecological footprint is due to the carbon emissions that are driving climate change , making the reduction of carbon emissions our No. 1 priority . There is growing recognition that we have to keep up to 80 per cent of our fossil-fuel reserves in the ground , because if we burn all we know we have , we will be off the dial in terms of global warming . <p> At the same time , we have to accelerate the drive to a low-carbon energy system . Clearly , we ca n't make this transition overnight ; it will take years . Which is why we have to learn to start saying no now to decisions that promote rather than reduce fossil-fuel extraction and use ; this is now a basic test of credible public policy in the 21st century . A good place to start is to say no to pipelines , as well as the liquefied natural gas business in B.C. that is so beloved by Premier Christy Clark and her government . <p> Prime Minister Justin Trudeau wants it @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and wind turbines to reach our climate goals . His remarks were rebutted this week by Harvard professor Naomi Oreskes in an interview on the Tyee website . Part of her point is that when you put in infrastructure , it is there for decades , so you lock in that way of life and work . So let 's not lock in more reliance on fossil fuels . <p> We need to say no to all new policies that make fossil-fuel use easier and cheaper , and yes to policies that make it less easy and more expensive . In B.C. , that means increasing the carbon tax , not freezing it as Clark has done . By making fossil fuels more expensive , we will create an economic climate that favours the development of energy conservation and clean , renewable energy systems . <p> Closer to home , here in the capital region , the McKenzie interchange provides an example of an important infrastructure decision to which we should say no . Yet there are much better options , including improving public transit and -- in this age of the Internet -- telecommuting . <p> Imagine for a moment that people who currently commute to work along the Trans-Canada Highway could , on average , work from home one day a week -- or that one-fifth of them could always work from home . You would reduce commuter traffic by about 20 per cent ; two days a week would reduce it 40 per cent . <p> So why not take the money saved from not building the interchange and use it to support the creation of telecommuting centres where people could go to work ? Not only would it reduce traffic and emissions , but it would stimulate local businesses .
