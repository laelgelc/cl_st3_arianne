<p> While a massive asteroid really did hit earth 66 million years ago , it was just the final push for an already failing species , rather than the main event . <p> Researches from the University of Bristol and the University of Reading found " overwhelming support " that dinosaur populations were already in a steady decline leading up to the Cretaceous -- Paleogene mass extinction event -- the proper name for the asteroid 's impact . <p> Researchers looked at the number of dinosaur fossils over a few hundred million years and found that around 90 million years ago , the dinos began to die . <p> " Our results highlight that dinosaurs showed a marked reduction in their ability to replace extinct species with new ones , making them vulnerable to extinction and unable to respond quickly to and recover from the final catastrophic event 66 Mya , " the researchers wrote . <p> The exceptions to the rule were Hadrosauriformes and Ceratopsidae , two species of herbivores that include triceratops . Both species instead saw a rise in populations around the same time . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ until the asteroid hit , causing their kind to perish and plunging the world into a prolonged winter . <p> Hypotheses of their decline include the breakup of the supercontinents Laurasia and Gondwana , an increase of volcanic activity , sea level rise , and the loss of their habitat . <p> " Our study strongly indicates that if a group of animals is experiencing a fast pace of extinction more so than they can replace , then they are prone to annihilation once a major catastrophe occurs , " Dr Manabu Sakamoto , a palaeontologist from the University of Reading who led the research told phys.org . " This has huge implications for our current and future biodiversity , given the unprecedented speed at which species are going extinct owing to the ongoing human-caused climate change . "
