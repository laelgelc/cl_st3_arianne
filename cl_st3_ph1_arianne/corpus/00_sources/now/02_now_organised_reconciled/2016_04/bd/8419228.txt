<p> Communities need to be involved in finding solutions to the challenges of climate change in urban areas <p> One billion people around the world live in informal settlements , lacking access to clean water , sanitation , secure housing and other basic services . These are the urban dwellers who will suffer the most from the impacts of climate change , such as floods , storms , heat waves or droughts . Already , although low and lower-middle income countries face only 33% of the world 's disasters , they see 81% of disaster-related deaths according to an UNDP report . <p> The residents of these settlements are struggling to adapt to climate change , not least because of their informal status . Many local governments regard informal settlements as illegal and therefore provide residents insufficient with basic risk-reducing infrastructure , such as drainage , piped water , and sanitation . <p> In other cases , local governments would like to support informal settlements , but lack the capacity to do so . In Cuttack and other cities in India , settlement and hazard mapping by local communities using simple GPS technologies is used to plan upgrading activities . This kind of partnership allows a much more far-reaching result than if either had worked on their own . <p> Beyond this , there are many cases of community-led innovations in addressing their development challenges . Many of these offer approaches that can now help them adapt to climate change -- for example , the establishment of city-level revolving loan funds run by community groups . These can be used to finance small-scale infrastructure projects that reduce people 's vulnerability to risk , or as an insurance scheme that allows them to recover more quickly after disasters . However , while individual households and organised communities can take some of their own adaptation measures , they can achieve much more when facilitated by supportive local governments . <p> In turn , local governments can not be absolved of their responsibilities .
