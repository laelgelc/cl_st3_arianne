These change the size and split of the cost savings that can be made . Fewer permits are traded and the benefits become smaller . <p> But such a tax can be tolerated and it may be worth sticking with a partner in spite of it . It might not be ideal , but it 's usually better than staying single . <h> Getting off to a good start <p> Negotiating the partnership , harmonising market rules and setting up a platform for international transactions is complicated work , and no link will make economic sense if these upfront costs are too high . This is likely the reason why negotiations between the EU and China have barely started whereas those between the EU and Switzerland have successfully concluded . <p> Where the picture is n't so clear , our analysis highlights other possibilities . We used data from China , various European countries , the US power sector , and the international aviation sector to model how each would benefit from wider carbon credit trading . <p> We found the benefits of links between " large and similar " markets can be dwarfed by those between " small but different " markets . So instinctive decisions -- such as trading with a large partner , or your neighbour -- can actually end up having costly consequences . <p> For instance , under a few strong but not outlandish assumptions , it may appear to make sense for the international aviation sector to partner with the US because the latter is so large .
