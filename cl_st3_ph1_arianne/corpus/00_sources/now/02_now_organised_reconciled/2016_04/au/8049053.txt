It 's an answer to global warming . It 's the most important car the company will make . <p> Apparently . <p> Given that the first of Tesla 's Model 3 cars is not due out until next year -- and the company makes no promises -- or not until 2018 in Australia , that is some seriously premature hype , backed by Apple-style queues outside its stores said to justify the parallels to the world 's most valuable company . <p> Illustration : Michael Mucci <p> By early on Sunday , Tesla chief executive Elon Musk said it had 253,000 orders for its Model 3 , each of which required a deposit of US$1000 @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the US estimated price , if even half those people end up buying the car they 've ordered with a significant downpayment , it represents a US$8.8 billion launch of a car . <p> If all of them stump up , it 's a US$17.7 billion one -- or about A$23 billion at today 's exchange rates . And Tesla is still counting . <p> The first pictures of the Tesla Model 3 were released on April 1 . Photo : Supplied <p> The hype is backed by money the likes of which have never been seen for an electric car . It must spook the competition , which for Tesla comes from General Motors , Nissan , Volkswagen , BMW , and Ford , all of which are speeding up their electric car efforts . <p> For now , Tesla has the required cachet , as Apple does in mobile phones , to spur people like Sydneysider Andreas Stephens , the first person in Australia to put down $1500 for a car he wo n't see for at least two years , and sit for two days outside the Tesla @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ caught the company 's attention when he started queuing in Sydney even before Tesla 's Model 3 launch razzmatazz had started in California . <p> When it did , the company said the car would cost US$35,000 , carry five adults and have a range of 345 kilometres per charge . Fans like Stephens responded with serious cash , and its share price spiked 5 per cent . <p> Whatever transpires with the Model 3 , the amount taken in deposits surely justifies the hope that the arrival of mass-market electric cars marks the point that private transport comes to the climate-protection party in significant numbers . <p> It demonstrates , at least , there are many people willing to pay to do what their governments seem incapable of , taking meaningful action to reduce damaging climate change . <p> Cars are important in that fight : about 17 per cent of Australia 's greenhouse gas emissions come from transport , with three-fifths of that from light vehicles . Last year 's enormous , frightening spike in global average temperature to 0.9 degrees above the 20th century average has emphasised the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ cars are no solution unless Australia , along with the rest of the world , changes the way the electricity they need to run is generated . <p> Unless Tesla drivers charge their cars with power from solar panels or from an electricity account solely supplied with renewable energy , they wo n't be doing much to reduce damaging global warming . <p> Buying a fully electric car does n't make greenhouse sense unless the electricity used to charge it emits fewer warming pollutants than running a petrol or hybrid engine to cover the same ground . <p> We do n't know how much power will be needed to charge a Model 3 when it finally arrives , but last year Matthew Bailes , pro-vice chancellor of research at Swinburne University of Technology , did the numbers on the existing Tesla Model S for The Conversation . <p> He estimated that that $130,000 car requires about 100 kilowatt hours of electricity , which in Victoria comes mainly from brown coal , meaning about 100 kilograms of carbon dioxide is emitted per charge . <p> That works out at about 28 kilograms @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ than the 13 kilograms of carbon emitted by his hybrid Prius running the same distance . Bailes concluded he would be doing more environmental damage by switching to the existing Tesla than by sticking to his hybrid . <p> Even if you quibble with those figures ( emissions are bound to be lower for the new model ) , to reduce avoid damaging emissions , electric cars have to use enough renewable energy to be lower-emitting than the cars they replace . <p> The crucial role for government is not in meaningless minor moves like allowing electric cars into transit lanes or discounting the registration fee to encourage their purchase . In London , electric cars are even immune from the congestion charge , a classic case of encouragement for something good ( low-emission engines ) making something else worse ( traffic ) . <p> It is in changing the way power is generated , and allowing people to achieve the change they 're trying to buy . <p> Tim Dick is a Sydney lawyer .
