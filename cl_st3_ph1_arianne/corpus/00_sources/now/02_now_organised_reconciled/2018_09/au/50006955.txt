<h> Friday essay : The rise of the ' bin chicken ' , a totem for modern Australia <p> In a time of environmental threat and lock-out laws , the humble ibis is a token -- a totem , even -- of urban grunge in our increasingly sanitised , corporatised cities . <p> UpdatedUpdated 7 September <p> By Paul Allatson , Andrea Connor <p> Share <p> In just a few decades , the Australian White Ibis ( Threskiornis molucca ) has made itself at home in many of Australia 's coastal and inland cities . And aside from the feathered birds we see daily ( often foraging amongst garbage ) , representations of ibis have exploded in popular culture . The humble ibis , it seems , has gone viral . <p> From ibis tattoos to artworks to TV series , this often maligned bird has become a cultural phenomenon , bordering on a national obsession . This ibis juggernaut says a lot about Australian identity and culture in the 21st century - and human-animal relations in a time of environmental threat and uncertainty . <p> Ibis have entered the Australian English lexicon as " bin chickens " , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ " picnic pirates " , to name just four of their many nicknames . <p> They can be found splashed across clothing ; probing human rubbish in cartoons and poetry ; on cups and tea towels , in children 's books and the daily press . Small ibis colonies were established by conservationists in the early 1970s in places like Sydney 's Taronga Zoo and Healesville Sanctuary in Victoria . <p> Ibis also migrated from interior wetlands to the coasts of east and southeast Australia and the southwest . Between the 1970s and the 1990s , the Australian White Ibis was classified as the Sacred Ibis , the same species worshipped in ancient Egypt as an emblem of Thoth . This God was responsible for maintaining the universe , judging the dead , and overseeing systems of magic , writing and science . <p> Thoth was often depicted with an ibis head . Because of this association , as Georges Cuvier wrote in 1831 , Egyptians let ibis " stray unharmed through their cities " . Sacred Ibis were also mummified ; there is a stunning example in the Australian Museum . <p> Today , however , the Australian White Ibis is regarded as its own species , close kin of the Sacred Ibis . Many older Australians still refer to our white ibis as sacred . A different order of sacredness inspires Indigenous representations of ibis , an example being ceramic work by the Queensland artist Thanakupi . <h> The ibis and the artist <p> In our research , we have been struck by how Australian artists work with the ibis backstory of sacredness @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the urban life and times of ibis , and the bird 's new , precarious , celebrity status . <p> Clive , Janice and Derek are the stars of the 2018 online TV animated series , Bin Chickens . Targets of human rage against ibis as abject and filthy , the trio struggles to survive in Sydney 's overly cemented Darling Harbour . In vain they also try to recover their lost God-head status . The series , funded by Screen Australia and the ABC , is a sardonic sendup of rampant urban development and its ecological costs . The first episode alone has had more than 30,000 viewers . <p> For many artists , ibis-human interactions are emblematic of Australia 's new multi-species urban ecologies . Sue Pedley 's installation " Rolling Musical Screech " ( 2017 ) featured street signs of birds , and celebrated the sensory contributions ibis make to the contemporary city . <p> Australian artists also regard ibis as a bellwether species that makes links between habitat loss , climate change and excess human consumption . The film parodies the David Attenborough wildlife documentary , in which animals rarely live alongside humans . <p> Planet Earth : Bin Chicken cleverly shows how urban ibis challenge the myth of a city-wilderness divide . Here , moreover , urban Australia is facing a deadly rubbish overload . The winner in this tale of ecological ruin and over-consumption is the ibis , shown god-like at the film 's end as the new overlord of Planet Bin Chicken . <p> All this ibis anthropomorphism raises questions about how we interpret new human-animal relations in the Anthropocene : the current geological epoch forged by humans and our devastating environmental impact . The ibis is not alone among animals across the world that are adapting to life beside humans .
