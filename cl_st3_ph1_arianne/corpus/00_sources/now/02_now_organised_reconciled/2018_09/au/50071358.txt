<h> Another reason for the western swamp tortoise to smile <p> I defy anyone to look at a western swamp tortoise 's smile and not smile back . <p> But behind their eternally cheery grins , these little reptiles hide a lot of hardship . <p> At one point , things got so rough that they were actually thought to be extinct . At their worst population crash , it was estimated that only 20 to 30 individuals were left in the wild . <p> Thankfully , Perth Zoo , the Department of Biodiversity , Conservation and Attractions ( DBCA ) and the WA community all pulled together to help save the species -- most recently , releasing 12 captive bred tortoises into Ellen Brook Nature Reserve . <p> How can you resist this little face ? <p> But how did these animals get in such dire straits in the first place ? <h> Left high and dry <p> Western swamp tortoises are very picky about their habitat . They like ephemeral wetlands , which are wetlands that are wet in winter and spring , then dry up in the summer . Their breeding program has been a great success , with more than 700 tortoises bred and released into the wild . <p> The latest tortoises released by Perth Zoo have another extra special job to do . They 're part of a research release , which will help us help them in the future . <h> A tortoise transmission <p> The released tortoises at Ellen Brook Nature Reserve were fitted with two trackers . The first was a basic radio transmitter so that researchers could find the tortoises again after they disappeared under the murky clay waters .
