<p> People generally assume that when markets run out of one resource or , in the case of whales , exterminate several species , then civilization will find a new resource that replaces the old one -- the way renewables are supposed to retire fossil fuels . Right ? <p> Oilmen , of course , like to think the 1859 Pennsylvania oil boom , which poured kerosene into the North American marketplace , slackened the demand for whale oil as a source of illumination . <p> But the historical evidence , as documented by York in his essay , shows that most of the concerted killing of whales took place in the 20th century and largely after the Second World War thanks to fossil fuels and other innovations . <p> Oil simply made it possible to kill more whales , and more efficiently , than ever before . <p> York has checked the evidence and it strongly suggests that market economies are n't using solar , wind or geothermal to retire oil , gas or coal , but to boost overall energy consumption . <p> Moreover the oilman and the greenie share a glaring blind spot : they both @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to a better future . <p> Unfortunately , that assumption is n't worth a Tesla production promise : the energy world , a complex technical system , does n't move in a linear fashion . <p> The simple reason has to do with the very nature of technology itself , explains York . <p> Technology , whether fossil-fuelled or green , increases products and services that use energy , which in turn creates more demand . <p> York deftly summarizes the issue . " Technologies are typically deployed to increase profits , not to conserve resources , " he writes . " Producers work to create markets and expand consumption of their products so as to further the accumulation of wealth . " <p> This not only explains why oil did n't save the whales , but it also explains why savings made through energy efficiency generally do n't translate into reductions in energy use .
