Both began as scientists of the front rank ; both are good ( Ehrlich ) or gifted ( Suzuki ) communicators . Ehrlich is the archetypal prophet of doom . His 1968 bestselling book The Population Bomb earned him a regular spot beside Johnny Carson on The Tonight Show . <p> Erhlich became infamous for his forecasts of the coming apocalypse . It was too late , he said , to save humanity from the ravages of overpopulation . Hundreds of millions across the globe were doomed to perish in famines over the next decade . His solution ? Humanitarian triage . Countries too far gone , such as India , had to be cut off from food aid to save others . <p> When Ehrlich 's predictions failed quite spectacularly to materialize , like those forecasting the date of the rapture , he simply made bigger predictions pushed further into the future . Suzuki went full-blown prophet of doom in his 1991 book It 's a Matter of Survival , co-authored with Anita Gordon . It painted a surreal picture of the next half-century under runaway climate change : the tropics become uninhabitable , and by 2020 , Canada is overrun with climate refugees ; Vancouver , Montreal and Toronto swell to populations of more than 10 million , most living in shantytowns outside decaying city cores ; millions more scour the countryside for food . Basically , a scene from the ? post-apocalyptic drama The Road . <p> We 've now got just over a year until 2020 . The last time I looked , Vancouver , Toronto and Montreal were surrounded not by millions in shantytowns , but by million-dollar condos . And I must have missed the swarms of people scavenging the countryside .
