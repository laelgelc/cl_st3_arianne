<p> In the roof of a waste incinerator outside Zurich , the Swiss firm Climeworks has built the world 's first commercial plant to suck CO2 directly from the air . <p> Climeworks says that its direct air capture ( DAC ) process -- a form of negative emissions often considered too expensive to be taken seriously -- costs $600 per tonne of CO2 today . This is partly covered by selling the CO2 to a nearby fruit and vegetable grower for use in its greenhouse . <p> Climeworks hopes to get this down to $100/tCO2 by 2025 or 2030 . It aims to be capturing 1% of global CO2 emissions each year by 2025 . <p> Carbon Brief travelled to the opening of the plant and interviewed co-founder Christoph Gebald to find out more about Climeworks ' ambitions , how the technology works and how it might contribute to global climate goals . <h> Negative emissions <p> Under the 2015 Paris Agreement on climate change , nearly 200 countries agreed to limit warming to " well below " 2C above pre-industrial levels and to aim for no more than @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ " between greenhouse gas emissions sources and sinks in the second half of the century , equivalent to reaching global net-zero emissions . <p> Even before Donald Trump said the US would pull out of the deal , national climate pledges , when viewed cumulatively , fell far short of what would be needed , with the carbon budget for 1.5C set to be used up within as little as four years . <p> This has sparked a growing realisation that so-called negative emissions might be necessary to meet the goals of Paris , where an overspend against the carbon budget is paid back by pulling CO2 from the air . <p> Some estimates suggest as much as five billion tonnes of CO2 ( GtCO2 ) would have to be removed from the atmosphere , and then locked away underground , each year by 2050 . A large hot water storage tank sits alongside , along with two further containers housing control equipment . <p> It works by adsorption -- desorption , with fans blowing ambient air over the capture material , which traps CO2 and water , until they are released with heat . Water is a by-product of the process . The machinery is capable of capturing 900tCO2 per year . <p> Gebald tells Carbon Brief : <p> " With this plant , we can show costs of roughly $600 per tonne of CO2 , which is , of course , if we compare it to a market price , very high .
