<h> A compromise can still break through the fog of climate change policy <p> Yes , a week is a long time in politics but a week after the chief scientist Alan Finkel delivered his review of energy policy to the government is too soon , way too soon , to write off the prospect of bipartisanship on climate action . We can not kiss goodbye to a constructive debate on clean energy and emissions reductions when few have had time to read the 266-page report , let alone come to any conclusions about its recommendations . <p> The Finkel panel knew what it was up against . " The single most important characteristic of any emissions reduction mechanism ... is that it is agreed expeditiously and with sufficient broad-based support that investors can be confident it will endure through many electoral cycles " , the panel said . Let the games begin , it may as well have said . Regrettably , the battle lines over Finkel have been rapidly drawn . <p> The panel 's task was to come up with a blueprint for a reliable , cost-effective @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ sector in line with those expected from everywhere else in the economy and are consistent with Australia 's targets under the Paris Agreement . If nothing changes , electricity emission reductions will be only 5 per cent on 2005 levels by 2030 , compared with the national target of 26 to 28 per cent reductions on 2005 levels . As Finkel explained , there has been disinvestment in the Australian energy industry , leaving the system vulnerable to high prices and shortages , because of the lack of a stable policy framework has made investment risky . <p> Based on his record , Malcolm Turnbull understands the importance of breaking the impasse and wants to set a sustainable course , but not at the cost of his prime ministership . Being ahead of his party on this issue has already cost him the leadership once , so this time around he is going steady , emphasising the need for " a clear national plan founded on economics and engineering " , framing it as a " complex task " , a " hard task " . <p> " Glibness is @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ not going to pay the energy bills , " the Prime Minister has said , in a clear jab at his predecessor Tony Abbott . Mr Abbott , a climate change sceptic , seems determined to wreck the Finkel process , describing it as a " magic pudding " and a " tax on coal " , apparently before having even read the report . It 's hard to say which is blinding him more -- his ongoing rage at losing the leadership or his ideological contempt for those who accept the reality of human-induced climate change , as the Herald does . Either way , Mr Abbott 's readiness to now scaremonger over the renewable energy and emissions reductions benchmarks he championed while in office -- he said of the Paris targets this week that " we should n't be doing that if it 's going to clobber power prices , hurt households and cost jobs " -- has been unedifying . <p> For now we have to take Labor at its word that it is carefully examining the Finkel report , and that the recommendation for a Clean @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ is not a deal breaker . According to Finkel the two types of schemes are neck and neck on their ability to achieve the required emissions reductions . A Clean Energy Target has the advantage of slightly better price outcomes to consumers and the ability to build on the existing Renewable Energy Target framework . The Liberals have ruled out an Emissions Intensity Scheme in any case so a Clean Energy Target is the pragmatic choice . The deal breaker for Labor is rather the prospect that for the sake of appeasing the coal champions clotting around Abbott in the Coalition party room , Mr Turnbull would agree to set the emissions intensity threshold high enough to allow all forms of coal , including new coal-fired power stations , to be certified as producing clean energy . <p> That has n't happened yet . It need not , if common sense prevails . There remains scope , albeit narrow , for negotiation and nuance in finding a Finkel compromise .
