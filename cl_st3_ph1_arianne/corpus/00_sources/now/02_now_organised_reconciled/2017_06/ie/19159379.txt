<p> A SIX-MONTH Icelandic lava field eruption could provide the crucial key for scientists to unlock the role aerosols play in climate change , through their interactions with clouds . <p> An international team of climate scientists , led by the University of Exeter , have meticulously studied the effects that the 2014-15 eruption at Holuhraun , in Iceland had on cloud formations in the surrounding region . <p> They found that the 2014 Holuhraun fissure eruption , the largest since Laki which erupted for eight months in 1783-4 , emitted sulphur dioxide at a higher rate than all 28 European countries added together causing a massive plume of sulphate aerosol particles over the North Atlantic . <p> As would be expected , these aerosols reduced the size of cloud droplets , but contrary to expectations did not increase the amount of water in the clouds . <p> The researchers believe these startling results could significantly reduce uncertainties in future climate projections by outlining the impact of sulphate aerosols formed from human industrial emissions on climate change . <p> The study was published in the scientific journal ' Nature ' @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of the study and from the Mathematics department at the University of Exeter said : <p> " The huge volcanic eruption provided the perfect natural experiment in which to calculate the interaction between aerosols and clouds . <p> We know that aerosols potentially have a large effect on climate , and particularly through their interactions with clouds . However the magnitude of this effect has been uncertain . <p> " This study not only gives us the prospect of ending this uncertainty but , more crucially , offers us the chance to reject a number of existing climate models , which means we can predict future climate change far more accurately than ever before . " <p> Aerosols play a pivotal role in determining the properties of clouds as they act as the nuclei on which water vapour in the atmosphere condenses to form clouds . <p> Sulphate aerosol has long been recognised as the most significant atmospheric aerosol from industrial sources , but other natural sources of sulphate aerosol also exist , including that formed from sulphur dioxide release as a result of volcanic eruptions . <p> The 2014-15 @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of sulphur dioxide every single day during its eruptive phase . <p> Using state-of-the-art climate system models , combined with detailed satellite retrievals supplied by NASA and the Universit ? libre de Bruxelles , the research team were able to study the complex nature of the cloud cover formed as a result of the eruption . <p> They found that the size of the water droplets produce was reduced , which in turn led to cloud brightening -- which results in an increased fraction of incoming sunlight being reflected back into space and , ultimately , providing a cooling effect on the climate . <p> But these aerosols had no discernible effect on many other cloud properties , including the amount of liquid water that the clouds hold and the cloud amount . <p> The team believe the research shows that cloud systems are " well buffered " against aerosol changes in the atmosphere . <p> Professor Jim Haywood , co-author of the paper and also from the University of Exeter added : " Explosive and effusive volcanic eruptions are very different . Without a doubt , the effusive eruption at Holuhraun will become the go-to study in this regard .
