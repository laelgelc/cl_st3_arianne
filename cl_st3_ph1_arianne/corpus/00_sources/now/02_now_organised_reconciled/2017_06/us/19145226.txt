<p> Cooler temperatures allow the coffee to ripen more slowly -- and that means more time to develop more complex flavor elements like acidity and sweetness . <p> But when temperatures rise , as they have slowly been doing in Ethiopia for years , the warmth causes the coffee to ripen too quickly , which means less flavorful beans . <p> A shift in quality is the main difference consumers will see . Even though a decline in suitable farming land would logically lead to a decline in supply , Neuschwander said it 's not likely that coffee drinkers will see any hike in price . <p> Even if production drops in one country , such as Ethiopia , prices could drop at the same time in other major coffee-exporting countries , like Brazil or Vietnam , Neuschwander said . <p> " It takes a lot longer for consumer effects to travel , " Neuschwander said . " It 's a very distorted market as a commodity market . Fifteen million people , or 16% of the population , make a living through coffee farming . <p> Most of those coffee farmers are smallholders , according to World Coffee Research . Smallholders manage modest , usually family-owned farms , which means they might not have the resources available to adapt to these climate changes . With less ability to adjust , they are more likely to stop growing coffee altogether . <p> " The problem is coffee producers are n't paid enough , so helping them adapt to a very difficult complex , changing situation like you see with climate change and extreme weather events is very , very difficult to do , " Neuschwander said . <p> And there 's science to back up the farmers ' observations : Research cited in the Nature Plants study shows an uptick in warmer days in various areas where coffee is grown . <p> On top of the observable effects , Ethiopia 's average annual temperature increased by 1.3 degrees from 1960 to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ decade , and that average could push up temperatures another 3.1 degrees by the 2060s . <p> Ethiopia has also seen big changes in precipitation . Since the mid-1970s , rainfall has decreased by 15% to 20% in the southern part of the nation , and droughts have become more common in all parts of the country for the last 10 to 15 years . <p> None of this is great news for lovers of Ethiopian coffee , who may eventually want to look to other countries for their coffee fix .
