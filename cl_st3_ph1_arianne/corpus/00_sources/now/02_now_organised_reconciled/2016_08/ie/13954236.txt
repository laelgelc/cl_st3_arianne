Monitoring in colonies will help understand how seabirds adapt to climate change . Photograph : Mark Jessopp <p> Seabird colonies extending from Ireland 's Skellig Michael up to the Arctic are being monitored with time-lapse cameras as part of an international project on climate change . <p> University College Cork ( UCC ) scientist Dr Mark Jessopp , who is one of the project leaders , has recently returned from testing the technology in the Spitsbergen archipelago on the Arctic Sea . <p> Black-legged kittiwakes are the current focus of the research , which will be extended to other species , including fulmarks and gannetts , according to Dr Jessopp . <p> " These birds are very responsive to changes in the environment , being dependent for feeding on fish stocks which are in turn being affected by plankton and changing ocean temperatures , " he said . <p> Dr Jessopp , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Renewable Energy , explained that a particularly bad ice year can delay birds from setting up colonies , which in turn affects their breeding cycle . <p> Ocean acidification , caused by chemical changes due to carbon dioxide emissions , is also affecting seabird populations which are at the top of the marine food chain . <p> Working with Dr Tom Hart of Oxford University in England , he has set up a series of cameras located at colonies on Skellig Michael off the Kerry coast , in Wales and Scotland , Iceland , the Faroe islands and Spitsbergen . <p> " We have deliberately chosen some of the most inhospitable sites for the cameras , to test them out over a number of seasons , " Dr Jessopp said . <p> " The cameras are set up about 20 metres away from colonies , which allows for monitoring about 50 nests at a time . " <p> He said images are taken every hour throughout the year and these cover the period when " the birds turn up , nest , the chicks hatch and take flight " . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ we will be using citizen science to engage volunteers , who will in turn " train " computers into recognising and annotating particular types of seabirds , " he said . <p> " We have two years of data to date , and so far we have noticed no big fluctuations in time of breeding ... Within another two years we should be able to draw some conclusions . " <p> Dr Jessopp , who spent four years on South Georgia in Antarctica , received firearms training before undertaking the expedition , due to the risk of polar bear attack . Happily , there were no serious encounters , and he and his colleague , Dr Hart , counted 14 polar bears on this recent trip .
