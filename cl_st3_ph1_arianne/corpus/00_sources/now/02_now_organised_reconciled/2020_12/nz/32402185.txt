<h> Progress on privacy <p> The computer and internet revolution has overturned the world in all sorts of ways , from business to leisure , from lifestyles to record keeping . <p> Traditional privacy has been laid waste in its wake . Vast amounts of data can be stored and retrieved on just about each and every one of us . We can be tracked and , no doubt , traced . <p> The tools of George Orwell 's 1984 Big Brother have well and truly arrived . <p> If we are to maintain a semblance of privacy , we must have safeguards . The law must be on our side . Certainly , technology is not . <p> We give our data away every time we shop , search and share . This is a commodity valued by businesses , advertisers and marketers . Information about us can also be collated , sold and accessed . <p> In this context , the 2020 Privacy Act , which came into law last week , is progress . It updates legislation from 1993 . Analysts see it as a middle-of-the-road measure @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Europe . <p> A key provision is that businesses are to notify information breaches . As evidenced overseas , including in Australia , these breaches are relatively common . <p> This change is overdue . It is about time we knew when information held about us has been compromised . <p> Cyberattacks these days are increasingly common . Companies should no longer be able to avoid the poor publicity of falling foul of such incursions . <p> The Act also lays out privacy principles . <p> Data stored overseas , as much of it is , must at least be at the equivalent of New Zealand 's standards . <p> Concerns have been raised , however , about the low level of fines , up to $10,000 , much less than in Australia . Ameliorating that concern to some degree is the potential for private civil action against breaches . <p> It is ironic , however , that New Zealanders are often their own worst enemies , giving away private information to social media companies and the like with little regard . They are diligent and sensible . <p> Privacy issues and technology will remain in the public eye . It was only last Friday that Victoria University researchers said the Government had the infrastructure for mass or targeted surveillance using facial recognition technology . <p> Police , for example , have spent millions of dollars on systems that can take feeds from CCTV cameras and identify people . Police already have photographs of more than 800,000 people on their database . <p> The police themselves seem to be showing some caution in its use , while the researchers call for more oversight . In particular , facial-recognition algorithms overseas have been blamed for racial and ethnic stereotyping . <p> The police have said they will not use technology for live facial recognition in public spaces , but their new system allows them to do this . <p> Concerning is the fact the police began advancing such technology without first consulting Mr Edwards or making @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Victoria researchers has said the technology is not inherently bad . It could be used , for example , to identify suspects in serious crimes if a threshold of reasonable suspicion is reached . <p> But such systems have immense surveillance power . <p> The Government has responded to the report by calling for advice on whether safeguards at present are sufficient . The privacy commissioner seems satisfied for now . <p> While additional crime-fighting tools are welcome , civil liberties must also be protected . These are more precious than most realise , especially should the political climate change . <p> Liberties gradually and perniciously can be eroded . Technology has made that all the more likely . <p> The rules , safeguards and practices need to be regularly reviewed . Oversight is required .
