The internet and the fast-paced news environment mean that information travels quickly , leaving little time for fact-checking . <p> As a researcher interested in science communication and controversies , I study how scientific misinformation spreads and how to correct it . <p> It is also common to encounter people who are misinformed but do not know it yet . This is often called a dialogue approach to communication . <p> That means you care about the person behind the opinion , even when you disagree . Instead of rejecting their resources , I offered to trade with them . For each of their sources I read , they would read one of mine . <p> It is likely that the misinformation people have received is not coming from a credible source , so you can propose an alternative . For example , you could offer to send them an article from the Centers for Disease Control for medical and health information , the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change for environmental information , or the reputable debunking site Snopes to compare the information . If someone you are talking to is open to learning more , encourage that continued curiosity . <p> It is sometimes hard , inconvenient , or awkward to engage someone who is misinformed . But I feel very strongly that opening ourselves up to have these conversations can help to correct misinformation .
