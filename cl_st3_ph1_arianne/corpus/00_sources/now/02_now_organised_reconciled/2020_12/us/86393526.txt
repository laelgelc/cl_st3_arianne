<p> In response , we must significantly adjust how we approach the problem . Given that we are in the midst of the crisis , policymakers should focus on how to better protect Americans from existing climate-related risks . <h> States are not prepared <p> In the United States , much of this responsibility falls to states and their local partners . To help focus attention on what must be done , the Bloomberg American Health Initiative and the Trust for America 's Health have released a report assessing states ' preparation for the health hazards of climate change , as well as their vulnerability to those hazards . <p> Ominously , our report , which will be released this week as part of the Bloomberg American Health Summit , found that many of the states most at risk from climate change are also the least able to cope with the consequences . These include many states in the Southeast and southern Great Plains , including Mississippi , Oklahoma , South Carolina and Texas.This group faces increased hazards from hurricanes , floods , heat waves and vector-borne diseases , to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the least to prepare . <p> At the same time , we found a great deal of variation : Some states , such as Maryland , Massachusetts and Utah , have been working for years on adapting to climate change . They have identified key risk factors as well as specific populations that are most vulnerable to climate-related hazards . <p> Some locales have moved beyond plans and are taking concrete action . Three years ago , New York City launched " Be A Buddy , " a pilot project in which volunteers check on people at high risk for heat-related illness . Since 2018 , the network has activated 18 times for extreme temperatures , reaching over 1,300 at-risk residents . In Vermont , the department of health and the forestry program have provided more than 1,200 trees to residents in communities that lacked tree cover . Shade is a crucial way to reduce the risk of heat-related illness . <p> On the other end of the spectrum , states such as Georgia , Oklahoma , South Carolina and South Dakota have done less to prepare . We hope that policymakers , officials and the public will use this report as a resource , a tool to help them set ambitious but realistic goals for how to adapt to and prepare for the health impacts of climate change -- not in the future , but today . <h> Different measures for different risks <p> As they increase preparedness , states will need to adopt varying approaches , depending on the risks they face as well as the particular vulnerabilities of their population . For example , California faces a far higher risk of wildfire than Michigan . Florida is more likely to experience a severe hurricane than Idaho . People are more likely to be infected with West Nile virus in Arizona than in Minnesota . <p> Policymakers and planners should also recognize that some populations remain more vulnerable than others . In this way , helping Americans prepare for and adjust to climate change is yet another area where we must counteract the longstanding effects of systemic racism and environmental injustice . <p> As we compiled this report , we were continually reminded of a Chinese proverb : The best time to plant a tree is 20 years ago ; the second-best time is now . Because of past inaction , we no longer have the luxury of planning for a phenomenon that has yet to arrive . Climate change is here , and the best time to respond is now . <p> Megan Latshaw is an associate scientist at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health .
