<p> Every year in the early hours of a December night , in some outer suburb of a far-flung city in the echoing corridors of a vast conference hall , you will find a ragged horde of weary journalists and TV news crews slumped over tables , crashed out on floors , and staring aimlessly at the doors of locked-up coffee shops . <p> That is how the final hours of the annual United Nations climate summit always end up -- be it in Lima , Doha , Warsaw or Marrakesh . <p> Suddenly , there is a flurry of activity as rumours circulate that a deal has been reached by the even more tired , but still talking , teams of national negotiators . <p> It will almost certainly be a false alarm and everyone goes back to their pillow of a rolled-up coat awaiting the mercy of conclusion . And so the talks will go on , until we are told to shuffle back tomorrow . <p> The next day , it starts all over again , lasting long into the night .
