The bear 's paw pads were so burnt that he could barely walk when state wildlife biologists found him and rushed him to a treatment center . <p> Confronted by a surge in the number of hot and fast-moving fires , UC Davis ' School of Veterinary Medicine joined forces with the state Department of Fish and Wildlife to create a network of experts to rescue and rehabilitate bears , mountain lions and other burned animals . <p> The efforts of the Wildlife Disaster Network , launched in October , represent a new strategy for veterinary medicine . The network is modeled after a successful UC Davis program that rescues and treats oiled birds and other wildlife in the wake of oil spills . <p> Volunteering their time , the veterinarians in the new network are performing search-and-rescue operations and triage , in addition to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ devastating wildfires . <p> " When we saw an injured animal in the past , the philosophy was , ' Just let it be . After he was treated and released , the wildlife agency did n't see another burned animal until 2017 , when they treated two bears and a cougar . But in recent decades , forest management strategies have relied on suppressing fires .
