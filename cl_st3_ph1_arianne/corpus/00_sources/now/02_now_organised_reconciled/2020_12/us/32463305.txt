<p> Migrants rest at " La Roca , " or The Rock shelter in Nogales , Sonora state , Mexico , near the border fence that separates Mexico from the US File : AP/Moises Castillo <p> In March , I ended up stuck in the southern Mexican coastal village of Zipolite on account of the pandemic -- an abrupt change of pace from the past 17 years , which I had spent darting schizophrenically between countries . There was no official lockdown or curfew in Zipolite , but checkpoints were installed on either side of the village to restrict access and departures . <p> In a split second , then , my daily routine changed from one of being constantly on the road to one of lying in a hammock watching ants parade across my stomach and thinking of all the things I could be doing were I not lying in a hammock . <p> While the individual days passed excruciatingly slowly , the months flew by . The Washington Post remarks : " Every day is Blursday " . <p> Over at Wired magazine , Duke University cognitive neuroscientist Kevin LaBar explains that the human brain " likes novelty ... Trauma and anxiety also alter the perception of time , as does uncertainty about the future . <p> In my own privileged case of quarantine lite -- in which I have not had to deal with added stressors like unemployment , lack of food , or domestic discord -- the time warp has featured an element of " coronastalgia " , if you will .
