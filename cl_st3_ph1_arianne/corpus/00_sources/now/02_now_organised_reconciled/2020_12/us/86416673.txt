In that time , he suggests , extreme wildfires driven by climate change have burned much of their habitat , and warming temperatures have increased their metabolisms , leading to a food shortage . Furthermore , the increased planting of nonnative tropical milkweed by gardeners who want to help has led to increased breeding during the winter . But because tropical milkweeds do n't die off seasonally , they can accumulate a debilitating , sometimes deadly , parasite that infects monarch larvae . <p> In addition , Shapiro suggests that increased carbon dioxide from California 's fires also could have triggered milkweed to increase production of toxic steroid called cardenolides .
