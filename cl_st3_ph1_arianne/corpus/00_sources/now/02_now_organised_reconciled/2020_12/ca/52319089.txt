That 's how it is for John Degen , executive director of the Writers Union of Canada ( TWUC ) . Degen and his family , who spend as much time as they can at their Thessalon getaway , first found the home just over a year ago , in July 2017 . By Aug. 1 , they owned it . Now , despite full-time jobs in Toronto , they visit at least monthly and more during the summer . " I can leave Etobicoke at 5 a.m. and be in my chair here reading just after noon . And driving is part of the fun , " Degen told the Sault Star in a recent interview . " I love it up here , " says Degen . So does his wife , Julia . That made the decision to buy a home here an easy one to make . So how did this town 's little secret get out ? Degen first got a close look at Thessalon in 2007 when he came for a literary event at the request of acquaintances Gordon Graham and Angie Gallop who 'd moved to Thessalon recently themselves . A writer as well , Degen was in town to read from his then just published novel , The Uninvited Guest . Since the novel was a fictional tale about three men , including one who was keeper of the Stanley Cup , promoting it in a northern hockey town seemed appropriate . When Degen was hired to serve as literature officer for the Ontario Arts Council ( OAC ) just over a year later , his eyes were opened to the rest of the province outside of Toronto . His job saw him frequently travelling the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to artists and writers . Degen noticed statistics that showed much of the OAC funding was distributed in or close to the Greater Toronto Area and , despite there being a lack of applications from the north , he knew there were writers here . That 's when he implemented the Northern Writers Works in Progress , a program that reserves money specifically for Northern Ontario residents . " I hope it 's impacted some careers , " Degen said . Eventually , Degen began keeping an eye on real estate , thinking if he ever saw an affordable summer home here , he 'd buy . And then he and Julia agreed on something : Why just a summer home ? Why not buy a place that could eventually become a permanent home ? " I 'm a writer -- I can work anywhere , " he says . Southern Ontario is so congested it 's becoming unliveable . Additionally , climate change has made summers even more humid and winters worse than ever . " It 's not only things like weather and congestion that combine to make Toronto a place that does n't always work for the Degens . High housing costs mean most people working in the arts have been priced right out of downtown . And though Degen commutes from his Etobicoke home to the TWUC offices in downtown Toronto , his job is becoming more and more internet-based , with business conducted with such tools as e-mail and Skype . When not working online , much of Degen 's work for TWUC sees him on the road lobbying in Ottawa or overseas , working for the international side of things . " With really good internet , working from Northern Ontario is becoming more and more likely , " he said . Recently , he participated in a meeting of the National Reading Campaign from his back deck in Thessalon . While the National Reading Campaign is a volunteer organization that 's turned Sudbury into a reading town , Sudbury is of interest to the family for reasons other than literature . Now that their teenaged sons -- twins -- have spent time in Thessalon , they 're eying both Laurentian and Algoma universities as possible choices for continuing education . Despite only being here on weekends and for a few weeks in the summer , Degen has embraced the community , greeting locals by name as they pass and talking about what he read in the local newspapers . " I was reading in the Sault Star about the Sault 's revitalization , " he says . They are setting themselves up as a liveable community , something Sudbury has already been successful with . " Continued growth in southern Ontario is going to make things even worse , but Degen predicts , once Highway 69 is completed , the blind spot will go away and there will be a shift toward the north . " I feel like we 're getting in here ahead of a trend , " Degen said . Degen plans to write a series of fictional novels , dealing with murder , in Algoma . It 's part of his five-year plan and a first draft of one , titled Blind River , is almost complete . For now , he 'll keep working his day job . Dubbed the national voice of book authors , TWUC currently has more than 2,100 members . The organization is also currently reviewing the Copyright Act . " We 're very active to ensure author 's rights are n't weakened , " Degen said . Another aspect of TWUC is the International Authors Forum , an umbrella organization that extends around the world . " Authors everywhere have the same experiences and issues as Canadian authors . In a way we 've been leading the conversation in talking about conditions and rights , " he says . Then he adds that he likes to say he 's actively trying to work himself out of a job . If that happens he 'll be prepared for his second career -- being a full-time author . That brings Degen to one good thing about Toronto ; when he commutes , he writes . He worked on sets for such shows as Royal Canadian Air Farce and Kids in the Hall and when he finished university , he started working full-time until layoffs in the early 1990s sent him back to freelance work . But freelancing in Toronto is not feasible with a house and kids . And though Uninvited Guest had good success -- it was even short-listed for Amazon.ca/Books in Canada First Novel Award -- kids and a full-time job meant getting back to writing took longer than he 'd planned .
