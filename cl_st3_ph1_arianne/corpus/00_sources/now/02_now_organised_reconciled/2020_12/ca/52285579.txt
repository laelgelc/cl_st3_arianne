<h> What We 're Watching : Visions of vaccines ( and party donations ) <p> Having finally managed to get MPs to sign off on his proposed changes to the laws on access to medical assistance in dying -- just one day before the Commons shut down for the holidays -- Justice Minister David Lametti now faces an even tighter turnaround time in the Senate . This is courtesy of both the looming end of the sitting and the court-imposed deadline to rewrite the sections that a Quebec judge concluded were unconstitutionally restrictive , both of which kick in on Friday . <p> As of now , it 's not clear whether senators will be willing to put the bill on the pre-holiday legislative fast track . The legal and constitutional affairs committee has already conducted a pre-study of the contents , though , which could reduce the time required for debate . <p> It does not , however , reduce the chances that one or more senators puts forward amendments . If duly adopted , this would mean an automatic return trip to the Commons for re-approval . As the Canadian Press points out , this could be tricky to arrange on the fly , since the all-party deal to allow hybrid sittings expired last week . <p> Given the chances the bill wo n't make it to the legislative finish line by Dec. 18 , Lametti announced last week he 's taken " the prudent step " of formally asking the court to extend the deadline to Feb. 21 , 2021 . <p> " We should have never had to do this , but the Conservatives ' tactics have put Canadians in a potentially precarious situation , " he said via Twitter , though it seems he has n't abandoned his goal of meeting the original cutoff . <p> " I hope the extension will not be needed , " he said . " I am optimistic that senators can pass this by the 18th . We know Canadians , especially those who are suffering , are anxious to see these amendments come into effect . We will continue to do everything in our power to see them passed . " <p> First wave of vaccines hits Canadian soil <p> With the first batch of an expected steady stream of Pfizer-BioNTech 's COVID-19 vaccines arriving in Canada early this week -- 30,000 doses in the first shipment , 249,000 by the end of the year , and up to four million by April -- all eyes will be on the cross-country rollout plans that Ottawa and the provinces have been scrambling to arrange over the last few weeks . <p> According to the latest round of technical briefings , that includes everything from storage and transport protocols to deciding how to divvy up the available doses , both among the provinces and within high-risk groups . <p> Unforeseen complications in the plan 's initial execution are inevitable , however , whether in the logistics of ferrying the doses to their destinations , or determining who gets the first jabs . The test for the governments -- federal and provincial -- will be how swiftly they can adapt to changing circumstances without eroding public confidence in either their ability to run a massive vaccination program or the vaccine itself . <p> First day on the job for newly installed Finance deputy , Michael Sabia <p> While the PMO-issued notice of his appointment will likely be the only official public pronouncement on the start of Michael Sabia 's open-ended tenure as deputy minister of Finance , it 's a safe bet that monetary-policy wonks and deficit hawks alike will be working their contacts inside and outside the department for any intel on what to expect from the new era . Sabia 's tenure will begin Monday , less than two weeks after his predecessor , Paul Rochon , announced he was stepping down . <p> In an interview that appeared in the Globe and Mail over the weekend , Sabia describes himself as " technocrat , " with a r ? sum ? that includes " three decades at the top rungs of corporate Canada , " in addition to his recent stint as chair of the Canada Infrastructure Bank . In the interview , Sabia says Canada needs " to be on a better growth trajectory , " and suggests that investing with that goal in mind could " trigger a virtuous circle , " in which " our fiscal realities become easier to manage . " <p> Among his " priorities , " according to the Globe : " expanding the labour force by improving daycare , helping the oil patch cope with climate change by investing in carbon-capture technology , and pushing for innovation in every corner of the economy " -- albeit while also keeping a watchful eye on the " record red ink " in the latest economic update . <p> As former Bank of Canada governor Stephen Poloz told the Globe : " There are all kinds of ideas floating around , all sorts of ways to spend money , and the role of the Finance department is to bring structure to the conversation . " <p> With a full-fledged budget expected in the spring , Sabia will likely want to kickstart those discussions sooner rather than later .
