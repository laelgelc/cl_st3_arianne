<p> Quesnel Mayor Bob Simpson says pulp mills , including two in his town , will lose money if Site C dam is approved . Some mills sell power to B.C. Hydro by burning waste wood . <p> As the deadline nears for the B.C. government 's decision on whether to proceed with the $9-billion ( and counting ) Site C dam , one aspect of the controversial B.C. Hydro project deserves more attention : What do rising hydro rates and a glut of power mean for the financial viability of numerous forest companies and the rural communities in which they operate ? <p> Contrary to what many British Columbians think , many forest companies produce more than just lumber , panels , pulp and pellets . Some also produce power that is sold to B.C. Hydro . <p> Currently , 17 B.C. firms produce a combined 850 megawatts of " biomass " power -- 77 per cent of the power equivalency of Site C should that dam be completed . <p> Chemical pulp mills , in particular , are at the forefront of forest company gains in energy efficiency @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ $1-billion " green transformation " fund initiated by the federal government that assisted nearly 100 mills across Canada . <p> In my community of Quesnel , where more than 450 people are employed in two pulp mills , the fund provided $41.5 million to Cariboo Pulp and Paper to install equipment and gain efficiencies that allowed it to produce enough electricity to power more than 14,500 homes annually and to slash its greenhouse gas emissions by 18,000 tonnes . <p> But with Site C 's potential to add another 1,100 megawatts of hydropower to a province already awash in electricity , questions must be asked . <p> What happens when Cariboo Pulp and Paper 's sales agreement with B.C. Hydro expires ? What happens when the agreements with B.C. 's other 16 biomass power suppliers expire ? A strong possibility is that such agreements wo n't be renewed and that as they lapse , mills will be far less financially viable . <p> The other pulp mill in town , Quesnel River Pulp , is an energy intensive operation that uses mechanical rather than chemical means to make pulp . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ hydro rates pose serious competitiveness issues that can put these mills and the jobs they create at risk . <p> The two mills mentioned above represent 34 per cent of Quesnel 's tax base . Together , they paid $4.9 million in municipal taxes last year . But , the significant contribution these operations make to the fiscal sustainability of our community and the formidable energy pricing challenges these companies face rarely get mentioned as the government moves toward a decision on Site C. Also not mentioned is that B.C. Hydro has indicated that it is unlikely to renew at least half of its biomass power agreements and will seek to slash purchase prices in the contracts it does renew . <p> So , between walking away from an alternate renewable energy source that taxpayers basically paid to build and escalating pricing , the economic viability of the pulp sector is being put at risk for a hydroelectric project that will not have any significant job supporting impact on B.C. 's economy once it is built . <p> Making matters more pressing , B.C. 's pulp-and-paper industry has enormous untapped @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ for petro-chemical products , including biofuels , resins and bio-chemicals that can serve as feedstock for bio-plastics . <p> This is precisely what B.C. forest companies should be encouraged to do , especially given what climate change has unleashed in our forests in the form of ferocious wildfires and unprecedented insect and disease outbreaks . <p> In response to these climate-change related events , the industry must work with smaller , younger trees and be relentless in reducing the amount of usable wood left behind at logging sites . However , this much-needed shift to bioproducts is being severely hampered by current energy policy ( i.e. walking away from the already existing bioenergy contracts ) and escalating energy pricing . <p> If Site C proceeds , we will not only be walking away from taxpayer-funded bio-energy facilities but risking future investment in B.C. for bio-fuels and bioproducts , which the world desperately needs and in which B.C. could be a leader . <p> The provincial government should reject what amounts to a political boondoggle of its predecessor 's making .
