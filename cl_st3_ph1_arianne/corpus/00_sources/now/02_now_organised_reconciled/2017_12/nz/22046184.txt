<h> The NZ tech researchers working to make asthmatics ' lives a little easier <p> Scientists hope to help asthma sufferers and others needing oxygen at home by developing ' molecular sponges ' with nanoscale-sized pores to purify the air . <p> There 's possibly nothing more frightening than struggling to take a breath . Something asthmatics and others with respiratory diseases know all too well . <p> Many of these people depend on portable oxygen concentrators , small machines that concentrate oxygen out of air . But there are limitations on the portability and longevity of these devices . <p> New technology allowing small , lightweight , more efficient and affordable oxygen concentrators could underpin a new generation of portable oxygen devices used in hospitals and in people 's homes . And it turns out that the fastest growing class of materials in chemistry , metal-organic frameworks ( MOFs ) , may be able to make this happen . <p> A MacDiarmid team of researchers is looking at using MOFs to enrich oxygen from air , as Massey professor and MacDiarmid researcher Shane Telfer explains . <p> " MOFs @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , with a metal at the corners and an organic component as the rods or linkers . " <p> To understand how MOFs work , Telfer says , you need look no further than your kitchen sponge . " The pores in your kitchen sponge are the same size as a water droplet . That 's why your sponge works . The pores in metal organic frameworks ( MOFs ) can be made the same size as gas molecules . That 's how they can absorb O2 . <p> " One of our MacDiarmid PhD students has run calculations to show that if you pass air through a MOF you can capture the oxygen . So now we are trying to replicate this experimentally to find a MOF that is more efficient , cheaper and has a longer lifespan than the current oxygen generation technology . Ideally , it 'll also be resistant to other components in the air such as water vapour . We 'd be able to patent a material like that . " <p> Researchers worldwide are finding these three-dimensional ' molecular sponges ' , with pores @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , rather useful across a range of areas . <p> One of these is climate change remediation . Absorbing CO2 is high on the priority list right now , with many companies ( such as Mosaic Materials , a start up based in Berkeley , California ) seeking to commercialise MOFs to absorb CO2 , from smoke stack emissions to prevent global warming . MOFs can also store other gases , such as hydrogen and methane , for fuel . <p> The MacDiarmid team , which pulls together researchers from all over New Zealand , includes Professor Paul Kruger from the University of Canterbury , who is looking into precisely this area -- how MOFs can store gases for fuel or capture CO2 from emission streams -- and Dr Carla Meledandri from Otago University , who is investigating new and more efficient ways to make known MOFs . <p> Telfer says MOFs are useful for more than just gas capture . " MOFs could also deliver a drug to a specific site within the body . " Like much of materials science , most current MOF research is focused on pushing back the boundaries of fundamental knowledge . " <p> Yet MOFs are soon to be used commercially in New Zealand , with a new technology to slow fruit-ripening licensed for use here by Irish company MOF Technologies . <p> Telfer says the potential applications from MOFs would fit well with the manufacturing landscape in New Zealand . <p> " We 're are looking to work with New Zealand companies to develop new MOFs for various gas storage and separation applications .
