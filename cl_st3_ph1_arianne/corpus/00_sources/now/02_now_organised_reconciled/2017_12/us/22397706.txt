<p> The storm approached from the ocean like an invading force , picking up fuel in the late summer waters . Soon , the tides swelled and the moisture-laden clouds released their loads , turning Charleston 's streets into rivers and swaths of South Carolina into disaster areas . <p> Tropical Storm Irma in September gave the aptly named Lowcountry yet another reminder of the dangers posed by extreme weather in an age of climate change and rising seas . The storm swamped homes and businesses across the city -- many for the third time in as many years -- and turned the vital medical district into an island . <p> The storm also exposed just how much work remains to address the problem . <p> Irma 's 4-foot surge brought our surrounding waters to roughly the same level many scientists believe our seas will reach in 80 years . Lower-end predictions call for a 1.5-foot rise , while higher-end ones come in at 8 feet and above . <p> After years of slow-walking a response to the problem , Charleston has emerged as a regional leader .
