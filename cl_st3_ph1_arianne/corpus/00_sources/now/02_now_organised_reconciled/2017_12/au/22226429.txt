<p> The Sumatran rhinoceros ( Dicerorhinus sumatrensis ) , one of the most threatened mammals on earth , has seen its numbers dwindle through human activity , but its troubles began with climate change about a million years ago , around the middle of the Pleistocene , according to a new study published in the journal Current Biology . <p> An international team of researchers has sequenced and analysed the first Sumatran rhino genome from a sample belonging to a male animal , Ipuh , which lived at Ohio 's Cincinnati Zoo , in the US , for 22 years until his death in 2013 . <p> " This species has been well on its way to extinction for a very long time , " adds Terri Roth , from the Centre for Conservation and Research of Endangered Wildlife at the Cincinnati Zoo . <p> The new insight into rhino demographic history is useful for placing the current population status of the species into a broader ecological and evolutionary context , the researchers say . <p> Today , the World Wildlife Fund estimates fewer that 100 Sumatran rhinos remain in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ fossil evidence shows an invasion of continental mammals into Sundaland , a biogeographical region of south-eastern Asia , around 900,000 years ago , according to the researchers . ( Effective population size is the size of a population consistent with optimum genetic diversity . It gives an estimate of the number of reproducing individuals at any given time . ) <p> By 9000 years ago , the genome evidence suggests , the effective population size was reduced to only about 700 animals . The findings suggest that climate change in the distant past reduced the genetic diversity of Sumatran rhinos , leaving them even more vulnerable to later pressures from human activity . <p> The rhino Ipuh , named for the locality on the island of Sumatra where he was originally collected , fathered two male offspring that live at the Sumatran Rhino Sanctuary in Sumatra .
