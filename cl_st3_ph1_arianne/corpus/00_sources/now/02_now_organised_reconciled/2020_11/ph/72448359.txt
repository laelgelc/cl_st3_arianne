<h> OPINION In defense of resilience <p> With typhoons Rolly and Ulysses adding to the dire impact of COVID-19 on the country , and emotions still running high because of the supposed glamorization of people 's resilience and the eschewing of accountability by the government , let me put out an idea , if not manifesto , in defense of resilience . <p> " Community self-organization and wider good governance define resilience to disasters , " as Collins put it in his seminal article on advancing the disaster and development paradigm . <p> Unpacking this complex idea is essential in invoking the theory and practice of resilience-building , and in developing needed capacities at different levels of our society , from the national to the individual . <p> This is critical , as we are facing concurrent emergencies and disasters coming our way , one with another , one after the other , seemingly in perpetuity . <p> High exposure to multiple hazards , extreme vulnerability , and staggering risks to health and well-being , economy , and society -- these require a clear definition of resilience , a word with which all of these are associated . <h> What is resilience and what are its components ? <p> Resilience as a word is often used , interpreted loosely , and depicted vaguely . Getting to the definition of resilience is not for mere academic discourse in universities elsewhere , but is a matter of life and death for a hazard-prone country like ours . We are and will be living with climate and disaster impacts , and health and development issues for a good measure of our existence . With COVID-19 still within our midst , we know that resilience -- whatever we really think of it -- is one thing that we should have . <p> It is imperative to know what resilience is all about if we are to make it happen . <p> Sifting through 83 definitions and 46 frameworks on resilience from 5 decades of work , Manyena et al in their treatise on the transformational nature of resilience found out that resilience is all about capacities . <p> Resilience is having the capacity to withstand the negative effects and impacts of destabilizing events due to various disasters and even day-to-day interruptions . This entails having the capacities to address the multifaceted drivers of risks -- the same things that can be construed as elements of resilience in any sound disaster risk reduction framework . <p> These are : individual behavior , the structural context ( including political will ) , and education , science , and technology . <p> Looking at the unfortunate flooding incident in Cagayan Valley that killed scores of people -- that the local government dubbed as their worst flooding event in 40 years -- and the recent Marikina deluge that is reminiscent of the Ondoy flooding in 2009 , flood risk and exposure can be analyzed using the said framework . <p> Risk and resilience are a function of human exposure and behavior , the prevention and response systems and policy processes of the government , and environmental changes . <p> Prevention and response systems include political will , adaptive capacity , role management , governance style ( especially focused on prioritization of resources and politics of assistance ) , environmental maintenance and governance , and assumption of responsibility . <p> Environmental changes on the other hand are largely determined by science and approaches being undertaken , looking at predictability , uncertainty , and opportunity for precautionary actions . These are related to the dynamics of hard and soft catchment , river and coastal management , dam management , and long-term environmental management . <h> How can resilience be applied ? <p> While the real cause of the inundation of communities can not be determined with certainty -- release of water from dams , swelling of rivers after continuous raining , deforestation , plain topography of areas being a " catch basin , " lack of early warning , failure of evacuation , or poorly executed response -- resilience should have saved the lives of people if capacities to prevent , anticipate , prepare , and respond had been built . Furthermore , capacities to build back better and bounce forward could have addressed the issue . <p> Resilience-building should aim at enhancing 3 levels of capacities . First , resilience entails improving the capacity of communities for self-organization so they are empowered to do collective action . This includes enabling them to tap available resources within their reach and to harness their social capital so they are guided by shared values that can serve as impetus for purpose-driven initiatives . <p> Second , resilience means bolstering the capacity of the government for leadership and governance . This should generate effective strategies for disaster risk reduction and management to make sure people are out of harm 's way and cushioned , if not shielded , from the impacts of any disaster . Accountability is a crucial dimension of this political domain . <p> Finally , resilience should spur the development of capacities to facilitate use of scientific evidence to educate and mobilize people and to aid in the evidence-informed decision-making of leaders . This can also be complemented by efforts to institutionalize resilience in the curriculum and to embed it in our culture -- our way of life . <h> Why the call for resilience ? <p> As the resilience definition and application are already made clear and capacities at all levels described , we need to talk more about resilience . <p> Resilience is in governance and governance is in resilience . The government has the chief responsibility for any resilience-building initiative . Disaster risk reduction , climate change adaptation , sustainable development -- all of these are underpinned by resilience . The government should muster all of its efforts to implement resilient programs and processes and to produce resilient outcomes and impacts as well . <p> Communities and people will benefit from this resilience and governance symbiosis , but they can also take an active role . They have a wealth of experiences and resources to invoke and tap respectively . They just need to be involved and empowered . A lot can happen when a community owns resilience . <p> Finally , science and education should lead the way . Evidence is growing on resilient good practices , and more lessons are coming to the fore ( on how not to be resilient ) , waiting to be learnt and relearnt . The opportunities and possibilities to live and thrive in the new normal -- with resilience as guide -- are opening up . <p> If resilience can teach us a thing or two about how to go about our lives , these days and in the next generations to come , it is that even in the most unforgiving of storms , resilience can really make a big difference . <p> Ronald Law is a physician-public health practitioner with expertise in health emergency and disaster risk management . He is also a public health professor and academic , being a former Australian leadership fellow and Fulbright scholar .
