<h> ' Tree equity ' : U.S. cities urged to focus planting in areas most at risk <p> WASHINGTON ( Thomson Reuters Foundation ) - U.S. cities are increasingly recognizing trees as critical infrastructure but still need help figuring out where to plant them , conservation advocates said Tuesday as they launched a tool to map where greenery is most needed . <p> Beyond urban tree cover , officials also need to look at local data on income , race , age and employment , as well as average temperature and population density , said American Forests , the country 's oldest national conservation nonprofit . <p> The group released a tool on Tuesday that uses these metrics to map what it calls " tree equity " down to the neighborhood level in three locations : the Phoenix area in Arizona , California 's San Francisco Bay area and the state of Rhode Island . <p> " We 're not just showing you where there are n't trees but also the highest consequences - higher health or economic <p> Urban trees can trap pollutants and significantly cool local environments , research has shown , and investments in urban forests can create thousands of jobs . <p> Those effects can prove critical in low-income communities , Daley said , where residents may lack good health insurance or the means to run air conditioning , especially amid global warming . <p> Early findings from the Tree Equity Score show that poorer neighborhoods have fewer trees than more affluent areas , which American Forests links to " a legacy of disinvestment " , including decades-old federal housing segregation . <p> In most U.S. cities , marginalized communities lack equitable access to nature and green space , said Dan Lambe , president of the Arbor Day Foundation , which focuses on tree
