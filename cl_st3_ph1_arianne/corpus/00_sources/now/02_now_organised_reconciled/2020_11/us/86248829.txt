<h> Marshall Islands could be lost to climate change as early as 2080 , new study finds <p> The Marshall Islands could be lost to sea level rise as early as 2080 , according to geological data analyzed by University of Hawaii researchers . <p> Geologists Haunani Kane and Charles Fletcher researched 5,000 years of geological history to project sea level rises . The study aims to reduce confusion about the effect of high sea levels on low-lying islands . Researchers found that preserving coral reef systems may mitigate the damage caused by sea level rise . <p> The research analyzed historical photographs , fossil data and modern observations to define thresholds where islands transition from stable to unstable , Kane wrote in the study . <p> Findings published in the Earth Future 's journal , which examines the state of the planet and its inhabitants , suggest higher tides and destructive waves will deteriorate freshwater resources by the midcentury . <p> Results show higher water levels will increase chances of extreme flooding , and saltwater resources will inundate the islands -- unless inhabitants adopt measures to adapt to the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ erosion by restoring coral reef systems that produce sediment and protect the coast , the researchers suggest . <p> Communities can also build natural wetlands , coastal buffers and other nature projects to extend the distance from the shoreline to inhabitant 's homes . <p> Atoll islands are low-lying locations made up of sand and gravel that support freshwater resources and lush forests . <p> Notable for their ring-shaped coral reef , the future evolution of atoll islands requires an interdisciplinary approach to study the loss of remote islands . Rising sea levels threaten the political sovereignty of island nations and the land 's place as an inhabited location . <p> Since atoll islands have been subjected to rising sea levels before , scientists debate the impacts of additional increase in levels . Conflicting messages disagree on the impact of sea level rise to islands and their communities . <p> According to Kane , her team 's model treats islands and their people as inseparable components , unlike researchers who separate an island from the inhabitant 's identity .
