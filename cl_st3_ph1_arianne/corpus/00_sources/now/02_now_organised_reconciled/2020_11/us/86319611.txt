<p> Long blue tanks sit in a cinderblock building near the employee-only entrance of the Phoenix Zoo . Water percolating in the tanks echoes softly through the room . <p> They appear to be empty until -- " there 's one , in the corner ! " says Tara Harris , the zoo 's conservation and science director . She points to an inch-and-half-long spotted frog , crouched in the corner of the tank . <p> The place may not be glamorous , but it 's the center of a 25-year-long effort to save this small , spotted , slippery species , the Chiricahua leopard frog . <p> These particular tanks are currently home to six of the threatened amphibians , which are native to Arizona . They were named after the Chiricahua mountains in the southeast corner of the state , where researchers first documented them . These six frogs are from a higher-altitude population in northern Arizona and will go on to breed new generations of their species . <p> In the 1980s , Chiricahua leopard frogs had declined across nearly 80% of their range in Arizona . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and groundwater pumping , but a drastic die-off in the 1990s was primarily a result of something else : a fungus-fueled skin disease that was decimating amphibian populations across the globe , including the leopard frog in Arizona . <p> In response , in 1995 the Phoenix Zoo launched a leopard frog recovery program . What started with just a few small tanks in a curator 's office has become a robust conservation effort with partners from the state and federal government , as well as private landowners . <p> When the program started , Chiricahua leopard frogs were found in only 38 locations across the state . Today , there are 150 sites where the frogs live . More than 26,000 tadpoles and frogs raised at the zoo have been released into the wild since the program began . <h> Zoo gives frogs a ' head start ' <p> The six frogs in the zoo 's conservation center are the latest part of that effort .
