( AP ) <p> More than four billion people could be overweight by 2050 , with 1.5 billion of them obese , if the current global dietary trend towards processed foods continues , a first-of-its-kind study has predicted . <p> Warning of a health and environmental crisis of " mind-blowing magnitude " , experts from the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research ( PIK ) said that global food demand would leap 50 percent by mid-century , pushing past Earth 's capacity to sustain nature . <p> Food production already hoovers up three-quarters of the world 's fresh water and one-third of its land and accounts for up to a third of greenhouse gas emissions . <p> Providing a long-term overview of changing global eating habits between 1965 and 2100 , the researchers used an open-source model to forecast how food @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ population growth , ageing , growing body masses , declining physical activity and increased food waste . <p> They found that " business as usual " , a continuation of current trends , will likely see more than four billion people , or 45 percent of the world 's population , overweight by 2050 . <p> " The increasing waste of food and the rising consumption of animal protein mean that the environmental impact of our agricultural system will spiral out of control , " said Benjamin Bodirsky , lead author of the study published in Nature Scientific Reports . <p> " Whether greenhouse gasses , nitrogen pollution or deforestation : we are pushing the limits of our planet , and exceeding them . " <p> While trends vary between regions , the authors said that global eating habits were moving away from plant- and starch-based diets to more " affluent diets high in sugar , fat , and animal-source foods , featuring highly-processed food products " . <p> At the same time , the study found that as a result of increasing inequality along with food waste and loss @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ lack of storage or overbuying , around half a billion people will still be undernourished by mid-century . <p> " There is enough food in the world , the problem is that the poorest people on our planet have simply not the income to purchase it , " said co-author Prajal Pradhan . <p> " And in rich countries , people do n't feel the economic and environmental consequences of wasting food . " <p> The UN 's Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change warned in a special report last year that humanity will face increasingly painful trade-offs between food security and rising temperatures within decades unless emissions are curbed and unsustainable farming and deforestation are halted .
