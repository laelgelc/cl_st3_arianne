<h> Jakarta is running out of time to stop itself sinking into the sea <h> The parks where kids play are vanishing , houses with their own backyards are gone , even the cemeteries are disappearing as an entire city sinks towards disaster . <p> 24 June 2019 <p> Year after year , centimetre by centimetre , Rudi Suwandi 's home in north-west Jakarta is being swallowed . <p> The trees and park where kids would play have vanished , houses with their own backyards - gone , entire rooms of Rudi 's house have been consumed . Even the village cemetery where his ancestors and thousands of others are buried has disappeared . <p> In its place lies stagnant , murky water . <p> Rudi first remembers the water appearing in the mid-90s , when the village started flooding every rainy season . Every year , it would get higher and take longer to drain away . <p> In 1995 it was 20 centimetres deep and took three to four months to dry out . By the early 2000s his village , Kapuk Teko , was permanently flooded . <p> Now , entire areas are submerged two metres @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the lake , connected by gangplanks and concrete paths . The only way into Rudi 's village is via a low , narrow concrete bridge . <p> " The places where we were born , played and grew up , all are gone , " Rudi said . " Everyone here feels deeply sad about what 's happened to our village , the neighbourhood with trees and land to play has all gone . " <p> For years , the ground beneath their village has been sinking . And it 's not just here . It 's happening right across Jakarta . <p> Roads are warping , breakwalls have been swallowed and entire buildings have been left abandoned . <p> Worst of all , the city , which straddles the Java Sea and serves as the exit point for a handful of major rivers , is more prone than ever to flooding . <p> Researchers at Indonesia 's Bandung Institute of Technology ( ITB ) have been at the forefront of tracking the phenomenon , known as land subsidence , for more than two decades . That means these areas are sinking faster than any other city in the world . <p> The subsidence of the city combined with climate change-induced sea level rise creates a perfect storm of natural disaster . <p> " If we model this and project forward , around 95 per cent of northern Jakarta 's surface in 2050 will be under the sea , " said Heri Andreas , an expert on land subsidence at ITB . <p> " That means if we do n't protect Jakarta with a seawall or other means , then Jakarta would be inundated by sea water . " <p> Without major intervention to prevent sinking , Dr Andreas ' research team predicts that more than a quarter of the city could be swamped by the sea by 2025 . By comparison , built-up Sydney has about 2,000 people per square kilometre . <p> The data paints a staggering picture of consistently high densityacross large parts of Jakarta . As a result , the city is buckling under the pressure . <p> Jakarta 's traffic gridlock is infamous ; it regularly ranks among the most congested cities in the world . This congestion contributes to the city 's poor air quality . <p> On top of that , only a tiny portion of the city 's wastewater is treated . Much of it pours into the city 's vast networks of rivers and canals . <p> Satellite imagery captured each decade from the 1970s shows just how much the city 's urban reach @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ has not matched the speed of growth . <p> Large parts of the city do not have access to or can not afford piped water . Instead , construction sites , shopping centres , high-rise apartments and residents are extracting huge amounts of water , often illegally , from the aquifers below Jakarta . <p> " The formula is simple ... if we can stop using the groundwater , the subsidence will stop , " Professor Andreas said . <p> " The question is , can we stop using groundwater ? " <p> Other major cities have done it before . Tokyo , a city of 38 million people , sank more than two metres in the 20th century before measures were put in place to restrict the mass extraction of groundwater . The city 's water supply was also largely shifted to surface water . <p> The changes stopped the Japanese capital from sinking into the ground . <p> But experts believe not enough is being done in Jakarta to stop the subsidence . <p> Indonesia 's President Joko Widodo has overseen heavy investment in infrastructure and flagged earlier @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ part of a US$40 billion investment in the city over the next decade . <p> The city 's governor also toughened policies to crack down on the illegal use of groundwater . But it 's unclear what impact this will have on subsidence because of the lack of reliable methods to monitor deep groundwater levels . <p> Construction of the " Great Garuda Sea Wall " began after devastating floods in 2013 sharpened the focus of policy makers on the damage subsidence could cause . The plan was to build an elaborate US$40 billion district on the water in the shape of a garuda , a mythological bird-like creature , to act as a buffer from the Java Sea . <p> But after concerns the structure would create a giant toxic bay filled by Jakarta 's heavily polluted rivers , and a corruption scandal linked to some of the islands in the project , the concept was scrapped .
