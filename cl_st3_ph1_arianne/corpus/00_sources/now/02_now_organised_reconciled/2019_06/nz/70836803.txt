<p> Whether or not future generations get to eat green-lipped mussels may depend on actions we take today , writes Dr Alison Collins <p> OPINION : Our changing climate will affect our environment , economy , and way of life -- a notion both familiar and daunting . The Ministry for the Environment and Stats NZ recently released a health check on our environment - Environment Aotearoa 2019 . The report identified the challenges we face ; from the loss of biodiversity , to the deteriorating health of our land , water and seas , through to the impacts of climate change . <p> The big headlines can make us feel simultaneously disconnected and overwhelmingly powerless . However , the reality is what each of us does , daily , creates change in the environment -- just like a pebble thrown into a still pool propagates a series of ripples . <p> An example might help make the challenge relatable . So let 's trace the path from one of our daily choices , out to the furthest ripple of change . A case many of us might connect to is the choice we make moving from A to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ have the highest rates of car ownership in the OECD . However , that family car is responsible for a quarter of New Zealand 's carbon dioxide emissions . <p> Most of us are probably aware that carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas that traps heat in the atmosphere , giving rise to a warming climate . But there is another effect , perhaps less well known . The carbon dioxide we emit dissolves in our oceans to create an acid ( carbonic acid ) . This acid , also found in fizzy drinks , is largely undetectable to us but it has a very detectable effect on creatures with certain shells . It makes the ocean acidic enough that some species find it harder to build their shells , and some shells may even dissolve . The result is that a number of marine species , including paua , mussels and oysters , are growing more slowly and breeding later . <p> Rippling out , those emissions we generate from driving to work , the supermarket , or on the school run could threaten the health of our marine @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . Over time this could impact on the viability of commercial fishing industries , and reduce opportunities for the recreational fishing we enjoy . Declines in kaimoana could limit what we can put on the table , including for special occasions , undermining our cultural practices and traditions . <p> This is just one example . Every choice and every action we make creates a ripple of change , most of which we are yet to recognise or predict . More work is needed to unpick , unravel and understand the complexity of these ripples . <p> But the complexity should n't paralyse us from acting . Indeed , the ripple effect shows us that individually and collectively we have the power to create ripples of positive change . <p> There 's been recent action . Our parliamentary leaders recently passed the first reading of the new Climate Change Response ( Zero Carbon ) Amendment Bill . And school students led the charge as they marched in protest for climate action . In fact , the kids ' climate marches taught us that a single , young voice from under @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ climate strike , Greta Thunberg - can propagate a tide of change , a call for action . Even more profound is her refrain that ' no-one is too small to make a difference ' . <p> Each of us has the power of choice . Whether we carpool , catch the train , walk , or glide along the waterfront on a bright green scooter - we have a choice . And that single , small , daily choice may just enable our grandchildren to taste that bowl of steaming green-lipped mussels .
