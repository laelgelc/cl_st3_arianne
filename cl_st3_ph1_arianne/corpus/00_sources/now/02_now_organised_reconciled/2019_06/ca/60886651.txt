<h> Lawrie McFarlane : Pollsters often miss , thanks to fickle public opinion <p> Australian Prime MInister Scott Morrison , leader of the Liberal Party , handily won re-election recently after polls suggested he would lose . Columnist Lawrie McFarlane says there is a problem with the way polls are conducted . <p> Once again , political pollsters have dropped the ball . A week ago yesterday , Australia 's right-wing coalition government won re-election , and by a relatively safe margin . <p> Yet this was supposed to be a contest the left-of-centre Labor party could not lose . Polling firms had the party well ahead until the closing day or two , and so certain did the outcome appear , some bookmakers actually paid out to backers of Labor before the vote even took place . <p> This is the third time in three years that not just pollsters , but more broadly the political commentariat , completely missed the mark . The talking heads in Britain were so sure the Brexit referendum would fail , one of its backers conceded defeat on the night of the vote , only to discover his side had won . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ president . Three days before the election the Princeton Election Consortium , a non-aligned survey group , gave Hillary Clinton a 99 per cent chance of winning . <p> Now you could say those are outliers , that more often than not polling companies do get it right . But several factors make these particular misses stand out from the pack . <p> First , all three votes were on matters of national importance . Indeed , in the case of Brexit , the future of the country for decades to come was at stake . You would expect polling companies to go the extra mile on such occasions . <p> Second , in each of these cases , there existed a certainty of mind about the outcome that turned out to be wildly incorrect . <p> Third , again in each instance , the party or issue misread by the pollsters was on the conservative side of the aisle . <p> I mention this latter point because it is sometimes used as an explanation , or at least an excuse . Hence the wrong call . <p> There may be some truth in that . But I think a more convincing explanation is that in each of these cases , pollsters ( and others ) missed an underlying sentiment among voters that did not accord with received opinion . <p> The most obvious instance came during the Australian election . The Labor party had made climate change the centrepiece of its campaign . <p> Now there is no question a majority of voters in developed countries believe global warming is both a reality and a threat . A recent Angus Reid poll in Canada had respondents placing climate change at the top of their concerns . The federal Liberals have taken that to heart and are betting the bank on the issue . <p> But here is where things get tricky . Deeper delving might seem pointless . <p> But in Australia , it turned out there was a huge difference between believing climate change is a threat , and facing the consequences of combating it . Basically , voters recoiled from the Labor party 's platform of slashing carbon emissions because of the hardships it would impose , both on the economy and on personal lifestyles . Polling firms did n't see that , because they were n't looking for it . <p> The same thing happened in the 2016 U.S. election . The Democrats had made social issues such as universal health care and repairing relations with minority groups their main priorities . <p> And if you asked Americans whether they supported these proposals , you got a loud yes . However , it turned out there were other more compelling concerns , such as job loss and shrinking blue-collar wages . <p> Here again , the pollsters had embraced , almost unconsciously , the prevailing orthodoxy .
