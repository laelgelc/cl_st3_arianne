<p> The frozen coastline of a Canadian Arctic island is eroding at up to a metre a day as a warming climate leads to longer summers , new research has found . <p> Researchers say the rate of collapse they found is six times faster than the average for the previous 65 years . <p> " Big chunks of land were breaking away and waves were eating them away , " said the study 's co-author Isla Myers-Smith , a geoscientist at the University of Edinburgh . " They were often gone by the next day . " <p> Myers-Smith was part of an international team that flew drone-mounted cameras to survey the tundra coast of Herschel Island , also known as Qikiqtaruk , off the Yukon coast in the Canadian Arctic . It is an unoccupied but historically significant island of about 116 square kilometres . <p> The researchers determined that summer storms are sweeping away coastal permafrost at a higher rate because it is being exposed for longer periods . They showed that the coast had retreated by an astounding 14.5 metres during the period , sometimes as much as a metre a day . <p> Researchers also compared the results with surveys taken between 1952 and 2011 that showed the 2017 rate of erosion was more than six times the historical average for the area . <p> The research team included university researchers , government scientists , and local park rangers from England , Germany , Netherlands , the United States and Canada , along with community members who live and work in the Arctic . <p> A Vancouver native , Myers-Smith has been travelling to Herschel Island-Qikiqtaruk to study plants since 2008 and has spent every summer there since 2013 . " <p> Herschel Island-Qikiqtaruk is uniquely suited to study because there has been a history of ecological documentation and it is located above the treeline but south of the extreme Arctic . <p> " Scientists expect to see lots of ecological change there , " said Myers-Smith . <p> The island 's Pauline Cove was at one time a significant settlement for Indigenous people and later served as a community for European and American whalers starting in the late 1800s .
