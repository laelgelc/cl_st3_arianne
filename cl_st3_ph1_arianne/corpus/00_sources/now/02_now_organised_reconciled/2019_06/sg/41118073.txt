<p> PARIS ( SPARKNEWS ) - Brexit , climate change , migrants ... many topics such as these divide Europeans , fuelling mistrust and miscomprehension among citizens . <p> In order to bridge the gap , a group of journalists from the German news outlet Zeit Online , together with media outlets from across Europe conducted an experiment called Europe Talks , a couple of weeks before the European elections . <p> The idea was to host face-to-face conversations between people from different countries who had opposing political views . <p> Philip Faigle , Special Projects Editor of Zeit Online and one of Europe Talks ' project leaders , discussed how this kind of initiative can help break down stereotypes . <h> In an age of online forums , social media and 24-hour news channels , why bring together political opponents from different countries ? <p> It all started in the newsroom , a few months before the German general elections of 2017 , which revealed a number of controversial topics that were dividing society . <p> Our idea was to spark conversations between people who had opposing views , for them to initiate a dialogue outside their echo @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ <p> We asked our online readers a series of questions on topics such as refugees , nuclear power or Russia , and whether they were willing to meet someone with opposing views . <p> Then we paired them up with an algorithm , a sort of Tinder for people with divergent political opinions . Once the matching process was complete , they met in person . <p> Some discussions lasted a half-hour ; others went on for nine hours . Overall it worked really well . <h> What did you hope to achieve with Europe Talks ? <p> Our goal was to get people from different European countries to talk to each other , like we had done in Germany , and to discover how their fellow Europeans think . <p> Our impression was that people in Germany might not know how a truck driver in France taking part in the Yellow Vests movement actually lives , or what were the reasons for someone in the UK to vote for Brexit . <p> Ahead of the European elections , we thought these conversations could help people to better understand events @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ topics such as fuel taxes to tackle climate change , national border controls , or whether rich countries should help poorer ones . <h> How did it work ? <p> People who wished to participate had to answer seven questions allowing us to match them with someone from a neighbouring country . <p> Roughly 21,000 people from 33 countries - more than those in the European Union - registered , and more than 16,000 confirmed they wanted to participate . <p> Our algorithm found a partner for almost everyone , but in the end 6,000 people actually went through with the meeting . <p> Some met online , through video conference , but some actually travelled longer distances - for instance , a Greek citizen met with someone from Bulgaria . <h> How did you manage the risks of getting people with divergent opinions together ? <p> We were aware of the risks , so we asked them questions about their backgrounds before matching them up . <p> We also recommended that everyone meet in a public place , not at home . After Europe Talks , we gathered some 500 participants in Brussels , and everyone seemed truly happy with the outcome . <h> Did you hear about anyone whose views on a topic changed ? <p> The Briq Institute and the University of Bonn conducted a study after Germany Talks showing that some people actually changed their opinions - although it was not always clear in which direction . <p> The research also showed that the talks reduced stereotypes about people from the other side of the political spectrum . <p> But what mattered most to us was that people started a dialogue . <h> Were there any challenges in scaling up from the German initiative to a cross- border experience ? <p> There was the language issue .
