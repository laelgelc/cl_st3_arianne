<p> The government proposes reducing UK greenhouse gas emissions to almost zero by 2050 , under a new plan to tackle climate change . The Victoria Derbyshire programme met a family who live self sufficiently and provide a glimpse of the kind of changes we may all need to make . <p> " The leap - handing your notice in , going off and not having a job - that 's scary . " <p> Charis Watkinson , 34 , is reflecting on her and husband Matthew 's decision two years ago to give up their jobs as vets and their lives in Essex to go completely " off-grid " . <p> The couple now live in rural Pembrokeshire with their two children - Elsa , five , and 18-month-old Billy . Their home , Beeview Farm - named after the bees they keep for honey - is so remote and hilly that they direct visitors to a field so they can be picked up in a 4x4 . <p> The family generate their own electricity from solar panels and gas from their food waste . <p> Their home - swapping gas on the grid for renewables - is @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ live if the government is to meet its net zero emissions target . <p> Britain is the first major nation to propose this target - widely praised by green groups . <p> The Watkinsons ' house is bigger than it first looks , built from old horse boxes , caravans and trailers . <p> Image caption Beeview Farm is made from a truck and camper van - among other things <p> " It 's basically junk , scrap vehicles and junk - we 've put most of it together , " 42-year-old Matthew says , before showing off his many inventions . <p> For example , the spare bedroom is an old 4x4 with a full double mattress and an enviable ocean view . They have an outdoor and indoor shower , both equipped with hot water , made from old IBC tanks , commonly found on British farms . Metres away is a Swedish-style hot tub .
