Justin Tang / THE CANADIAN PRESS <p> The UCP government is following through on its campaign promise to spend $100 million over the next four years to improve mental health and addiction services , and an additional $40 million to specifically respond to the opioid crisis . <p> This is a smart and bold investment at a time when the government is looking to restrain and even cut spending in almost every other area . <p> If correctly applied , the additional funding could have profound preventative effects , allowing professionals to catch and treat issues before they become serious problems . Similarly , while we know the study will explore impacts on surrounding communities , it 's uncertain how deeply it will look at other factors such as how effective the sites are . <p> On one hand , it is completely reasonable for a new @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ change . And communities who feel they have been hurt need to be heard . <p> At the same time , something about this process feels predatory . <p> At least some of the site leaders are nervous about the underlying motivation of the review , and it 's hard to blame them given the rhetoric from UCP circles . <p> Premier Jason Kenney , for one , has denigrated the sites as venues to help people " inject poison " and " commit slow motion suicide , " while also asserting they have had " devastating " effects for surrounding neighbourhoods . <p> Such overblown and insensitive commentary likely has n't played well beyond the UCP base , so the government has now adopted a new set of talking points . <p> Remarks from Luan , the premier 's director of communications and others this week have started to focus on the need for a " balanced " approach built around a " continuum of care , " while suggesting the NDP is hung up on the sites as the only solution to the opioid crisis -- which is @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ While more subtle , the effect of such language nonetheless undermines the importance of supervised consumption services .
