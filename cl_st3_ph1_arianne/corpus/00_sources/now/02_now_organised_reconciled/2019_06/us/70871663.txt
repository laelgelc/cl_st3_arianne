<h> EDITORIAL : Too early to make judgments about viability of medical marijuana <p> Daily Press staff <p> Jun 15 , 2019 <p> Last week , a team at the Stanford University School of Medicine revisited a 2014 study suggesting states with medical marijuana were logging fewer opioid deaths . The researchers now say there 's no connection between readily available cannabis and a drop in opioid overdoses . <p> There are no Ph.D.s or tenured scientists on the Tahlequah Daily Press payroll , but we keep up with current events , and we know widespread legalization of medical marijuana has only stepped up over the past year or so . That does n't seem like enough time to render a definitive verdict . <p> Details of the studies , when compared side by side , are interesting - and both raise questions of motive . Many people claim " climate change " is a fallacy developed by researchers to keep the grant money flowing . The same might be said for studies like this one , which was funded by the National Institutes of Health and the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs . The federal government has yet @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , so why would it be out of line to suspect bureaucrats of steering money toward a study that could validate their draconian policies ? <p> One of the professors involved in the Stanford study made this comment : " If you think opening a bunch of dispensaries is going to reduce opioid deaths , you 'll be disappointed . " But that 's not necessarily an ample cause-and-effect argument . Especially in states like Oklahoma , where voters just approved medical marijuana last year , there were n't enough prescriptions and sales during the study period to reject the correlation . While it may be true that those who prefer to abuse opioids will do so even with marijuana readily available , there 's a good chance that within a few years , patients who were taking oxycodone to relieve terrible pain , but feared addiction , will try the cannabis route and possibly have luck with it . <p> At least , it 's worth a shot - and the TDP , as a newspaper , knows that first-hand . TDP has published several stories on the local @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ in Tahlequah . TDP reporters have also interviewed a number of people with prescriptions for medical marijuana , and some have described what they consider to be miraculous transformations in the quality of their lives . Some are now pain-free and able to function better than before . Cancer patients and children afflicted with serious diseases have found significant relief through some form of cannabis . Should n't we give them the benefit of the doubt , or must we assume they are lying ? <p> It 's worth remembering that not all marijuana makes its users " high , " and there are many forms for those who do n't smoke - and most pain patients prefer another form . That 's one reason why Oklahoma voters overwhelmingly gave the nod to medical marijuana . <p> Some of the votes likely came from folks who hope to eventually be able to use the substance recreationally .
