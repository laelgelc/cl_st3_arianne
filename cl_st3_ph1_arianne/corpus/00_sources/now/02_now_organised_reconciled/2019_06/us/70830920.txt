<h> Jason Leslie : No one has a good handle on the risk of mortgage-backed securities <p> After the 2008 financial crisis the common wisdom was that prudent Canadian banks and financial regulation had saved us from the worst excesses of the U.S. and other countries , and will do so in future . But few of us are in a position to assess whether this is true . <p> Canada 's mortgage market still depends heavily on " mortgage-backed securities , " a type of financial product that caused the financial crisis to spread from the U.S. to the rest of the world . What many find shocking is that , unlike most other countries , the Canadian government directly guarantees these securities for investors . In other words , if there is another crisis , Canadian taxpayers are on the hook for investors ' losses . According to a CD Howe analysis from 2015 , that could reach $9 billion . <p> For my PhD thesis at the Peter A. Allard School of Law , I 'm asking whether this system is worth the risk . But even for those who do own homes , the " grand bargain " struck by the Canadian government with the purchasers of these securities may not measure up . The rationale is that this program provides an attractive place for international investors to put their money , ensuring a steady supply of funds for mortgage loans . Yet , as admitted by experts such as Steven Schwarcz , professor of law and business at Duke University , no one really knows how to evaluate the risks of these complex financial instruments . The federal government does charge investors a fee for its guarantees , but who knows whether those fees are high enough to cover such uncertain risks ? <p> Imagine , for comparison , an insurance company offering policies against the risks of climate change . We know enormous consequences are coming from the rise in global temperatures . But even the world 's best scientists ca n't predict exactly what will happen . We know that financial shocks will come . We do n't , and ca n't , know what effects those shocks will have . <p> There are a number of ways to address this problem . One is to get the government out of the mortgage securities business , leaving the risk in private hands . However , this still leaves the financial system vulnerable , and could lead to even greater risk-taking by private lenders . <p> Another option , recommended by Schwarcz , would be to redesign the entire financial system for greater resiliency against unpredictable shocks . <p> While this idea has merit , there are less dramatic alternatives for our mortgage system . The government could reduce the amount of securities it offers , encouraging financial institutions to seek other sources of mortgage funding . It could take measures to make rental housing more affordable and appealing to the Canadian middle class , as it was during the mid-20th century , especially in urban areas .
