<h> The complex fate of Antarctic species in the face of a changing climate <p> IMAGE : John Spicer collecting intertidal amphipods from South Cove ( Rothera Research Station , British Antarctic Survey ) looking west to Ryder Bay on the Western Antarctic Peninsula . view more <p> Credit : Simon Morley <p> Oxygen concentrations in both the open ocean and coastal waters have declined by 2-5% since at least the middle of the 20th century . <p> This is one of the most important changes occurring in an ocean becoming increasingly modified by human activities , with raised water temperatures , carbon dioxide content and nutrient inputs . <p> Through this , humans are altering the abundances and distributions of marine species but the decline in oxygen could pose a new set of threats to marine life . <p> Writing in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B , scientists present support for the theory that marine invertebrates with larger body size are generally more sensitive to reductions in oxygen than smaller animals , and so will be more sensitive to future global climate change . <p> It is widely believed that the occurrence of gigantic species in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ is more oxygen dissolved in ice cold water than in the warmer waters of temperate and tropic regions . <p> So as our ocean warms and oxygen decreases , it has been suggested that such oxygen limitation will have a greater effect on larger than smaller marine invertebrates and fish . <p> The study was conducted by John Spicer , Professor of Marine Zoology at the University of Plymouth , and Dr Simon Morley , an Ecophysiologist with the British Antarctic Survey ( BAS ) . <p> They investigated how a number of different sized amphipod species - found in abundance in Antarctic waters and relatives of the sandhoppers on temperate beaches ) - performed when the oxygen in the water they were in was reduced . <p> Overall , there was a reduction in performance with body size supporting the theory that larger species may well be more vulnerable because of oxygen limitation . <p> However , the picture is a little more complex than this with evolutionary innovation - such as the presence of oxygen binding pigments in their body fluids to enhance oxygen transport , and novel @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ species - to some extent offsetting any respiratory disadvantages of large body size . <p> Professor Spicer , who has spent more than 30 years examining the effect of climate change on marine organisms , said : " Over the last 50 years , the oxygen in our oceans has decreased by around 2-5% and this is already having an effect on species ' ability to function . Unless they adapt , many larger marine invertebrates will either shrink in size of face extinction , which would have a profoundly negative impact on the ecosystems of which they are a part . This is obviously a major cause for concern . <p> " Our research also shows that some species have evolved mechanisms to compensate for reductions in oxygen , and so it is not always as simple as drawing a link between size and future survival . But it would be foolhardy to pin our hopes on such ' evolutionary rescue ' . Many large species will almost certainly be the first casualties of our warming , oxygen-poor ocean .
