<h> Leaving microbes out of climate change conversation has major consequences , experts warn <p> Comments Off on Leaving microbes out of climate change conversation has major consequences , experts warn <p> An international group of leading microbiologists , including UvA professor Jef Huisman , have issued a warning that climate change will have a major impact on microorganisms with cascading effects on ecosystems , agriculture and human health . In a consensus statement published in Nature Reviews Microbiology , they point out that microbes are a major missing link in climate change studies and are calling for the world to stop ignoring this ' unseen majority ' in Earth 's biodiversity when developing policies to halt global warming . <p> Climate change studies in a native Australian forest . Many of the effects of climate change on plants , animals and soils are mediated by microorganisms . ' They support the existence of all higher lifeforms and are critically important in regulating climate change . However , they are rarely the focus of climate change studies and not considered in policy development . ' <h> Unseen majority <p> Microbes are the ' unseen majority ' of lifeforms on earth , playing critical functions in animal and human health , agriculture , the global food web and industry . For example , the Census of Marine Life estimates that 90% of the ocean 's total biomass is microbial . In our oceans , marine lifeforms called phytoplankton take light energy from the sun and remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere as much as plants . The tiny phytoplankton form the beginning of the ocean food web , feeding krill populations that then feed fish , sea birds and large mammals such as whales . <p> Jef Huisman , professor of Aquatic Microbial Ecology at the Institute for Biodiversity and Ecosystem Dynamics ( IBED ) of the University of Amsterdam ( UvA ) , contributed to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ ' With continued global warming , warm waters will spread like a blanket across large parts of the ocean surface . This will diminish exchange with deeper water layers , which is expected to suppress phytoplankton growth , resulting in less carbon dioxide uptake and a lower biological productivity of the oceans , ' Prof. Huisman explains . <p> However , many cyanobacteria ( a.k.a. blue-green algae ) thrive at higher temperatures . They can be toxic , and there are several indications that global warming is increasing the frequency , intensity and duration of cyanobacterial blooms in lakes and reservoirs across the globe . <p> Cyanobacteria in Oosterduinse Meer ( The Netherlands ) . The expectation is that as a result of global warming , water quality problems caused by these potentially toxic ' blue-green algae ' will increase worldwide . Image : Giovanni Sandrini . <h> Greenhouse gases <p> Beyond the ocean , microbes are also critical to terrestrial environments , agriculture and diseases . And lastly , climate change worsens the impact of pathogenic microbes on plants and animals ( including humans ) -- that 's because climate change is stressing native life , making it easier for pathogens to cause disease . ' <h> Raising awareness <p> With their statement , the scientists hope to raise public awareness on the impacts of climate change on microorganisms as well as the influence of microbially-driven processes on the global climate . They call on institutions , researchers and governments to advance innovative technologies and improve education on microbes and climate change in classrooms . The statement emphasises the need to investigate microbial responses to climate change during the development of policy and management decisions . Additionally , climate change studies should pay more attention to the key role of microorganisms in the coupling between biological processes , global biogeochemical cycles and climate change . <p> ' This goes to the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ sufficiently taken into consideration , it means models can not be generated properly and predictions could be inaccurate , ' says Prof. Cavicchioli .
