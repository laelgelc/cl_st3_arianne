Our wet sacks , shovels and beaters seemed futile but a change of wind and patch of native bush enabled us to control the fire after four , physically demanding hours . <p> The locals thanked God for our let off and I smiled gratefully at the green vegetation barrier and dying easterly wind that intervened . <p> DAVID WALKER/FAIRFAX NZ <p> Dry vegetation surrounds the top of Kennedys Bush Rd , showing how close the fires came to residences . <p> Fire is an ever present danger in dry east coast regions of New Zealand but its impact has been exaggerated by recent changes in land use on the Port Hills . <p> Decisions to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ there is a ready and growing fuel source that makes ongoing summer fires inevitable . <p> DAVID WALKER/FAIRFAX NZ <p> Smoldering pines in the aftermath of the Port Hills fires . <p> The tall oat grass and clumps of cocksfoot amongst the tussocks , that combine to give the hills their characteristic tawny hue , are symptoms of lax grazing and poor pasture management . On commercial farms throughout the east coast , sheep and cattle are right now in mobs rotating around paddocks to clean up this excess feed and reduce the fire risk after a wet spring . <p> For many reasons this is no longer happening on the Port Hills , or for that matter on some high country areas recently retired under tenure review . <p> The few stock left on the Port Hills can now be selective about what they eat . They eat clovers and any green material and leave the dry , carbon dominant , grasses behind allowing them to grow over a metre tall in spring . These grasses go to seed in late spring and strong drying nor'west winds sap @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ fuel . <p> When luck pervades , and there is no spark to ignite the fuel , the cycle is left to repeat the following year . The old tillers of grasses die but low winter rainfall means that dead material does not decompose . Instead it builds up over several years , or even decades , as each spring adds tonnes of dead material per hectare , accumulating and ready to burn . <p> Ecologically , the species that overtop this thickened grass mat are highly flammable gorse and broom . They fix their own nitrogen and are therefore earlier colonisers of the nutrient poor ground that develops the longer stock are out of a pastoral area . <p> In theory these woody shrubs protect seedlings of native trees to nurse them back into the landscape and restore the bushland that once clothed the Peninsula . In practice , this progression is most likely to be successful at higher altitudes and on east and south facing slopes of the hills , where more moisture is squeezed from the clouds into creeks and gullies . <p> On the dry @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the practice is a recipe for disaster . This week they have provided a spectacular and devastating public display of what happens when stock numbers are too low and nature is left largely unchecked to clean up the vegetation . <p> This situation is our example of a world-wide conflict that has developed between graziers and those people who prefer to exclude stock from grazed areas to allow them to regenerate natural vegetation . In the United States livestock have been removed from wilderness areas and grasses and woody weeds are ready to ignite through lightening or human intervention . The manpower required to control the resulting wildfires employ college students and professional fighters in their thousands each summer . The same fate awaits dry areas of New Zealand wherever stock are excluded or reduced significantly in number . It is not a matter of if , just when , these areas will burn . <p> The risk and frequency of fire on the east coast of New Zealand are also predicted to increase with climate change , so fires from Central Otago to Hawkes Bay can be expected @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ lessons from the Wither Hills in 2000 or Port Hills in 2017 we are in danger of repeating these disastrous events . <p> Once the fences are rebuilt and the stock water systems reconnected , nature will cloak the blackened soil with greenery . We can help that process and at the same time reduce the risk of a recurrence of fire in years to come . <p> Legumes like sub clover flown onto these areas can provide nitrogen to the grasses to ensure they remain palatable and will be grazed as they mature . More fencing allows greater control of where stock graze , so they can be targeted to encourage the regeneration of other species , while reducing the build up of dead material . Small life-style and large commercial farms alike have a responsibility to control grasses in paddocks and fencelines in summer , just as is done in vacant section in town . <p> Sheep and cattle have evolved a rumen specifically designed to cope with low quality herbage on prairies and grasslands of the world to harvest grass . Green fire breaks of lucerne can be created as done in areas of Marlborough , to provide a buffer in valley floors , while highly flammable exotic pine trees are confined to Bill Sutton 's paintings . <p> If we do n't reduce the fire risk with stock we may all one day find ourselves , on a hillside with a shovel and wet sacking praying for a favourable wind change or oasis of green . <p> Dr Derrick Moot is the Professor of Plant Science at Lincoln University .
