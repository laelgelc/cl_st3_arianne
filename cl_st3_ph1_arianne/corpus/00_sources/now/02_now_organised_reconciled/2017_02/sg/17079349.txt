That oxygen might be able to kill pathogens , Dr Lamb realised . The plants also host fungi , which are known to produce bacteria-killing compounds . Dr Lamb and her colleagues decided to study sites around four islands in Indonesia with and without nearby seagrass . Dr Robert Orth , a seagrass expert at the Virginia Institute of Marine Science who was not involved in the new report , predicted it would lead to similar research on seagrass meadows in other parts of the world . <p> " It 's going to make people think , " he said . <p> The growing recognition of this plant 's importance comes even as it is disappearing . <p> Nearly one-third of the world 's seagrass meadows have died since the 19th century . Eroded dirt washes into the Chesapeake , making the water cloudy . <p> Seagrass gets so little sunlight that the resulting dimming can be deadly . Seagrass is also being pummelled by climate change . Warmer summer temperatures in Chesapeake Bay cause the plants to lose much of their oxygen through their leaves . <p> With less oxygen to pump into their roots , they are poisoned by toxic sediments . <p> The cloudiness of the water makes the heat even worse . With less sunlight , the plants produce even less protective oxygen to begin with . " We have a double whammy , " said Dr Orth . <p> As the underwater meadows disappear , economic value disappears with them . The Chesapeake Bay meadows are a nursery for blue crabs , for example .
