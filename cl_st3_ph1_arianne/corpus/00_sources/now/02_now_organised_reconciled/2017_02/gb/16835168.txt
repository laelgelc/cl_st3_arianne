<h> This one simple trick gets climate negotiators to update their understanding <p> Most of the information we receive is n't really new . Instead , it 's related to things we already know , which means that we have to update our beliefs based on the new information . It may not surprise you that not everyone is great about updating their beliefs . And a new study in Nature Climate Change reports that there 's a rather important group that seems to be bad at this process : climate negotiators . <p> There is some good news . While uncertainty about climate change is generally considered a challenge for setting policy , emphasizing the uncertainties helps negotiators bring their beliefs up to date with the current information . <p> Uncertainty means different things in different contexts . We may not be sure whether the planet will heat up by 2.7 degrees Celsius or 3.5 degrees Celsius by 2100 , but it 's pretty likely to be around that range . To explore the effects of uncertainty , Valentina Bosetti , an economist at Bocconi University in Italy , led a group of researchers in conducting a field experiment at COP21 negotiations in Paris in 2015 . Their test subjects included 217 climate change negotiators and policymakers from more than 100 countries . <p> All the participants were first asked about their current beliefs about how climate change would progress by the year 2100 , assuming global emissions stayed roughly the same . They were given a chart that showed four different possible outcomes : an increase of less than 2 degrees Celsius ; of 2-3 degrees Celsius , of 3-4 degrees Celsius , and more than 4 degrees Celsius . They had to show how probable they thought each outcome was by circling a percentage on an annotated scale . For instance , they might mark an increase of 3-4 degrees Celsius as 70 percent probable , which puts it @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ an increase of less than 2 degrees Celsius as 10 percent probable , which puts it in the range marked " very unlikely . " <p> The participants were then shown data from the 5th Assessment Report of the IPCC ( Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change ) , which included data from 30 different models that were used to project the temperature increase that would occur with consistent emissions . Each of those models came up with a slightly different answer , depending on what was accounted for in the model , but most of them clustered around 3 degrees Celsius , which was the mean of all the estimates . Most of the estimates -- 90 percent of them -- fell between 2 degrees Celsius and 4 degrees Celsius . Only a few outliers fell at less than 2 degrees Celsius or more than 4 degrees Celsius . <p> Here 's where the study got clever . The 217 participants were divided into three groups , and each of them got to see the information from these models presented in a slightly different way . A third of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of all the model outcomes , with a horizontal line at 3 degrees Celsius . The chart also showed that 90 percent of the estimates fell between 2 degrees Celsius and 4 degrees Celsius . <p> The next third saw a slightly more complicated chart . It looked basically the same , but this setup also showed dots for the outliers that fell below 2 degrees Celsius or above 4 degrees Celsius . And the final third saw that same chart , but this time there was a dot for every single model , which meant that it was possible to see how all the model estimates fell in relation to one another . <p> Next , all the participants were given the first question again to see if their answers had changed . On the whole , they were pretty conservative about updating their beliefs based on the information they had just seen . But they did a much better job of doing so when they had seen the chart with all the information about uncertainty . <p> There are plenty of reasons why this could be the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ information to really change how the participants thought about probability ; perhaps seeing the distribution of the model estimates really helped to cement how certain we are about certain ranges . It 's also possible that all the additional information forced them to pay more attention to what they were looking at . <p> The curious thing , though , is that the same effect did n't appear in a control group . <p> The control group was carefully chosen to match some characteristics , but not others . The researchers went to Erasmus University Rotterdam , where a group of MBA students was holding a two-day role play of a climate change negotiation and had been preparing for it for months . These students were far more knowledgeable about climate change than the average person , but they were n't driven by actual national agendas or professional concerns . It might be because of the difference in confidence , or it might be because professional policymakers have to represent their national interests , which might be hitched to a particular prediction . <p> There are a lot of questions opened up by this result , with the most obvious one being why showing all the uncertainty worked better on professionals but not students . It would also be worth testing whether the same effect appears in other areas of science and policy . But one of the most important things to come out of this research is the suggestion that different communication techniques will work on different groups of people . The authors write : " These results highlight the importance of testing visualization tools directly on the population of interest . "
