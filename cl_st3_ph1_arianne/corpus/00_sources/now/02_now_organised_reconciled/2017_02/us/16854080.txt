<h> An Antarctic ice core details changes in the Earth 's temperature , rainfall , and wind over hundreds of thousands of years <p> In 2005 , on an Antarctic ridge that 's considered the coldest place in the world , a team of researchers plunged a drill through miles of ice . Once complete , the hole looked like a nightmarishly deep toilet -- complete with a little blue lid that plonked down after the metal drill dropped out of sight . <p> The mission , led by Japan 's National Institute of Polar Research , was to extract nearly 800,000 years of Earth 's climate history , left as an imprint in ice beneath the surface . The two-mile-long ice tube they extracted is n't just a window into the past ; it may also give scientists a glimpse of the future . Data suggests that when the arctic would cool , the Antarctic would warm . The research team believes that the inverse relationship between the two poles is due to an ocean current that keeps Europe and North America warm during the winter . <p> Called the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation or AMOC , this current shuttles warm water from south to north , keeping temperatures in the North Atlantic from plummeting during the winter . It influences rainfall and wind patterns across the planet , and circulates nutrients in ocean .
