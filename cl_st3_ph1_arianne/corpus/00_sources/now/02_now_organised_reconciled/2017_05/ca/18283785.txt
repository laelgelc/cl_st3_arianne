<p> Former prime minister Kim Campbell once now famously remarked , " An election is no time to discuss serious issues . " <p> The gaffe was one of many that led to her historic defeat in 1993 , but despite her blunt awkwardness , it 's tempting to acknowledge she may just have a point . <p> Typically only five weeks long , Canadian election campaigns are a frenzied , caffeine-fuelled sprint during which any number of critical policy decisions with sweeping and lasting consequences are made by acutely addled campaign strategists and vote-hungry politicians . <p> In this zero-sum environment , these folks would happily exchange a future employment crisis , trade dispute , or budget boondoggle for a few more votes from a target block -- or slightly fewer votes for the other guys . <p> Which brings us to British Columbia . On Tuesday , B.C. Liberal Leader and Premier Christy Clark made one of these decisions . <p> With the NDP breathing down her neck and the election only days away , Clark ratcheted up her climate change agenda by promising to slap a $70 @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ ports . <p> She green-wrapped the levy to burnish her environmental bonafides and make moderate eco-voters think twice about defecting to the surging Green Party , which is threatening to eat into Liberal support on Vancouver Island . <p> She also suggested the levy was a counterpunch to the United States after the Trump administration imposed punitive softwood lumber import tariffs that will hit B.C. particularly hard . Clark 's levy would render U.S. coal exports uneconomical as Trump attempts to revive the coal industry . <p> If it all works out , Clark keeps just enough greenies in her camp to stave off the NDP while picking up populist points by hitting back on softwood lumber . Not a bad day 's work on the campaign trail . <p> But what are some of the practical , non-political ramifications of Clark 's levy ? <p> Despite the retaliatory buzz around Clark 's move , the levy would be applied to exports evenly regardless of point of origin .
