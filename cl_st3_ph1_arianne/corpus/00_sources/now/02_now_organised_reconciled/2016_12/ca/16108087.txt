<p> The worst oil shock in Canadian oilpatch history is letting up . Rig jobs are re-appearing , investment is rising and stock prices are responding . <p> The two-year nightmare -- concentrated in Alberta , Saskatchewan and Newfoundland -- started when Saudi Arabia unleashed another price war to drive competitors out of business . Pipeline bottlenecks , uncertainty about Canada 's environmental policies , and the sector 's large size made it tough to absorb the shock this time around . <p> But Canada 's oil and gas companies did n't help themselves . Bad decisions came home to roost , and others will stand in the way of a recovery . <p> The wise -- and their board of directors -- will reflect on lessons learned to avoid repeating the same mistakes . <p> First , about those mass firings . The industry 's human resources practices are crying out for serious reform . It 's estimated some 100,000 people were ( metaphorically ) executed , their careers left in shambles . Many of them were professionals working in Calgary 's head offices . <p> Did it @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Natural Resources Ltd. weathered the downturn by keeping their teams and reducing salaries across the board . But the majority switched almost overnight from aggressive hiring -- remember those lavish paycheques , bonuses and golden Fridays ? -- to pushing thousands of people out the door . <p> Some of those responsible for the firings even ended up with pay increases . The practice validated perceptions that the oilpatch is old , vicious and dirty , and the damage will stick . <p> " The downturn and resulting layoffs across the industry threaten to damage the industry 's brand as a career destination , " John England , Deloitte vice-chairman and U.S. Energy &amp; Resources leader , wrote in a recent report on his outlook for 2017 . <p> " As a large number of senior employees head toward retirement , companies should find ways to transfer this wealth of knowledge to the next generation of employees . It 's the predominant culture in oil towers , especially during boom times , and it 's done a lot of damage . It 's meant the oil and gas sector is over-confident in its view of the world , while insufficiently appreciating that of others . Many do n't even want to know or engage with others . The result has been resentment and blocked plans . <p> Some leaders have learned their lessons and are devoting more of their time listening to those outside the bubble . One of them is Al Monaco , president and CEO of Enbridge Inc. , the company that proposed and was denied a permit for Northern Gateway pipeline . <p> He said in a recent speech : " Today , you ca n't lead the business from your office -- you need to engage on a personal level .
