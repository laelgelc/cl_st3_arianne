<h> Conservationist Jane Goodall brings message of hope to University of Minnesota <p> Michael NeugebauerAfter making groundbreaking discoveries about the ways in which chimpanzees interact with one another and the world around them , Dr. Jane Goodall has taken her message of respect for chimpanzees and the environment worldwide . <p> She held no notes . She used few slides . But once Jane Goodall stepped onstage at Northrop auditorium Friday night , it was clear she carried a message summoned easily from memory . <p> " Hoo hoo , hohohoo hoho hooh ! Hoooh ! " the renowned primatologist called , mimicking the chimpanzee welcome she heard so often during her time in Africa . " This is me . This is Jane . " <p> So it was . As soon as she came into view , the audience in the packed auditorium at the University of Minnesota greeted her with a standing ovation . <p> Before a sold-out crowd , the famed conservationist shared why people should believe in the future even in the face of mounting threats to the planet . <p> " There is hope , " said Goodall , 83 . " Just think about the consequences of the different choices you make each day . " <p> Goodall 's pioneering study of chimpanzees revolutionized the world 's view of the relationship between humans and primates . She is perhaps best known for her 1960 discovery that chimpanzees make and use tools . <p> " What I was learning so vividly was how like us they are , " Goodall said . <p> She recalled her childhood in England and lifelong passion to understand the Earth 's creatures . Tarzan , she told the giggling crowd , " married the wrong Jane . " <p> " I was born loving animals , " Goodall said in her quiet British accent . <p> ' An inspiration to us all ' <p> At 26 , she ventured into Gombe National Park in Tanzania , soon becoming a neighbor with chimps rather than a far-off observer . She upended scientific norms and gave them names . It 's because of Goodall that people across the globe learned their names , too . <p> Figures like Goodall are the reason that Cailin McMahon , 19 , a sophomore studying genetics and cell biology at the U , first became interested in conservation . <p> " She 's just a living legend , " she said . <p> After decades studying chimpanzees in the wild , Goodall became a crusader for conservation , working to raise awareness about animal welfare issues . She now spends 300 days a year traveling to share her story and stress how all living creatures are connected . <p> Friday was n't Goodall 's first visit to the U. The international icon has a long friendship with the university , said Michael Wilson , an associate professor of anthropology who studies chimpanzees . <p> A former faculty member , Anne Pusey , and her team digitized Goodall 's data from Gombe , and students from the U still travel to the Tanzanian national park for research , Wilson said . <p> " Jane is an inspiration to us all , " Wilson said . " She is a pioneer . " <p> Goodall came to Minnesota with the same warning and plea she carries everywhere about the risks that humans pose to the planet . <p> She spoke of the " reckless burning of fossil fuels , " climate change and the destruction of the rain forest . <p> But as she began to detail youth conservation programs , the word " hope " sprang to Goodall 's lips like a quiet benediction . <p> She said young people , the resiliency of nature , social media and the power of the human brain and spirit give her optimism for the future . <p> Ben Mai , a 19-year-old biochemistry major , listened closely as Goodall shared her story and insights . <p> " I really admire how she kind of puts a spin on the positive side , " Mai said . " It encourages us to do better . "
