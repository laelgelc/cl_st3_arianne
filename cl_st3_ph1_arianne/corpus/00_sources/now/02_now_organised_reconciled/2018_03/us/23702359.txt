<h> The country 's growing almond habit means that there are lots of shells and hulls to put to good use . These government scientists are working hard to find the best options . <p> Inside a USDA research building near Berkeley , California , research leader Bill Orts holds up a mottled-looking black plate . It 's " really ugly , " he says . But the rough prototype is the first of its kind , made with recycled plastic and almond shells that were transformed into a charcoal-like black powder in an on-site lab . By adding the powder to bio-based or recycled plastics , the materials get stronger . For the almond industry , it 's one possible way to make the best use of everything grown in an almond orchard . <p> advertisement <p> On almond trees , each tiny almond grows inside a shell and a larger , apricot-like hull .
