<h> Maple producers question effects of climate change <p> The consensus among those attending the fifth annual Climate and Pancake Breakfast at Plymouth State University earlier this week was that changes in the climate are threatening the maple syrup industry . Dave Broer of Balsam Woods Farm doubts that conclusion . " We need only look at places south of New Hampshire that currently have a climate that is 4 degrees Celsius warmer to see what is happening with maple production , " he said . " Not only will maple syrup still be produced but it will still be produced at the same levels as they are now . " Broer 's farm is in Connecticut , where the maple sugar season begins in early February because of the warmer temperatures . " My climate is 6-8 degrees Fahrenheit warmer than New Hampshire 's , " he said . " I have no problem producing syrup and I have many neighbors that produce syrup , many of us commercially . I know that there are producers in states as far south as Tennessee and Virginia , so I do n't think that maple syrup is going to disappear any time soon , as many speakers try to be hinting . " He is not the only one who questions those opinions . " I think climate change is hocus pocus , " declared Brad Streeter of Young Maple Ridge Sugarhouse in Sandwich . " It is happening , probably . Who knows ? But there are so many other factors that affect maple production . We wo n't know the effects for another 20 years . " Streeter , who has been producing maple syrup for 20 years , said he started tapping a little earlier this year , but he has started boiling on the same date for the last four years . He hopes to get in another week of boiling before the season ends . " This will be our best year yet , " he said.Last year , his operation produced 720 gallons of maple syrup , and he is predicting 900 gallons this year , even though he has fewer taps out there . " The technology of getting sap out of the woods makes it more manageable that it used to be , " Streeter said.Tim Runnals of Winnipesaukee Maple Syrup in Wolfeboro produces 15-20 gallons of maple syrup a year and said he is concerned because the sugar content the sap has dropped to 1 percent.Runnals has been boiling for more than 15 years , and said he has seen a pretty steady start time , in mid to late February . " We do n't usually get a freeze like we did this year , " he commented . " Mother Nature 's hard to predict . Every year is different , but it all averages out . " Dana Pendergast of Windsong Farm in Gilford is a " very small " producer who has been tapping trees only five years , so he does not have much of a historical perspective . Still , he said , the winters have been " weird . " " It 's variations in the weather , and whether it 's a change in climate , I do n't have a solid answer . " <p> Other factorsBroer believes the earlier maple seasons have more to do with the technology . " In the old days , producers used a spout or spile that was 7/16th in diameter , and were reused every year , " he said . " This allows bacteria that cause the tap hole to start the healing-over process to occur after about 4-6 weeks . Because of this limitation , producers had to be careful when they would tap because if they tapped too early , their trees would heal over and the sap would not flow . So they would tap when the weather was typically good . " Today we use a much smaller 5/16th spout that is replaced every year . Most producers also use vacuum systems and the spout has a check valve that prevents sap from going back into the tree . ... This allows producers to tap earlier and capture early runs without the fear that their tap holes would heal up . " Streeter said the technology allows producers to tap earlier , but they do n't necessarily get syrup earlier.Broer agreed , saying the sap flows when the temperatures are ideal . However , it allows the larger producers to get a head start by having their taps in earlier . " Even if you have a few warm days , you will get some sap , " he said.Large producers like Bascom Maple Farms in Ackworth have tens of thousands of taps , and start tapping in late December and early January . " They do this because of the logistics of tapping so many trees , " Broer said . " They can do this because they do n't have to fear the sap not flowing at some point or the tap hole drying out . " Broer is more worried about other pressures on the industry . " I know that Vermont is starting to research the impact large producers have on forest health because they tend to cut down all non-maple trees and , when you have large tracts like that , there is a question as to the impact on the forest health . There is also a threat from the Asian Long-Horned Beetle which was brought here through wood crates . " Streeter said the tent caterpillar has defoliated a lot of trees in northern New Hampshire , and knows that invasive insects are becoming a concern . " Then there is regulation , " Broer said . " The FDA and USDA considers maple syrup to be a low-risk product and so to date there has been limited regulations regarding its production , but larger producers are not ones to balk at regulations that will not really impact them . These regulations require things like commercial kitchens to produce syrup for public sale and so that would push smaller producers out of business because they do n't have the time , space or money to meet the regulation . Thankfully , they are mostly not in place , but it 's a threat to smaller producers that make up most maple syrup production . "
