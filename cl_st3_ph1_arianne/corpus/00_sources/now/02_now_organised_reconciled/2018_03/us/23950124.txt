<p> Jackson Trappett/Special to The Daily Sentinel The Brown-capped Rosy-finch , a small bird that lives year around in Colorado 's high country , is one of several birds threatened by the Trump administration 's re-interpretation of the Migratory Bird Treaty Act . <h> Bird lovers must act to preserve species threatened under new interpretation of Migratory Bird Treaty Act <p> Jackson Trappett/Special to The Daily Sentinel The Brown-capped Rosy-finch , a small bird that lives year around in Colorado 's high country , is one of several birds threatened by the Trump administration 's re-interpretation of the Migratory Bird Treaty Act . <p> It was Dec. 22 , 2017 . I had a warm feeling , having just read " Why Birds Matter ! " in the January 2018 issue of National Geographic . The article celebrated the 100-year anniversary of the Migratory Bird Treaty Act ( MBTA ) . Then I saw a newsfeed reporting the Trump Administration had re-interpreted the MBTA such that only activities intended to kill migratory birds would be addressed . Inadvertent kills from industrial activities could not be prosecuted . An analogy would be to no longer make reckless driving a crime because the driver did n't intend to endanger anyone . <p> For more than 50 years the MBTA has been the only tool for reducing bird deaths from industrial activities . Now toxic waste ponds no longer must be covered . Wind energy sites do not have to worry about bird migration patterns . Thanks to the law , Exxon paid $125 million in fines for the deaths of more than 36,000 birds caused by the Exxon Valdez oil spill in 1989 , and BP paid $100 million for the deaths of possibly 100,000 birds resulting from the Deepwater Horizon spill in 2010 . No longer ! From now on , only intentional killings , such as poaching , are covered . Industry kills vastly more migratory birds than poachers do -- as many as 1.1 billion a year , according to Brad Bortner , former chief of migratory bird management at the Fish and Wildlife Service . <p> Besides industrial kills , habitat loss from development and climate change are also devastating bird populations . For example , do you enjoy a hummingbird feeder ? The most common hummingbird in our mountains , the Broad-tailed , has declined 1.5 percent a year for about 50 years . If you lived here in the 1970s , there are now half as many . <p> In contrast , White-winged Doves , very common along the U.S. southern border , were very rare here in the 1970s . In 2004 , Grand Valley Audubon Society published a book that documented only a dozen or so sightings of this species in Western Colorado . Just over 10 years later , most likely a consequence of warming climate , White-winged Doves live here year around . I see them nearly every day . <p> Unfortunately , most migratory bird species are not as adaptable as White-winged Doves and are undergoing severe declines . Do you enjoy the flash of orange from an oriole dancing across your yard in the springtime ? Our Bullock 's orioles are widespread and common , but populations declined by 29 percent between 1966 and 2014 . <p> A once common mountain migrant , the Olive-sided Flycatcher has suffered much more , declining by over 3.3 percent per year between 1966 and 2014 , resulting in a cumulative decline of 81 percent -- as much of a decline as the Greater Sage Grouse . Pinyon Jays , whose squawking was described by the great conservationist Aldo Leopold as the characteristic sound of a pinyon-juniper habitat , are down a drastic 85 percent . <p> Do you ever notice birdsong when you fish mountain streams in the summer ? There are fewer than there used to be : Yellow warblers down 25 percent , Black-throated Gray warblers down 52 percent , MacGillivray 's Warblers down 35 percent , etc . <p> Have you climbed a Fourteener and had a flock of finches flutter off ? The Brown-capped Rosy-finch -- a bird almost confined to Colorado , numbers only 45,000 with such limited habitat requirements that experts have put it on the " red list , " meaning action is required or it will become extinct . <p> I suspect you are tired of statistics . Look at it this way . If you had a sum of money that was n't going to grow , but you could live on 2 percent per year ( 2 percent being the mid-range of the rate of decline of most migratory species ) when would you worry about running out of money ? When one-quarter is gone ? How about one-half ? We 've passed those thresholds already . Now is the time for action -- if it is n't already too late . <p> Indeed , a recent study , published in the Audubon magazine in 2014 described as " the best possible future , not the worst possible future , " shows , 314 North American bird species struggling to have sufficient habitat by the year 2080 . As one of the researchers said : " That was just a punch in the gut ... it just takes your breath away . " <p> I call on elected officials , regulators , developers to stop pretending that we do n't need the MBTA . Stop pretending that we can have the level of development and fossil fuel production you desire while maintaining most of our wildlife . If you do n't think you are pretending , review the data objectively so you can stop the wishful thinking . <p> Being honest may mean acknowledging a belief that fossil fuel development is so important that the loss of a large percentage of species is acceptable . That position can be debated honestly with my contrary view that " balancing development " and the environment can only mean adhering both to the previous interpretation of the MBTA and ensuring that when an acre is newly-developed , an acre elsewhere must be preserved or restored . ( For example , if you are going to develop a well pad , then you must clean up an orphan one somewhere else -- see Feb. 9 , 2017 , The Daily Sentinel article ) . <p> I call on the rest of us to review our personal priorities . If we care about wildlife , and have the means , we must expect and even lobby to pay more for energy because we insist on saving wildlife . We must accept more restriction ( no new roads , limits on access in protected areas ) on our outdoor activities . We must advocate for wildlife . We must make personal choices such as adding solar electricity and solar hot water and electric/hybrid cars to our own lifestyles . None of us can pretend we are not losing our wildlife . We can only condone it or act to fight it . <p> ? <p> Nic Korte is a retired geochemist who has been watching local birds for more than 40 years . He is the Conservation Chairman for Grand Valley Audubon Society as well as a past president .
