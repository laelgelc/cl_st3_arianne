<p> In a laboratory in Copenhagen , scientists believe they are on the verge of a breakthrough that could transform computing . <p> A team combining Microsoft researchers and Niels Bohr Institute academics is confident that it has found the key to creating a quantum computer . <p> If they are right , then Microsoft will leap to the front of a race that has a tremendous prize - the power to solve problems that are beyond conventional computers . <p> In the lab are a series of white cylinders , which are fridges , cooled almost to absolute zero as part of the process of creating a qubit , the building block of a quantum computer . <p> " This is colder than deep space , it may be the coldest place in the universe , " Prof Charlie Marcus tells me . <p> The team he leads is working in collaboration with other labs in the Netherlands , Australia and the United States in Microsoft 's quantum research programme . <p> Right now , they are behind in the race - the likes of Google , IBM and a Silicon Valley start-up called Rigetti have already shown they can build systems with as many as 50 qubits . Microsoft has yet to demonstrate - in public at least - that it can build one . <p> But these scientists are going down a different route from their rivals , trying to create qubits using a subatomic particle , whose existence was first suggested back in the 1930s by an Italian physicist Ettore Majorana . <p> This week scientists from Microsoft 's laboratory in Delft published a paper in the journal Nature outlining the progress they had made in isolating the Majorana particle . <p> Their belief is that this will lead to a much more stable qubit than the methods their rivals are using , which are highly prone to errors . That should mean scaling up to a fully operational quantum computer will be far easier . <p> At the Copenhagen lab they showed me through a powerful microscope the tiny wire where they have created these Majorana particles . Later over dinner , Prof Charlie Marcus tried , not altogether successfully , to demonstrate to someone whose last physics exam was more than 40 years ago what was unique about this approach with three pieces of bread and some cutlery . <p> " What 's really astounding with this activity compared with what everybody else is doing is that we have to invent a particle that 's never existed before and then use it for computing , " he explains . <p> " It 's a profoundly more exotic challenge than what 's going on with other approaches to quantum computing . " <p> Other scientists taking those other approaches are looking on with great interest and a little scepticism . <p> " It 's one of those things that on paper look incredibly exciting but physics has a habit of throwing up spanners in the works , " says University College London 's Prof John Morton , whose research involves using good old fashioned silicon to build qubits . <p> " Until we see the demonstration we do n't know how well these Majorana qubits developed by Microsoft will really behave . " <p> He says this is a big year for the field , with the strong likelihood that Google or IBM will demonstrate what is known as quantum supremacy , where a problem that is beyond a conventional supercomputer is solved using quantum methods . <p> But Microsoft seems confident that its years of research will soon pay off . <p> She is already out selling the company 's customers a vision of a near future where quantum computers will help battle climate change , create new superconducting materials and super-charge machine learning . <p> " What it allows us to do is solve problems that with all of our supercomputers running in parallel would take the lifetime of the universe to solve in seconds , hours or days . " <p> So , the heat is on for the research team . Prof Charlie Marcus , who spent most of his career at Harvard before being recruited to run the Copenhagen lab , says his life has been about creating knowledge , not building products . <p> " My job is to find out what works and hand it off to the engineers and computer scientists who will turn it into a technology . " <p> Heading up the whole programme is Todd Holmdahl , the Microsoft executive previously in charge of the Hololens mixed reality headset and the Xbox games console - a measure of how serious the company is about making some quantum hardware pretty soon . <p> I pressed Prof Marcus on whether his team was going to hit that ambitious five year target set by his employer .
