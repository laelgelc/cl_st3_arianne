<p> Dr Marion Ferrat is in Christchurch for a meeting of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change . She specialises in making the panel 's reports more communicable for policy makers . <p> More than 100 scientists will descend on Christchurch next week to help governments better understand climate change . <p> The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change ( IPCC ) -- specifically the authors of a special report on climate change and land -- will convene in the city for six days to discuss various land-related matters . <p> The IPCC was established in 1988 by the United Nations Environment Programme and the World Meteorological Organization ? to provide a clear scientific view on the current understanding of climate change and its potential impact on the world . <p> GEORGE HEARD/STUFF <p> Ferrat says the key to explaining climate change issues is to " inspire people " and make the issues relate to people 's surroundings and day-to-day lives . Pictured is a glacier assessed during NIWA 's annual survey of glaciers in the Southern Alps . Recent hot weather resulted in a significant amount of glacier melt . <p> It has three working groups -- one looks at the physical science of climate change , one looks at how to adapt to it , and one looks at climate change mitigation and the actions that can be taken to reduce climate change . <p> Dr Marion Ferrat ? , head of communications for working group three , said creating these reports was " a big endeavour " . <p> " Climate change is a global issue , it affects all of us in different parts of the world in different ways and having access to all that expertise worldwide is great , " she said . <p> " An important thing to note is the IPCC does not do its own science , we assess the scientific , technical and socioeconomic information relating to our understanding of climate change . " <p> Ferrat was employed eight months ago " to help with the communicability of the reports " after a drive to make IPCC reports easier to understand . <p> She completed a PhD in climate science from Imperial College London in 2007 and worked as a postdoctoral researcher before returning in 2013 for a Masters in science communication . She took a job in the British Parliament as an energy and climate change specialist before moving to the IPCC . <p> " I was really interested in the science , but I kind of wanted to get out of the lab and do something that could relate a little bit more to people , " Ferrat said . <p> The IPCC offered her the chance to work with scientists from across the world and help " bring all these different perspectives together " . <p> Three special reports will be completed in the IPCC 's current assessment cycle , the first draft of which will be finalised in Christchurch before it is sent off for expert review . <p> Ferrat said the best way to get people to care about climate change was to frame it in an understandable way . Common climate change discussion points might be important in a scientific context -- like the difference between a 1.5 degree Celsius and 2C temperature rise -- but they could be overwhelming , she said . <p> " It 's really important to relate it to people 's lives and people 's surroundings . <p> " People do care about the environment , they care about what they can see . But also , trust people , anything can be explained . "
