<h> Oil and gas question a litmus test for PM on commitment to climate change <p> ALEXANDER GILLESPIE <p> Last updated 13:32 , March 21 2018 <p> ROSA WOODS/STUFF <p> Alexander Gillespie : The problem the prime minister faces is how she can reconcile her green goals with continuing to allow the future exploration , and later exploitation , for more -- not less -- carbon based energy sources <p> OPINION : The prime minister is currently thinking very hard about about the oil and gas exploration and exploitation process in New Zealand . <p> The reason this will be at the forefront of Jacinda Ardern 's mind is because she has knows that climate change is the biggest environmental challenge of the modern age , and unlike the previous government , the fight against global warming is a priority for her administration , or as she put it , " climate change is my generation 's nuclear free moment " . <p> This conclusion is justified because of both the global scale of the problem , and the poor response of New Zealand within the international framework of the Paris Agreement . Accordingly , she has committed New Zealand to be zero carbon by 2050 . <p> The problem the prime minister faces is how she can reconcile her green goals with continuing to allow the future exploration , and later exploitation , in New Zealand for more -- not less -- carbon based energy sources . <p> First , New Zealand has a long history of searching for , and producing , oil and gas . This industry which began in 1865 at Moturoa on the Taranaki foreshore , is today , our fourth largest export commodity by value . <p> The industry is responsible for thousands of high-wage jobs . Even though a vast amount of this money goes offshore , the processes still produce hundreds of millions of dollars of government tax and royalty revenue . <p> Given that only a small fraction of our seabed has been properly explored , it is quite possible that there are significant oil and gas reserves waiting to be exploited , or not . <p> Second , cutting New Zealand 's exploration into oil and gas , and even potentially its exploitation , will make very little difference to the problem of climate change globally . The reason it will have minimal difference internationally is that most other countries are continuing to explore for carbon based energy sources , as the profit motive continues to trump climate change concerns . New Zealand 's contribution to this area , in terms of the global production of carbon based fuels , is very small . <p> In an ideal world , this would be an easy decision for the prime minister to make as the push towards a moratorium of further exploration for oil and gas would be agreed at the global level , and all countries would act at the same time in a in a co-ordinated manner . As every country would be swallowing the same medicine , no country would suffer an economic loss disproportionate to any other . <p> First , if leadership on this issue is desired , she could follow the model of France and ban all future exploration for oil and gas within its territory . Although France has a very different economy and energy mix than New Zealand , its president , Emmanuel Macron , took the brave step of banning all exploration for oil and gas in French territories from the end of 2017 . In addition , although the existing contracts to exploit oil and gas will be allowed to run their normal course , all production must stop by 2040 . <p> This French law follows their announcement that the sale of all petrol and diesel vehicles will also be made illegal in their country , also , by 2040 . This approach by France has been echoed by the World Bank , which has announced it , " will no longer finance upstream oil and gas " after 2019 . <p> Second , if a ban is not possible , a short-term moratorium on exploration may be imposed and the topic reopened , after the next election . At the same time , the prime minister could commit to ensuring that the existing contracts for both exploration and exploitation will be fully honoured . This type of temporary moratorium would allow the Government time to carefully assess how such a prohibition could fit with our domestic commitments to , and the evolution of international responses under , the Paris Agreement . <p> The third option is drawing some bigger lines around no-go areas for exploration and exploitation . Although this does little in terms of initiatives on climate change , it can dovetail nicely with the protection of valuable marine ecosystems . <p> The United States has done this with parts of the Gulf of Mexico ( post its Deep-Water Horizon catastrophe ) , as has Belize with its prohibitions of exploration and exploitation around their fabulous reefs . This approach could be supplemented by doubling down on top shelf safety standards , while also mandating the industry to start work in hi-tech carbon capture technologies , as are currently being explored by the Norwegian oil at gas industry . <p> At the same time , the prime minister could try to create types of offsets , as in the exploration could continue , but she could promise lots of green goodies , from enhanced energy efficiency standards , through to climate specific targets for the agricultural sector . <p> There are no easy answers to this equation , although each option will determine the depth of her belief in whether climate change really is the nuclear issue of her generation .
