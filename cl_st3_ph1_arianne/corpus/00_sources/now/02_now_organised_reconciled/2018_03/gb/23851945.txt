<h> Global antibiotic consumption soars feeding spread of UK ' super-bugs ' <p> Drug-resistant superbugs are rising in the UK because of lack of regulation of antibiotics in developing countries , experts have warned . <p> One of the biggest studies of antibiotic use around the world has established that while antibiotic use in Britain has slowed , global consumption jumped by 65 percent , to 34.8 billion daily doses between 2000 and 2015 . <p> The analysis , led by the Center for Disease Dynamics , Economics &amp; Policy ( CDDEP ) in Washington DC , found the rise is being driven by skyrocketing use in low to middle income countries such as India , China and Turkey where consumption was up by 114% . <p> Resistant infections already kill an estimated 5,000 people in Britain each year . And global deaths are projected to grow to 10m a year by 2030 - one every three seconds - unless urgent action is taken , say experts . <p> The disclosures are likely to fuel fears about how common infections could become untreatable . <p> The latest study was published on Monday in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences . The authors of the report called for urgent action to better control antibiotic use around the world . <p> " As with climate change , there may be an unknown tipping point , and this could herald a future without effective antibiotics " , they said . " Even in the absence of tipping points , the decline of antibiotic effectiveness represents a major threat to human health . " <p> British doctors were at pains to stress that the issue of antibiotic resistance was not just a future problem but one which is already taking many British lives . <p> Bruce Keogh , the former National Medical Director of the NHS and now Chair of Birmingham Women 's and Children 's NHS Foundation Trust said : " I 've watched patients deteriorate in in front of my eyes because the germs are resistant . It 's a terrible thing for the patient and the family and all concerned . <p> " People think of this as a problem of the future but the reality is it is a problem now and it 's likely to get a lot worse in years to come . " <p> A recent study published in the British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology found that nearly two thirds of the fixed-dose combination ( FDC ) antibiotics sold in India had no record of regulatory approval . <p> The use of FDCs , which comprise two or more drugs in a fixed ratio , are only rarely approved in developed countries as they can be harmful and are more likely to spread resistance . <p> The study found that 53 FDCs manufactured by multinationals and sold in India between 2007 and 2012 were not approved in India , and only 4 were approved in the US and UK . <p> The multinationals involved included Abbott , Pfizer and GlaxoSmithKline . <p> Ramanan Laxminarayan , an expert in antibiotic resistance and director of the Center for Disease Dynamics , Economics and Policy in Washington DC , said multinational pharmaceutical companies had to take responsibility for the products they sold . <p> " If pharmaceutical companies sell antibiotics as FDCs where there is no clinical evidence for them and where there is likely to be antibiotic resistance there is no justification for it . It 's no longer sufficient for companies to say it 's okay because someone wants to buy my drug , " he said . <p> He said pharmaceutical companies had put " tremendous pressure " on the Indian government which is trying to ban the use of FDCs there . <p> " This is where they make their money - antibiotics are the most commonly sold drug around the world , " he said . <p> The Telegraph has established that some of these products are still available for sale in India without a prescription , with some packets costing less than one pound for ten tablets . <p> Last month , reporters were able to buy an FDC - called CEFI XL D RF , which is used to treat bacterial infections , at a chemist in Hyderabad , even though it has not been approved by the Indian national medicines regulator . <p> The antibiotic is marketed by the US pharmaceutical giant Abbott and other drugs linked to the firm also appeared on an electronic database use by pharmacists . <p> Mark Woolhouse , professor of infectious disease epidemiology at the University of Edinburgh , said the rise in the use of antibiotics in developing countries like India presented a global threat as superbugs travel round the world once created . <p> He said : " You can not restrict the problem to one country . Once resistance is established it will travel . " <p> Abbott said that the products listed in the study were medicines they acquired in 2010 and many were no longer sold in India . They said that " medicines still available in India all have proper approvals in place " . <p> A spokesperson said that " Fixed-dose combination medicines were created to improve patient adherence and they are important options for doctors when considering the best treatment for their patients , but should only be taken when prescribed by a doctor . This is important particularly in antibiotics , where overuse can contribute to antibiotic resistance " . <p> A spokesperson for Pfizer said that they are " committed to helping the health care community confront the challenges of antimicrobial resistance " and that " all our current formulations marketed in India have the applicable regulatory licenses and approvals in place " . <p> They also said that they were one of four companies who had " moved to decouple antibiotic sales volumes from sales agents ' bonuses " .
