<p> In light of the abundance of scientific evidence claiming that invasive species can have a detrimental effect on both biodiversity and human health , there still exist groups of people , even within academia , who believe that invasive species are not a concern . <p> With a leap of faith , U of T master 's student Darwin Sodhi suggested that the media can help scientists improve communication of their research and ideas . <p> As fake scientific news gains traction , true scientific facts become more misconstrued . In addition , Sodhi notes the challenges faced in determining whether scientific funding should prioritize health care or ecology , and how , in some cases , that may lead to fewer resources being available for biodiversity research . <p> At face value , this prioritization appears sensible because discoveries related to health would directly affect human beings . However , this overlooks the impact of invasive species on biodiversity and its immense effects on the human population . <p> According to the International Union for Conservation of Nature , between five and 20 per cent of all non-native species become problematic , or invasive . <p> A paper from last year noted that invasive species significantly affect and threaten the productivity of ecosystems through the alteration of biodiversity . This includes our food systems that rely on pollination . <p> Because invasive species can threaten resources such as food , water , fuel , and traditional medicines for millions of people , it may be high time scientists find more simplistic methods of articulating the essence and impact of invasive species research -- and why people should care about it . <p> Sodhi believes that when reporting science stories , the media could improve on asking the right questions and validating sources . He also acknowledges that some science has become too complex that scientists find it difficult to provide simplistic answers to general audiences . <p> The current generation may not have to deal with the negative impact of biodiversity loss from invasive species and climate change , but future generations will be burdened with the consequences of our negligence . <p> Sodhi , however , is holding onto positivity : he believes that society has it in them to acquire a sense of biodiversity consciousness . <p> " People do care about biodiversity and show genuine interest , " said Sodhi . " My friends and I go hiking in the summer and when I name species , they are like , ' Oh my gosh this is cool . ' And they all can walk around Toronto and point out the invasive species I work on .
