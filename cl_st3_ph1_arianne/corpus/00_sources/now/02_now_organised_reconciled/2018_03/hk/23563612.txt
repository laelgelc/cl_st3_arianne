<h> Leveraging local currency bond markets can help fund low-carbon infrastructure in Asia <p> By Ingrid van Wees <p> Viewpoint <p> Leading climate experts at last year 's COP23 conference in Bonn highlighted that we have only three years to safeguard our climate . There is no more room for delay in implementing the 2015 Paris Agreement on climate change . <p> One of the milestones to be achieved by 2020 is to expand the green bond market to at least 1% of the global bond market , currently about US$90 trillion . For this to happen , sovereign issuers must be completely on board . <p> In Asia , as elsewhere , the green bond market is now on policy makers ' radar . This is why ADB is supporting an initiative led by the People 's Republic of China to issue more green bonds within the Asean ( Association of Southeast Asian Nations ) , based on key principles like harmonization , quantification , and transparency . <p> Pushing for more Asean member countries to issue green bonds is part of a broader effort to mobilize more investment @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ developing Asia needs to invest an annual US$1.7 trillion until 2030 to build climate-resilient , sustainable infrastructure . Of this amount , US$200 billion per year alone should be allocated to adaptation , and US$41 billion more for climate-proofing vulnerable infrastructure . These amounts far exceed levels of investments achieved to date . <p> So , where can we find more money to build low-carbon infrastructure in developing Asia ? <p> The public sector to date provides 90% of the region 's overall infrastructure investment flows , mainly sourced from tax revenues . The rest is covered by multilateral development banks like ADB and the private sector , which only accounts for 0.4% of total investment . <p> We can mobilize other sources of funds such as retail savings , pensions or insurance funds to complement loans from banks , which are reluctant to support infrastructure projects that are often sub-investment grade and have long tenors . <p> For some of these investors it is important that the investment risk is mitigated for example by using a blend of concessional and commercial funds , as proposed in the ADB report @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ local currency bond markets , which are attractive for infrastructure investments that only earn in local currency because they reduce the foreign exchange risk . <p> Significant progress has been made in the China , India , and some Asean countries . In Asean+PRC ( China ) , local currency bounds outstanding by the second quarter of 2017 exceeded 70% of GDP , a significant jump from the average 58% over the past 5 years . <p> Many governments already directly finance infrastructure investments by issuing government bonds . Viet Nam issues bonds specifically to mobilize funds for public infrastructure projects . Such bonds could certainly be used for financing green projects , and therefore could be tagged as green bonds . <p> If other Asian governments start doing the same , there is enormous potential for rapid growth in the region 's sovereign green bond market . They should follow the example of Fiji , which last year became the first Asian sovereign issuer of a FJD100 million ( $48.13 million ) green bond . <p> Sovereign issuers in developing Asia see green bonds as an opportunity to set @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ through a growing local currency green bond market . <p> Issuing green bonds also demonstrates the sovereign 's commitment to meeting its Nationally Determined Contributions to reduce greenhouse gas emissions under the Paris Agreement in the medium term . This , in turn , encourages green business and shows citizens that their government is taking environmental issues and climate change seriously . <p> Finally , green bonds allow tapping into new segments of long-term capital like environmentally conscious groups of people , and thus help diversify the investor base . There are however some challenges on both the supply and demand sides . <p> On the supply side , developing Asia 's local currency green bond market is still characterized by a lack of liquidity , limited diversification of bond structures , and the absence of a large regular stream of bankable projects . <p> On the demand side , consistent demand from socially responsible investors is still pretty much restricted to Japan and only recently China . This hampers the market 's growth potential . <p> Initiatives in advanced economies in Europe and the US to decarbonize endowment , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ or to promote socially responsible investments -- are yet to gain traction in developing Asia . Mandatory requirements to diversify domestic investment portfolios are likewise missing across the region . <p> Sovereign governments could increase the pool of dedicated funds available to invest in green ( and social ) bonds by enacting regulations that integrate environmental , social , and governance ( ESG ) factors into the investment process and mix . <p> But there are reasons to be optimistic . The publication in March 2017 by the Chinese government of its green bond guidelines created impetus that helped the market to take off . <p> For a long time , ADB has been supporting its developing member countries to issue more green bonds . Based on our experience , sovereigns looking into issuing green bonds should take into account five fundamental concerns : <p> Green bond proceeds need to be used within a strong framework . Sovereigns that normally borrow in capital markets for general budgetary purposes should develop a robust green bond framework based on transparency to shore up investor confidence . The framework should be developed in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and rating agencies . <p> Sovereign issuers should set the standard for third party verification and/or third-party audit of their green bonds . <p> Treasury departments should collaborate with relevant government agencies to establish a green bond framework , and require investment managers to acquire knowledge and a license on impact investments and ESG factors to ensure credibility . <p> Sovereigns should create an enabling environment for other issuers to follow suit , including guidelines/standards coupled with fiscal support measures such as tax incentives . A good example is the green bond grant scheme proposed by the Monetary Authority of Singapore . <p> Efforts to promote green bond markets and national strategies for sustainable low- carbon development should be clearly linked . <p> There is a lot of potential for growth in the local currency green bond market in developing Asia , as long as sovereigns establish an enabling environment , and strong frameworks are applied . The key constraint will likely be the number and size of bankable green investments . <p> Ingrid van Wees is Vice-President for Finance and Risk Management at the Asian Development Bank .
