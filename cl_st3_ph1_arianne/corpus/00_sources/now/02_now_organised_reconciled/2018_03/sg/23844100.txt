<h> Living in limbo : Indonesia 's refugees face uncertain future <p> MEDAN , INDONESIA ( REUTERS ) - When she was eight , Ms Sharifa Begum was forced to flee her village in Myanmar 's Rakhine State after fighting erupted and homes were set on fire . <p> Separated from her parents in the panic , she escaped with her 17-year-old brother and 66 other Rohingya people on a boat , drifting at sea for a month with little food or drinking water . <p> Eventually rescued and towed to land by Indonesian fishermen , Ms Begum - whose name has been changed to protect her identity - has since spent almost half her life living as a migrant in limbo . <p> Like almost 14,000 other refugees in Indonesia and more than 250,000 across South-east Asia , Ms Begum , now 15 , has no access to citizenship and few rights in the country where she lives - but she is unable to go home due to safety fears . <p> " There is no peace back in Myanmar , so I can not return , " she told the Thomson Reuters Foundation at a refugee centre in Medan , one of Indonesia 's largest cities , on the island of Sumatra . <p> Since insurgent attacks last August sparked a military crackdown , amid reports of arson , murder and rape , nearly 700,000 more Rohingya have fled Myanmar , mostly to Bangladesh . <p> As many wealthier countries around the world resettle fewer refugees and impose stricter criteria to accept migrants , Ms Begum 's predicament could be shared in years to come by millions of others who flee their homes in response to pressures including climate change and persecution . <p> By the end of 2016 there were about 22.5 million refugees worldwide , according to the UN refugee agency ( UNHCR ) , the highest number recorded since the agency was established in 1950 , in the aftermath of World War Two . <p> During 2016 , 37 countries participated in UNHCR 's resettlement programme , taking in about 126,300 people , or less than 1 per cent of the world 's refugee population . <p> But in 2017 , the number of refugees resettled fell by nearly half , to just over 65,000 . It is likely to drop even further , refugee officials said , as the United States , Australia and Europe tighten restrictions . <p> For Ms Begum , the news is particularly bad : She has just been told by UNHCR that she - like many fellow refugees in Indonesia - is extremely unlikely to be resettled in a third country . <p> Several aid workers in Indonesia said some refugees had attempted suicide or killed themselves after being given that information . <p> Ms Begum now faces an uncertain future in Indonesia , where she has no right to settle , as the country is not a signatory to the UN refugee convention . <p> Ms Mozhgan Moarefizadeh , an Iranian refugee and co-founder of the Refugees and Asylum-seekers Information Centre , which runs projects around Jakarta , said those she works with often suffer mental health problems , including depression and anxiety as a result of past abuses or experiences in their home countries . <p> " Indonesia does n't give any rights to refugees , " said the Iranian , who has spent five years in Indonesia . " The challenge is to survive on a day-to-day basis . But most lose hope . " <p> Indonesia 's government should support migrants with housing , healthcare , counselling and training , she said . <p> Campaigns to boost awareness of the plight of refugees in Indonesia are needed both locally and overseas , she said . <p> In South-east Asia , only Cambodia , the Philippines and Timor-Leste have signed the 1951 Refugee Convention , which enshrines basic human rights for migrants and displaced people . <p> Although Indonesia has not , it does have a long tradition of offering protection to refugees , who in the past have used it as a transit country . <p> But after Australia began tightening rules for asylum-seekers about four years ago , many in Indonesia could hope only for a return home or official resettlement . <p> With these options now unlikely for many , refugees in Indonesia - largely from Afghanistan , Myanmar , Somalia and Iraq - have been left stateless and rely on aid agencies to survive . <p> " We have a population here that can not work , where every day is the same , " said Ms Mariam Khokhar , head of the International Organisation for Migration ( IOM ) office in Medan . " Families or single people stuck in time for four , five or six years . " <p> Many live on a small monthly handout from the IOM or from relatives to pay for food and clothing . The IOM also provides basic services , such as healthcare , accommodation and training . <p> Major humanitarian crises , such as the recent Rohingya flight from Myanmar to Bangladesh and Syrians escaping war to other Middle Eastern countries , often soak up a large chunk of international aid , officials said , with little funding left for those deemed to be residing in safer countries . <p> Life is monotonous for many refugees in Indonesia . Some have lived in the country for up to a decade , unable to move freely . <p> " We feel like we 're in an open jail . We can not work and we can not communicate freely , " said Mr Rahmatallah , 35 , a father of three who fled Afghanistan after death threats from the Taleban . <p> " My daughter , who goes to a school where the other children have beautiful clothes , asked me for coloured pencils , " he said , fighting back tears . <p> " It 's a very small thing that any father should provide for his child , but I can not , " said Mr Rahmatallah , who did not want to reveal his full name . <p> While Indonesia 's refugees largely live peacefully alongside local people , often in cramped , cheap apartments , rooming houses or detention centres , there have been flashpoints of conflict . <p> Some Indonesians , for instance , had wrongly believed their new neighbours were getting free government handouts , stirring resentment , said Mr Gelora Ginting , head of Tuntungan district in Medan . This was resolved when aid agencies explained the bleaker reality , Ms Ginting added . <p> Because more than half of Indonesia 's refugees are Shi'ite Muslims from Afghanistan , while Indonesian Muslims are mainly Sunni , religious tensions are another threat . <p> " These refugees , seeing the rise of sectarianism and Sunni militancy in Indonesia , and having already witnessed this back home ... do n't want to stay here , " said Mr Andreas Harsono of Human Rights Watch in Jakarta . <p> Barred from seeking paid work , some refugees attend classes run by themselves or aid agencies to learn skills like English , carpentry and computer repair , hoping to prepare for the future and help out their host communities , said Ms Mitra Salima Suryono of UNHCR Indonesia . <p> Refugees in Medan who have trained as hairdressers now regularly cut hair for local and street children , while furniture made by migrants is donated to an orphanage . <p> To keep the peace , many refugees attend regular meetings with police , community leaders and immigration officials . <p> Indonesian officials say they have sympathy for what the migrants have been through - one reason they are willing to support them . <p> " If we suffered conflicts like they had , we would want to be treated in the same way that us Indonesians now treat them , " said local official Ginting . <p> Some efforts are underway to improve life for refugees . In late 2016 , Indonesian President Joko Widodo began allowing refugee children to go to local schools . <p> His administration is also seeking to phase out detention centres in favour of housing migrants within the community . <p> Ms Suryono , of UNHCR Indonesia , said her agency had helped more than 40 refugee children enroll in local schools , and was in talks with the government about letting refugees do work that benefits local communities and provides a little income . <p> Indonesia 's Foreign Ministry did not respond to repeated requests for comment . <p> Occasionally , there is some good news . Mr Muhammad Nayeem , 18 , a Rohingya from Myanmar now living in Medan , received a text message a few weeks ago telling him he was one of the lucky few set to be resettled in the United States this month . <p> After travelling to Indonesia in 2015 on a boat crammed with 1,000 refugees - half of whom died at sea - Mr Nayeem is part of a batch of resettlement referrals that started back in 2016 . <p> Aid agency officials , however , say that offer is unlikely to be repeated after Republican Donald Trump became US President last year and began imposing additional migration restrictions . <p> " Education has always been my dream , " said Mr Nayeem , who teaches English to refugee children . <p> " I intend to study more so that later I can help my community and inform the whole world about what is going on in my country . I want to see democracy in my country . " <h> The Straits Times <p> We have been experiencing some problems with subscriber log-ins and apologise for the inconvenience caused .
