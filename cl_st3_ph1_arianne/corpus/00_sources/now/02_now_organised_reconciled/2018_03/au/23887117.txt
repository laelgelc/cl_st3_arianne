<p> It 's going on 9am and the foyer of Chifley Tower in Sydney is buzzing with corporate worker bees ordering their morning hits at the recently opened District Brasserie . I , however , am mentally elsewhere , down on a family farm in the Appalachias rooting for a lovestruck turkey as her inexperienced suitor tries to figure his next move under the watchful eye of their human matchmaker . <p> On the dot of nine , I look up from my Kindle and there she is , the matchmaker herself , being ushered to my table : Barbara Kingsolver , breeder of rare heritage turkeys , biologist , gardener and author extraordinaire . <p> It 's not easy getting an interview with Kingsolver , I 'd been warned , but she 's agreed to meet me for breakfast , in lieu of this column 's usual lunch date . <p> It 's a rush , I tell you , to look up from her 2007 book Animal , Vegetable , Miracle , about the year she and her family went cold turkey on America 's hyper-industrialised food industry to grow their own , and see her there . Sort of like Alice falling through the looking glass , but in the opposite direction . <p> Her books ' Animal , Vegetable , Miracle ' ; ' The Poisonwood Bible ' ; and ' Flight Behavior ' have all been New York Times bestsellers . She sits opposite me at the four-seat round table , but I move up a chair to get closer . I do n't want to miss a word over the noise at the preparation counter behind her . Walking among the Gondwana 's 5000-year-old antarctic beech trees " was a religious experience for me " , says Kingsolver . " They 're so old that the earth has eroded away from their roots and it 's like they 're standing up on stilts . They 're such ancient beings . I loved those old trees . " I was in the water all day , every day , and the diversity and the life there is so encouraging , because from a distance all we hear is that it 's dead , dead , dead . I mean , it almost gives you permission to give up when you think everything is already gone . So I spent four days in absolute wonder looking at living coral , living invertebrates , living reef fish communities . And I thought : I need to talk about this . People need to know how much is still here to love . " <p> This advocacy for the natural world we humans are intent on destroying is a recurring theme for Kingsolver , who switched to biology after going to DePauw University in Indiana on a piano scholarship in 1973 . <p> Her last novel and 14th book , Flight Behaviour ( 2012 ) , is about the effect of climate change ( and media exploitation and political opportunism ) on a rural community in Appalachia . <p> Animal , Vegetable , Miracle -- written with asides from Hopp and her elder daughter , Camille , then 19 -- is an excoriating critique of the United States ' all-powerful industrial agricultural complex . Her success in getting that fumbling male turkey to make nookie with his suitor , and then getting her to sit on the eggs until the chicks hatched was a radical act of defiance in a nation where male turkeys are milked for their semen ( there 's a job you do n't want ) , and the eggs are artificially inseminated and then hatched in incubators . It would n't do to hoe down , somehow . <p> Breakfast sorted , I ask the question that , nearly 18 months after the election of Donald Trump , still confounds outsiders : What happened in America ? <p> " I just wrote a novel about it , and it 's coming out this fall , " says Kingsolver . <p> So does she see herself more as a scientist or an artist ? " Well , I 'm both . " Supplied <p> Unsheltered , due out in October , is set in one rundown house in New Jersey over two troubled periods in history : 1871 and 2016 . " I wanted to look at paradigm shift , at how people behave at these moments of history when all of the rules they trusted to hold true suddenly do n't apply any more . " <p> In 1871 , thanks to Darwin 's theory of evolutionary biology , " the very notion of what it meant to be human was under threat " . <p> " In the US , especially , it was a difficult time because we 'd just been through our civil war . One in six men died . And it was very destructive . There was a lot of burning of cities , of crop lands . Then the country was just kind of artificially put back together and told OK , the war 's over , but there was still so much ill will and suffering and economic uncertainty . <p> " Then , suddenly , added to the mix is this idea that humans are not king of the world ; that natural laws apply to us in the same way they apply to all other life . It was a coalition of things that made people terribly insecure . " <p> As for 2016 , " the crisis of now is that our whole economic system is built on the presumption of infinite growth . The measure of a country 's success is its GDP . The measure of a company 's success is its growth . If you 're standing still , that 's failure . <p> " Oh my goodness , look at that , " she breaks off as her pastries arrive . But it 's a momentary distraction ... <p> " But this notion of infinite expansion has hit a wall . I mean , we 're using 125 per cent of the Earth 's biocapacity every year . Obviously , that 's not sustainable -- but nobody 's talking about it . And there 's a certain way people behave when all their assumptions suddenly prove to be wrong . They kind of hunker down and believe stronger . Or they look for leaders who can protect them from new disturbing truths that require a very dramatic change of mind . <p> " So that , in a nutshell , is what I think is happening . We will have to find a new way of living within our means as a species . And most of us do n't want to do that . " <p> When Trump came along , she says , she thought she could n't write him in because he was a flash in pan . " But he was just too irresistible , so I wrote him in -- and then , to my amazement , he did n't go away . So everything that happened , as terrible as it was for the country , was good for the book . " There 's that laugh again . <p> Kingsolver is wrestling her pain au chocolate with a fork . " These are huge , " she says . " I do n't even know if I 'll finish half of one . " <p> Not that huge projects usually intimidate her . Between the research and the writing , The Poisonwood Bible took more than 10 years . But it had its seeds even earlier , in 1963 , when her family moved from rural Kentucky to live in a remote village in the Republic of Congo , where her father worked as a doctor . <p> She began plotting her 2009 masterpiece , The Lacuna , set in Frida Kahlo 's Mexico and the US of McCarthyism , in 2002 . " Sometimes I did feel I was trying to move a mountain with a teaspoon , " she writes on her website . <p> She writes , she says , like a scientist . " Icreate my experimental conditions and then I let the characters sort of live out the hypothesis . " <p> So does she see herself more as a scientist or an artist ? " Well , I 'm both . I think I see the world as a scientist but I work as an artist . I think about these big ideas and I establish my scope and my conditions but then , day after day and year after year I 'm consumed with craft . I 'm writing sentences , I 'm creating metaphors . What I 'm mostly think about when I 'm writing is good writing . " <p> In between , she tends her rows of vegetables , cans tomatoes and cares for the sheep on the farm in west Virginia , southern Appalachia , where the family moved in 2004 from Tucson , Arizona . <p> It 's a lot of work , but it 's a pleasure . " It 's a good balance because writing is so cerebral . " <p> Kingsolver 's tone can best be described as mild -- until the question comes up of whether the #MeToo campaign has gone too far . <p> " No , " she says fiercely . " It 's women 's impulse to say , oh dear , we might be inconveniencing someone . That 's bullshit . No . No ! How could you go too far in reaction against that ? I really think women need to check our impulse for apology and say no , we are going to scream about this until it 's taken seriously . " <p> You wo n't , however , find Kingsolver on social media -- or the literary hustings , for that matter . As a working mother , she says , she 's always had to be careful about her time . She wrote her first novel , The Bean Trees , in 1987 while she was pregnant with Camille , and received word that Harper Collins had bought it on the day she got back from the hospital with her newborn . <p> " I became a professional author and a mother on the same day . I wanted to do a really good job of both , so I had to learn how to be fiercely protective of my time and attention . And that meant sorting out who really needs a piece of me . <p> " From the beginning whole categories of things that authors do that are not writing -- going to conferences , giving talks , going to workshops and schmoozing events -- I did n't have that luxury ; I had babies at home . When social media came along , I saw what a time suck that could be and I guess it was very simple for me to say , hey , that 's just not where I 'm going to spend my time . " <p> Besides which , her life is really not important , she says . " I 'm quite sure my readers will be happier with my books than me .
