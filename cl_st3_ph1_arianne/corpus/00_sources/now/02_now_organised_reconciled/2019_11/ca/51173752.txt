<h> The World Outdoors : Research shows growing perils of climate change <p> November is the best time to watch the skies for golden eagles , large raptors that are named for the golden plumage of their napes . They are among the last of our fall migrants . ( Don Taylor/Special to Postmedia News ) <p> Thanks to research around the globe , our knowledge of the avian world keeps expanding . <p> I reported in September on the most profound research results : An international research team led by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology , Environment and Climate Change Canada and four other organizations published a report in the online journal Science about dramatic declines in Canadian and American bird populations . <p> Since 1970 we have lost a staggering 2.9 billion birds , representing a 29 per cent drop . We expected to see ongoing declines in certain groups , such as grassland birds , aerial insectivores and shorebirds . It was shocking , however , that populations of " backyard birds " -- those commonly seen -- such as Baltimore orioles and sparrows were also found to be falling dramatically . <p> Without this science @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Information about these findings is at 3billionbirds.org . <p> Echoing these findings was the updated State of Canada 's Birds report , released in June by Environment and Climate Change Canada on behalf of the North American Bird Conservation Initiative Canada . " The data provided by thousands of citizen science volunteers and professional biologists in this report have helped reveal dramatic declines in many birds once common in Canada , " said Steven Price , president of Bird Studies Canada , which is part of the initiative . <p> The report also asserted : " What is good for birds is good for us . We need to keep our water , air and land clean by finding innovative alternatives to pesticide use and single-use plastics . " <p> There was a bit of good news in State of Canada 's Birds . The improvements in waterfowl and raptor populations demonstrate that deliberate implementation of credible conservation plans , including habitat restoration and protections , can make a big difference . <p> A team of University of Edinburgh researchers published a report last month that highlighted what 's known @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ food is available when young birds hatch . <p> Working with blue tits , the research team found that warmer nights induced by climate change caused earlier hatching of caterpillars on which the tits feed . The scientists found the chicks are hatching after their main food source was most plentiful . <p> Interesting research continues to be undertaken closer to home . A study conducted at Western University 's Advanced Facility for Avian Research determined that predator-induced fear resulted in conditions similar to post-traumatic stress disorder in black-capped chickadees . The research team led by Liana Zanette found this in turn harmed behaviours such as parenting . The findings were published in Scientific Reports at nature.com . <p> A team of Canadian researchers found songbirds that were exposed to a neonicotinoid insecticide lost weight and delayed their migration by 3 ? days . The delays can reduce survival during migration and decrease reproductive success . The team was working with white-throated sparrows to test its hypothesis . The study was published in September in sciencemag.org .
