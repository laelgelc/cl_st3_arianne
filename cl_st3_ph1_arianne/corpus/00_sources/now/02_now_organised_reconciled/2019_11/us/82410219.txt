<h> On the winner of , and finalists for , the National Book Award for Young People 's Literature . <p> The judges for the National Book Awards seem to have threaded the needle rather deftly in awarding this year 's winning medal for young people 's literature to Martin W. Sandler for the left-leaning nonfiction chronicle " 1919 : The Year That Changed America " ( Bloomsbury , 192 pages , $24.99 ) . It 's a decent book , well written and well organized , with lots of fascinating archival photographs . But it is not an unusual or strikingly distinctive work , given that the point of view that it takes on social history is , shall we say , the dominant , progressive one . <p> Mr. Sandler has written a great many other illustrated history books for young readers ( including " How the Beatles Changed the World " in 2014 ) . So why did this one get the nod ? Perhaps because the elements that Mr. Sandler highlights from 1919 can be made to connect so easily with certain favored contemporary hot-button issues . ( More about those books in a moment . ) <p> As Mr. Sandler reminds us , 100 years ago , World War I had ended , but America was still in the grip of the catastrophic influenza epidemic that had started the year before . This was the backdrop for a tumultuous new year marked by factious and sometimes violent social campaigns ( Prohibition , women 's suffrage ) , race riots , labor unrest and anarchist bombings . Mr. Sandler 's lively discussion of these dramatic occurrences in 1919 turns tedious when he seeks to find modern-day reverberations in such issues as gun control , climate change , voting rights and the debunked yet apparently unkillable claims of a gender pay gap . If 1919 changed America , why strain to show that it has n't ? <p> The past is another country , so too is middle school ; they do things differently there .
