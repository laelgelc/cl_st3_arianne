<p> In the July Democratic presidential debate , the candidates were asked how they planned to tackle the anthropogenic climate change crisis . " We 're too late , " tech entrepreneur Andrew Yang said , flatly . " We are 10 years too late . We need to start moving our people to higher ground . " <p> While throwing up our hands and effectively claiming we 're finished is at best inaccurate and at worst dangerous , Yang is right about one thing : Millions of Americans will eventually need to retreat from our flood-prone coastlines . The complexity of such a reality is staggering , but we can -- and should be -- starting with the simple question of who of our people will be the first to go . <p> New Jersey 's managed retreat program , Blue Acres , offers a glimpse of that " who . " The voluntary program began in 1995 , under Republican Gov . Christine Todd Whitman , as a taxpayer-approved and -funded initiative to acquire lands in the state 's coastal areas , where the risk of storm damage and flooding is high . Following Superstorm Sandy -- which , seven years ago , devastated New Jersey coastal communities from the Delaware Bay to Raritan Bay , Cape May to Sea Bright -- Blue Acres was infused with $300 million from the federal disaster relief package and given the mandate to acquire 1,000 flood-prone , Sandy-damaged residential properties . As of last month , the state had demolished 700 properties across nine counties . None of those acquisitions , however , have been of single-family homes on the oceanfront , where the worst of Sandy 's damages occurred . <p> Instead , the vast majority of the acquisitions have been in blue-collar communities along the state 's rivers and bays . In Cumberland , New Jersey 's poorest county , there have been 106 buyouts alone , all of which were in bayfront communities that have long suffered from the increased erosion brought on by sea level rise and a chronic inability to meet the state and federal cost-benefit thresholds required for the kind of shoreline hardening projects that the far wealthier Jersey Shore municipalities regularly receive . Clearly , no one can question the shore municipalities ' importance as key drivers of New Jersey 's overall economy , but the water is rising just as fast at the shore as it is in Cumberland County . As adaptation to sea level rise becomes more of a necessity , it can not be that only some of our coastal residents will be encouraged to retreat from their homes while others , whose properties fall on the positive side of cost-benefit , will be encouraged to remain . <h> Pattern of inequitable treatment <p> Several recent studies have shown that inequitable managed retreat is not just a New Jersey problem , but countrywide . These findings are consistent with the Blue Acres demographics in Cumberland County . <p> Additionally , a consistently overlooked slice of New Jersey 's coastal populace is the lower-to-middle class year-round residents of the Jersey Shore municipalities . For them , a Blue Acres buyout , even if they wanted one , is becoming increasingly difficult , since the program only purchases clusters of neighborhoods , not single homes . As multi-million-dollar investment properties -- whose absentee owners do n't have to worry about being displaced after a disaster like Sandy , or face financial insecurities from higher insurance premiums and other post-disaster costs -- spring up all around the homes of these year-round residents , the chance of bringing enough neighbors together to initiate a Blue Acres buyout is slim . <p> In the last century at the shore , the water has risen by 1.5 feet -- and since 1993 , the annual rate of rise has intensified by about 50% . Even if sea levels on the Jersey Shore increase at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration 's intermediate-low prediction of 2.3 feet by 2100 , significant portions of the state 's barrier islands , some of which hold millions in property value , will be under water on every high tide . At the extreme end of NOAA 's prediction of 8 feet or more -- which would be precipitated by mass collapse of Arctic and Antarctic ice sheets -- nothing will be left of the Jersey Shore as we know it today . <p> With each additional inch of rise , the specter of managed retreat looms ever larger . We are not too late -- through Blue Acres , the state is already adapting and it will have to adapt much more . But , will we continue to let our blue-collar communities bear the weight of retreat , or will we face the reality of sea level rise with an eye toward equity ? Will we armor our wealthy Atlantic coast enclaves with monolithic infrastructure in order to buy more time , or @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ adapt responsibly , thus heading off a devastating tipping point in the real estate market ? This country has too often failed its most noble founding ideal -- that all men are created equal . When it comes to retreat from the rising water , let 's try not to fail ourselves again . <p> Andrew S.
