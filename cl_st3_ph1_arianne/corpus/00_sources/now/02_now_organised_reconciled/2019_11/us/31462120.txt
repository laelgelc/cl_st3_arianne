<p> Gray 's Reef is little more than a drop in the ocean 19 miles off the Georgia coast , but do n't confuse size for significance . In one of his last official acts , President Jimmy Carter declared the reef a national marine sanctuary at the urging of conservationists who said its abundance of life was unique and worth saving for future generations . <p> For nearly 40 years , the U.S. government has protected the reef , home to more than 200 species of fish and an amazing array of nearly 1,000 different kinds of invertebrates . Recreational fishing and diving are allowed , but commercial fishing and other kinds of exploitation are not . <p> And Gray 's Reef has served as a global inspiration . Following the lead of the U.S. , other nations have designated similar sanctuaries and protected areas , which now cover about 6% of the world 's oceans -- a bonanza for researchers but , more importantly , an important tool for safeguarding the seas . <p> Doubts remain about how much of the ocean they can truly save . Scientists have tied the warming to the rise of sea levels , the disappearance of fish stocks and the bleaching of corals . The ocean also has become more acidic as humans have released higher concentrations of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere , and that jeopardizes valuable shellfish and the plankton that form the base of the food chain . <p> The supporters for the protected areas range from sustenance fishermen on the tiniest islands of the Pacific to researchers at the most elite institutions of academia . <p> " We 're not protecting these areas just for ourselves , " Roldan Mu ? oz , a research fishery biologist with the U.S. 's National Marine Fisheries Service , says during a research trip to the reef , " they 're for our nation .
