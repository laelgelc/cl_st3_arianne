( photo ; Sun Sentinel ) <p> The third-largest coral reef on the planet , the Florida Reef Tract , spanning from the Dry Tortugas to Stuart , has succumbed to death by sewage pollution . <p> Every day , nearly 300 million gallons of wastewater are still dumped from a half dozen sewage outfalls onto the reef tract from Miami-Dade to Broward counties . Until recently in the Keys , fecal pathogens and nutrients from roughly 30,000 homes and businesses using septic tanks and cesspits seeped onto the reef tract resulting in lethal coral diseases and reef-smothering algal blooms . <p> Advertisement <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Lucie County , leachate from another 300,000 septic tanks may presently contribute to the reef 's collapse . <p> The demise of South Florida 's reef tract has been a loss for Florida 's recreational divers , fishermen and those dependent on the tourist economy in the hotel , restaurant and other service industries . Yet , it was n't until after the reef tract suffered a near-total coral loss that a $1.2 billion wastewater infrastructure project was initiated along the Keys island archipelago to hook-up septic tanks and cesspits to modern centralized wastewater treatment systems . <p> Dr. Peter Barile is an environmental scientist and a published scientific expert on Florida 's water quality and health of coral reefs and estuaries . It does not account for the increased treatment capacity needed for Florida 's future population that expands by 1000 new residents every day , or the impacts of climate change-induced rainfall events , flooding and sea level rise that will increase in the coming decades . <p> When Irma hit southwest Florida , in September of 2017 , literally hundreds of millions of gallons of sewage were dumped , and flooded residential areas hemorrhaged septic tank effluent into coastal waters , creating a public health nightmare for residents and an impending environmental catastrophe . <p> Last October , in Michael 's path in the Panhandle , a damaged and dilapidated wastewater collection system dumped tens of thousands of gallons of sewage and may cost $200 million to repair . <p> Florida residents and tourists have first-hand experience with harmful and toxic algal blooms that result from sewage dumping @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Florida , a red tide event began one month after Irma and lasted for over 16 months , with massive fish kills along the coast , hundreds of manatee and dolphin deaths , and an unaccounted loss of tourism revenue . Along the Indian River Lagoon , in east-central Florida , sewage dumping caused by Irma totaled over 20 million gallons , which supported a brown tide that lasted for six months , and resulted in impacts on ecosystems , tourism and real estate value . <p> This past summer , Gov . Ron DeSantis ' Blue-green Algal Task Force concluded that to reduce harmful algal blooms , the State of Florida should aggressively mitigate its sewage leaks and dumping from its dilapidated wastewater infrastructure .
