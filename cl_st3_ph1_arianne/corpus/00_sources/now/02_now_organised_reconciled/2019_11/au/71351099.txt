<h> Opinion <h> Ripping up Murray-Darling plan would ignore the lessons of history <h> By David Papps and Chris Guest <p> November 4 , 2019 -- 12.00am <p> Referring to the Murray-Darling Basin Plan , NSW Deputy Premier John Barilaro said last week that he wanted to " rip the bloody thing up " . Times of extreme stress , like the current drought , can trigger bad decisions . <p> Ripping up the plan would be a mistake , which would in no way help the community through the drought . We need to stand back from the current tensions over the basin plan to disentangle the rhetoric from the reality . <p> There has always been conflict over water in Australia . It is scarce , unreliable and highly valuable . <p> The first attempt to agree on how to share the waters of the River Murray was a conference of the colonies of NSW , Victoria and South Australia , in 1857 . Water was the most contentious issue in the constitutional conferences . The debate delayed Federation and in the end was not resolved in the constitution . <p> Advertisement <p> A deal was not @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ for the first time , by mediating negotiations and putting enough money on the table to give the states everything they wanted . <p> Loading <p> The world has changed since 1914 . Then , the cry was " irrigate the Murray dry " . Now , the challenge is to ensure its sustainability by finding a balance between the needs of the basin 's rivers and irrigated agriculture . <p> The challenge was first recognised as far back as 1973 . At that time , Gough Whitlam pronounced " it was no longer sufficient to distribute the quantity of water -- there was now a need to restore its quality " . Efforts by the states over the following 30 years failed to address the problem . <p> The premise of the basin plan is simple -- a basin of interconnected rivers , which do not respect borders , needs one plan . More science , more thought and more consultation went into the plan than any water plan before or since . <p> Its central instrument is to set sustainable limits on water use . It was not intended to be , nor can it be , a drought-proofing instrument . So why has the plan gone off course ? <p> First , with the plan in place , every problem in the basin is now attributed to the plan . A powerful example is the terrible fish kill in the Lower Darling last summer . The fish kill was seen as a failure of the plan . In fact , the plan did not then , and still does not , have any impact on the Lower Darling . The river is subject to a NSW water sharing plan , made just before the plan took effect . It is an umbrella instrument delivered through state water resource plans . Effective implementation is a tough slog , requiring commitment and leadership from the Commonwealth and co-operation from states . <p> In recent years , from former Nationals leader Barnaby Joyce on , Commonwealth commitment to the plan has been lacking , as it has in a range of other important areas , such as climate change . <p> Loading <p> States have gamed the plan . As the Lower Darling fish kill demonstrates , the plan suits the states because it allows them to deflect responsibility . Despite the states having negotiated every element of the plan with the Commonwealth and then signed off on it , and regularly committing to deliver it " on time and in full " at meetings of the Council of Australian Governments , once premiers and ministers have got back home , the practice has been very different . <p> The result is that all major elements of the plan are running significantly behind schedule . While not officially paused , progress is glacial . <p> @ @ . How to deal with it is a wicked problem , for which there is no single or simple answer . The first step must be to establish a process to co-ordinate government responses . So far this has been sadly lacking . In 2006 , during the last drought , then prime minister John Howard set up such a group . This achieved a great deal by way of states sharing water according to need . <p> The context for governments working together must be to understand properly the effect of current policies , including the basin plan . This analysis needs to be based on science , not ideology , and underpinned by goodwill , not grandstanding . <p> There is no doubt that , even though it is only very early in its ( faltering ) implementation , the plan is delivering real environmental benefits . One hundred and twenty significant sites have received water they would not otherwise have had .
