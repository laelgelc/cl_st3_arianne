<h> Editorial <h> Yes , there is a climate emergency . Politicians need to do more than talk about it <p> The Pope has done it . So have the governments of Malta , Argentina and France . The likes of Nagasaki Prefecture , Christchurch City and Darebin City councils have got on board . And now more than 11,000 scientists from 153 countries have joined in with a new report that starts with a bold statement : " Scientists have a moral obligation to clearly warn humanity of any catastrophic threat ... We declare clearly .. and unequivocally that planet Earth is facing a " climate emergency . " <p> It 's a change in language from scientists that is surprising and welcome . The use of the term " emergency " as a substitute for " change " has become a rallying point worldwide for governments and organisations wanting to show off their climate credentials in a symbolic way . While " change " conveys a gradual process , " emergency " ups the ante on how quickly and dramatically action must be taken . <p> Credit:Glenn Campbell <p> In the exacting world of science , fear @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of global warming , such as those of the UN Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change , use opaque language to avoid being caught out . This new report shows no such hesitation . Presented with simple graphs showing the human influence on climate and easy-to-understand policy proposals , it 's a bold attempt to make the science more accessible to a wider audience . It 's a good start , and one that is long overdue . <p> Without such clear science , the climate debate has turned into a debate over whether people should believe , or not , what the scientists are saying . As former prime minister Tony Abbott discovered -- right up until his fall at the last election -- the case for doubt can be a powerful political tool to garner support within his party and the wider community , while former Labor leader Bill Shorten found the case for believing struggled to have the same impact .
