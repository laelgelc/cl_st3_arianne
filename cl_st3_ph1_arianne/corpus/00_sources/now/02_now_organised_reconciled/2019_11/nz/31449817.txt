<h> Floating the world 's most profitable company <p> Rarely does a troubled stock market float tell us so much about the world . When the company is Saudi Aramco , revelations gush like a blowout well , writes Rod Oram <p> Saudi Arabia 's Prince Mohammed bin Salman promised much three years ago by offering a small stake in the company to global investors . Worse , the float of WeWork , the office space-sharing company , was abandoned because of governance issues involving spending and investment decisions by Adam Neumann , its discredited founder . <p> Saudi Arabia was directly connected with those debacles . In August , it joined the MSCI Emerging Markets Index , thereby attracting some US$18b in foreign portfolio equity inflows , with a further US$5b expected by year end . The IMF estimates the country , given its young demographic , needs to create up to 1 million in the next five years . <p> Such diversification imperatives are driving Prince Mohammad 's Vision 2030 . He aims to transform the economy through strategic investment in the likes of manufacturing and special economic zones , such as a planned robotic city called Neom near the Red Sea . <p> With or without foreign investors , Saudi Aramco is crucial to his ambitions . It has 260 billion barrels of proved reserves , equal to 52 years of production at current production rates . That is more than triple the proved reserves of the next five state and multinational oil companies . Last year Aramco pumped one in eight of the world 's barrels of crude oil . <p> Thanks to those huge and easy to exploit reserves its extraction costs are US$2.80 a barrel , one-third the international average .
