<h> Rat-aclysm tests forest defences <p> The hills are alive with the sound of squeaking . Inside the Conservation Department 's battle against a record number of rodents <p> When Department of Conservation ranger Heath Sinclair and his team checked traps in the Landsborough Valley , in South Westland , in August , they were so alarmed by what they saw that , on their return , a flurry of emails was sent to see what more could be done . <p> Out of 280 traps , the trappers found 201 rats and 15 stoats . " That was a big number , " Sinclair says . A year earlier they 'd found only 29 rats . The previous record was 109 rats , in about 2010 . <p> " It 's concerning because it 's a lot of rats , and it makes the days a lot longer , " the DoC ranger says . The traps have to be scraped out , with carcasses flung into the forest , and reset with new bait such as eggs or a rabbit-based paste , called Erayz . Another check is being made this week . <p> The Landsborough is arguably DoC 's highest profile predator control site , a 21-year study of intensive pest control to protect the native mohua , or yellowhead bird . <p> This year the valley 's one of the many fronts in DoC 's fight against introduced predators , which have exploded in numbers because of the so-called mega mast -- a massive seeding and fruiting of various tree and plant species that provides a food bonanza for native creatures and predators alike . <p> Other battle areas include Arthur 's Pass , inland from Christchurch , which is experiencing a mouse infestation , and the Makarora Valley , near Wanaka , where record rat numbers are being reported . In August , overnight tracking reached 74 percent . <p> In response to the widespread explosion of rodent numbers , the department 's embarking on its biggest ever predator control programme , Tiakina Nga Manu , which is expected to cost up to $41 million and aerial 1080 drops covering some 900,000 hectares , as well as ground trapping . <p> " Predator numbers are exceptional , pretty much across the board , " says Peter Morton , DoC 's predator control boss . Rodent numbers are so high that the area covered by 1080 drops has shrunk and the cereal bait application rate increased , to knock the numbers down . <p> Crunch time to stop munch time <p> Rats are significant predators of New Zealand 's wildlife . ( With stoats and possums , they round out the so-called big three of predators . ) <p> Morton says rats eat chicks , eggs , and in some cases even the adults , particularly when they 're on the nest . Rats are also the main food of stoats , which kill a lot of adult wildlife species , particularly females . If you have a whole lot of rodents -- rats and mice -- in the forest , that inevitably boosts stoat numbers . <p> " They come a little later , " Morton says of stoats . " They 'll start showing up from December onwards . <p> " It 's sort of a double hit , with rats causing damage right now and stoats about to rise and cause damage over the first half of next year . " <p> At 30 key sites DoC monitors for rodents , numbers have gone through the roof at all but two . At those two spots -- Wet Jacket , on the West Coast of Fiordland National Park , and Blue Mountains , in Southland -- the numbers are still bad , they just have n't increased as rapidly as expected . Aerial 1080 will be dropped over almost 100,000 hectares for two overlapping reasons -- to protect the nationally critical orange-fronted parakeet ( kakariki karaka ) , and great spotted kiwi ( roroa , the tallest of the five kiwi species ) . <p> Morton says DoC is " throwing the kitchen sink " at predators in areas still frequented by the few hundred remaining orange-fronted parakeet . Those places are the Hawdon , Andrews and Poulter Valleys , as well as the south branch of the Hurunui Valley in Lake Sumner Forest Park .
