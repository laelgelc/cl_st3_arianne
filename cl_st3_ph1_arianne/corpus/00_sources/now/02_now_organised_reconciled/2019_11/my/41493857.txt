<p> In a warming world , the upper echelons of the cruise industry are focusing on lower temperatures . No , they are not the travel industry 's green pioneers ( though new ships pollute far less than their predecessors ) . Instead , they are building ships for Antarctica , the Arctic and anywhere that glacial ice , rather than tropical heat , is the main attraction . <p> Unlike the first generation of icebreakers -- mostly bare-bone expedition ships and ageing Russian research vessels with renovated interiors -- and the handful of pioneering expedition ships that started to break the mould in recent years , this new breed of polar ships is smaller , nimbler and far more comfortable . <p> " People 's expectations have changed , " said Bob Simpson , the vice-president of expedition for luxury tour operator Abercrombie &amp; Kent , which charters two ships from French line Ponant for sailings in the Antarctic and Arctic seas , including the Northwest Passage between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans . On most lines , this has limited cold-weather cruising to baby boomers , but the market is broadening . <p> Shorter trips , starting at seven nights , are drawing millennials and younger families . The fact that these destinations can not be visited by land also means that cruise companies are drawing the new-to-cruise folks they so badly desire . Whereas Caribbean- and Mediterranean-bound cruise ships can be run mostly on autopilot , that is not the case on the land of ice and snow .
