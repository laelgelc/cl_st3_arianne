These birds are bold and noisy , moving in large flocks . Conservationist Harsh Vardhan says he was surprised to see one Rosy Pastor grab a pakora from a roadside seller at Tilak Nagar . " One bird ringed in Hungary was recovered nine months later in Lahore , " says Harsh Vardhan . <p> These are hardy birds , a good number of them doing long-haul flights of over 7,000 km each year , travelling from parts of Europe , where they breed on rocky surfaces , to west Asia and India , their feeding ground in winter . <p> Jaipur-based bird watcher Harkirat Singh Sangha says many of the birds that fly into @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the monsoon months , when the Rajasthan desert can offer them an insect diet . On their flight back in April , however , they take a more northern route out of the subcontinent , flying over Punjab and Haryana to avoid the desert which at that time of year can not offer them food . <p> The Rosy Pastors - called Pastors because they look down , as if scolding one , like a pastor or priest ; these birds are now often referred to as rosy starlings - are easy enough to recognize , with the pink underside and black wings , tail and head of the male . The female is duller and browner . The beak and feet are orange in colour . They are about the size of a common mynah . <p> Zoologist Antony PU of Christ University , Bangalore , says theirs is a strange jazz -- their chi-chi-chi is melodious , yet abrupt and improvisational . " It reminds one of the squeaky-wheeled children 's toys pushed back and forth vigorously en masse , " he says . <p> Rosy Pastors are unique @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ fly north-south . The pastors follow the route of migratory locusts , on which they feed . In China , in the 1980s , some farmers saw that these birds could lessen their dependence on pesticides to deal with locusts . Artificial nests were set up in farms in Xinjiang to attract the birds , and the locust population declined significantly , at no extra cost ! <p> Although they prefer insects , the pastors also eat fruit and cereal and aid in the pollination of these species . They could be " pests " themselves , feeding on jowar and bajra crops . <p> Unlike the common starling , which Sangha says has declined in numbers with the increased use of pesticides in farming , there has been no significant change in the numbers of the Rosy Pastors . Antony , however , in the article written three years ago on the website ' Citizen Matters ' , noted that the birds had made an earlier appearance in Bangalore that year .
