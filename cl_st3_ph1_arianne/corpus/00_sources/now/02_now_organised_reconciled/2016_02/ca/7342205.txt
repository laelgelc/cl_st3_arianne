<h> Framework of Smiley 's century-long trilogy mars her massive talent <p> Some Luck , the first novel in Jane Smiley 's Last Hundred Years trilogy , introduced not only the central characters , the Langdon family , but also an unusual narrative conceit : each chapter would cover a year in the life of the family via a single vignette , ultimately covering a century over three volumes . The novel , with the benefit of more incremental cultural development ( over the years 1920-1952 ) and a manageable stable of characters , was an effective , promising portrait of an Iowan farming family during a turbulent period . <p> With the second novel , Early Warning , however , the experiment began to show its inherent , unsolvable weaknesses . As the cast ballooned with spouses , children and grandchildren , multiple locations and an endlessly accelerating culture ( chronicling the years 1953-1986 ) , time and history itself began to usurp the Langdons as the central force in the series . <p> SUPPLIED PHOTO <p> Jane Smiley 's ambitious trilogy follows an Iowan farming family @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ trilogy concludes , and with the knowledge of Smiley 's immense powers as a novelist , it remains shockingly unclear why she shackled herself to this particular structure . Over the remaining years ( 1987-2020 ) , Smiley expands the Langdon clan with almost biblical rapidity and spreads them across the United States . They bumble Zelig-like into every endeavour imaginable -- Wall Street , rodeos , mental hospitals , colleges and politics , among others -- returning to the farm only to chronicle its grim decline . <p> However , though the sprawl of time , geography and characters continues unabated , the narrative oddly retains a quietness and order that is equal parts triumph and detriment . Limited to this single vignette a year , major changes in the Langdon family linger frustratingly off-camera or in the blurry distance ; characters slip away for a few chapters and return aged or dismantled ; history barges in -- the 9/11 attacks , the various financial crises -- and walks straight back out again , closing the door gently . <p> Yet the vignettes , taken individually , play to Smiley @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ scene-setting , her masterful ability to sketch a character with a casual line or two . It is that casualness , that naturalness and spontaneity -- Smiley 's greatest tool as a writer -- that is most injured by the suffocating structure . As a result the exercise , and the series as a whole , seems to smack more of solemn , workmanlike dedication to a principle than the ambition one assumes would drive a 1,300-page work . <p> The disappointment in this series is multiplied by the fact that Smiley is not only comfortable but uniquely proficient at creating novels of enormous scope and vision . Her 2000 novel Horse Heaven magnificently wrangled a massive cast -- including animal perspectives -- into a continuously entertaining , deeply meaningful 650-page story , and her Pulitzer Prize-winning masterpiece A Thousand Acres examined a similar theme -- family dynamics around the tumultuous agricultural world -- with Shakespearian intensity . <p> It would be fair to say that if any living author were capable of executing this experimental presentation with these specific themes , it would be Smiley . But it 's impossible to escape how overwhelmed the narrative is by the peculiar limitations of the form . <p> Golden Age , in its later , disconcerting chapters , wanders speculatively into the near future , and Smiley 's vision is uniformly bleak for both the Langdons and the world itself . In Smiley 's fictional world ( and likely , given the narrative , in her factual diagnosis ) climate change and its corresponding droughts loom to crush both farm and family . <p> Though this desolate future syncs with the novel 's appraisal of a century of human history , it further cements the idea that the Langdons were just hapless bystanders , creating a strong thematic conclusion -- but an ultimately unsatisfying story . <p> Doug McLean is a Winnipeg writer and musician . <h> Book review <p> You can comment on most stories on winnipegfreepress.com .
