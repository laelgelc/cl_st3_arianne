<p> When Matthew Lutton 's stage production of Joan Lindsay 's cult novel Picnic at Hanging Rock appears at the Malthouse Theatre in Melbourne next week , it will not be merely a debut , but also something of a theatre victory . <p> The title alone has the kind of mass , instant brand-recognition yearned for by any theatre 's marketing department . Lindsay 's 1967 novel about a group of white schoolgirls who disappear into a mountain on Valentine 's Day in 1900 has been of seminal influence on the Australian cultural psyche , especially since Peter Weir 's 1975 film adaptation received world wide acclaim . The unusual dramatic conflict at the heart of Picnic at Hanging Rock , in any adaptation , has always been a tension between the white colonial generation attending the picnic , and the Hanging Rock itself , representing the ancient , mysterious Australian continent that white society vainly attempts to control . <p> Unlike his Night on Bald Mountain , which represented an impressive ziggurat-like structural monolith onstage , Lutton 's Hanging Rock is not depicted so literally ; he 's characterised the mysterious landscape that obsesses , possesses and disappears the girls as a metaphoric darkness . <p> " We evoke the rock through the language , and we have a deep blackness in the theatre , which acts as the palpable otherness that keeps impacting on the story , " he says . " There are blackouts that cause girls to disappear and reappear , so there 's this configuring and reconfiguring of the girls and objects as they come closer to the rock . He says that " the constant unpacking of the colonial narrative " offered by Picnic at Hanging Rock has been a consistent interest for local audiences , to whom its metaphoric references -- intrusion into the landscape , and displacement within it -- are readily understood . <p> The character of the remote Miranda exudes a particular fascination for both the characters within the story and audiences beyond it , says Lutton , because she reconciles herself to her surroundings . " The story of Miranda is of someone who intuitively understands land , and that 's what makes her beautiful . " <p> It 's the specific contemporary challenge of Australia -- to reconcile itself to its land -- that Lutton suggests inspired the recent spate of offers to adapt Picnic 's text to production . In the era of climate change , and fear of a destabilised environment , the story takes on a new relevance .
