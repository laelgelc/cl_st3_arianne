<h> Opinion : Economic growth can help fight climate change <p> Prime Minister Justin Trudeau , left , talks after meeting with premiers at a First Ministers meeting at the Canadian Museum of Nature in Ottawa on Monday , Nov. 23 , 2015 . They meet again in Vancouver Thursday March 3 . Sean Kilpatrick / THE CANADIAN PRESS <p> On Thursday , when Prime Minister Justin Trudeau and the premiers meet in Vancouver in an attempt to coordinate efforts to reduce Canada 's greenhouse gas ( GHG ) emissions , there will be a lot of discussion about the targets that governments should set and the policy instruments best suited to achieving them . <p> They will no doubt consider ways to structure regulations and economic instruments like carbon taxes and cap-and-trade systems . Collectively , their challenge will be to come up with a plan that achieves desired emission reductions while at the same time sustaining economic growth . <p> In the course of their deliberations , our leaders would do well to look at the evidence of the past 25 years . The difference , which is the rate of reduction in emission intensity ( emissions generated per unit of economic activity ) , reflects technological progress -- improvements in energy efficiency , the substitution of less carbon intensive fuels , the adoption of less carbon intensive industrial processes and land use management practices , and the construction of more carbon efficient energy and transportation infrastructure . <p> There has been no direct relationship between higher carbon prices and reductions in emission intensity over the past 25 years . Our emissions from transportation , for instance , continued to grow even as gasoline prices reached record highs . It is also apparent that a downturn in economic activity does not necessarily lead to lower emission levels . Output in Canada 's manufacturing sector fell by 25 per cent during the 2008-09 recession , but contrary to widespread opinion , manufacturing emissions actually rose . <p> The most rapid rates of emission-intensity reduction have been achieved @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ has been capital investment in new and improved housing and building construction , machinery and equipment , and public infrastructure . The lesson is that higher rates of investment accelerate emission-intensity reductions . And , when investment declines , emissions increase . <p> Our leaders should take this experience to heart . If Canadians are to meet our current target of reducing GHG emissions 30 per cent below present levels by 2030 , then we need to triple the rate of technological progress . In turn , that will require a higher rate of capital investment and more targeted investments in more productive and carbon-efficient technologies . <p> Whether regulation , carbon tax , or cap and trade , none of these options will be effective in achieving emission reduction targets ( whatever they may be ) if they are not accompanied by measures that encourage capital investment . In fact , they are apt to do a lot of economic and environmental damage on their own if they divert money that could otherwise be invested by consumers or industry in new technologies into general government revenues or high risk technology @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ here in Canada . They would boost productivity , help stimulate economic growth and accelerate the adoption of less carbon intensive technologies , which is essential in achieving actual emission reductions . <p> Let 's not confuse the introduction of regulations , carbon taxes or cap and trade for real progress in reducing GHG emissions .
