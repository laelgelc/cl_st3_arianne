<h> Decades of aerial photos reveal how an ancient desert city got its water <p> By combining old and new aerial images , archaeologists are getting a new look at the complexities of supplying water to an ancient desert city . <p> In northern Jordan , along a wadi once called the Golden River , the ancient city of Jerash sits uneasily alongside its modern namesake . With its columned temples , Roman forum , and other monuments , it 's one of Jordan 's biggest tourist attractions and a key to the region 's long , complex history . But the expansion of modern Jerash is slowly destroying what remains of the old city . <p> Recently , a team of archaeologists from Aarhus University and M ? nster University combined historical aerial images with modern , laser-scanning surveys to map those changes and get a big-picture view of the ancient city . Previous archaeological work at Jerash has mostly focused on individual sites or on standing monumental architecture rather than more subtle or mundane aspects of city life , even though the latter is what kept its residents alive . Under the Roman Empire , it grew into a thriving urban center , with nearly 2.5 miles of walls enclosing a city of colonnaded streets and large public monuments . Under the Byzantine Empire and then the Umayyad Empire , the city 's population continued to grow . <p> Jerash was at its peak in January of 749 CE when a massive earthquake devastated the region . Most of the survivors left Jerash , and only a few buildings were rebuilt during the medieval period . As in many cities in the Middle East , growing modern urban populations live shoulder-to-shoulder with the ancient past -- and when people start vying for space , the living tend to win .
