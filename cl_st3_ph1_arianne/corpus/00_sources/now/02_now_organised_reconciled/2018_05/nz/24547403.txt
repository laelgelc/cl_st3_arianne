<h> Keith Woodford explains why methane and carbon dioxide need separate accounting systems rather than being lumped together in a single emissions trading scheme <p> A key issue for New Zealand is how to meet the Paris commitments for greenhouse gas ( GHG ) emissions . Fundamental to any analysis is the different attributes of long-lived and short-lived gases . In particular , how should methane be accounted for , and how should it be brought into any emission trading scheme ? <p> Back in 2016 , current Commissioner of the Environment Simon Upton raised the importance of placing short-lived gases in a different regulatory ' basket ' from long-lived gases . Remarkably , our rural leaders appear to have failed to pick up on the importance of this issue . <p> More than any other country in the world , NZ 's gross emissions are influenced by methane-producing ruminant animals . No other developed country has a comparable emission profile , with the arguable exception of Uruguay . <p> Accordingly , the issue of methane accounting , which is crucial to us , really does not matter to almost everyone else . This means that half of the warming caused by methane as a greenhouse gas occurs within 12.4 years , and about 94 percent within 50 years . <p> Bringing these metrics together , the current situation for methane from New Zealand sources ( largely pastoral agriculture ) is that the amount of methane entering the atmosphere is approximately equivalent to the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ then back into grass and related feeds via the carbon cycle . Every time we produce more carbon dioxide , largely from burning fossil fuels , it stacks up in the atmosphere in amongst all the carbon dioxide that is already there . It takes some hundreds and even thousands of years for the extra carbon dioxide to be dissolved in the oceans or converted into inert forms , such as new coal or oil . <p> This means that if we keep burning fossil fuels at the current rate , then the world will continue to get hotter .
