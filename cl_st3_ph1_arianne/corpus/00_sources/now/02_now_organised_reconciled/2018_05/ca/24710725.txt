<p> Research says climate change is driving hundreds of fish species north from their usual seas and some of the biggest effects will be along Canada 's Pacific coast . <p> That is likely to create new challenges for governments seeking to regulate and share catches between competing jurisdictions , said James Morley of Rutgers University in New Jersey . <p> Morley found that even if climate change is kept under two degrees , species off Canada 's west coast will move by an average of more than 200 kilometres . <p> " Even in the best-case scenario , on the west coast we still expect to see some pretty substantial shifts of species northward . " <p> Morley looked at the impact of warming seas on 636 species of marine life on the Atlantic and Pacific coasts . About one-quarter of those species have some commercial value , he said . <p> He used extensive data about where those species hang out and collected oceanographic information on currents , water temperature and the shape of sea floors . The size of Morley 's dataset makes his conclusions , published in a paper this week in the online journal publication PLOS-One , more detailed and specific . <p> He concludes that with no more than two degrees warming -- the goal agreed to in the Paris agreement -- fish stocks on the Pacific coast will migrate north by an average of 236 kilometres . The Atlantic side will see average movement of about 100 kilometres . <p> If the climate warms by four degrees -- the so-called business-as-usual case -- many Pacific stocks are likely to school as far as 1,500 kilometres north of the current centre of their habitat . The east coast shift would be about 600 kilometres . <p> " They were pretty large changes throughout the west coast .
