<h> Protest is a school for democracy <h> Children are citizens , not mere citizens-to-be . They have political views that deserve an audience . <h> By Kei Nishiyama <p> 4 December 2018 -- 12:01am <p> " What we want is more learning in schools and less activism in schools , " Prime Minister Scott Morrison told Parliament last week . <p> His pronouncement was a reaction to a school students ' strike against government inaction on climate change all over the country . The young protestors had said : " We are school kids temporarily sacrificing our education in order to save our futures from dangerous climate change . " <p> Morrison is remiss in saying that learning in schools is better than learning in the streets . Activism and learning are complementary , not competing , ways of building young people 's skills . <p> Over the past three years , I researched the role of children in democracies . Here 's what I found . <p> Advertisement <p> First , children are not in school to be trained as future participants in democracy . They are already citizens with views that deserve an audience . Morrison 's preference for " more learning than participation " goes against this principle . <p> Second , activism is learning in action . Protesting challenges students to be creative in finding ways to communicate their ideas . Remember Emma Gonzalez ? It was one of the most powerful speeches in recent history . <p> Taking a political stand also teaches students to respond to criticisms -- a real-world skill they will need throughout life . Her Facebook video of the protest has been viewed more than 13 million times . <p> Young people on the streets are not just ranting . They are building their future . They are taking action because adults are not doing enough . <p> This week , we will see more students protesting not only against climate change but also against Morrison .
