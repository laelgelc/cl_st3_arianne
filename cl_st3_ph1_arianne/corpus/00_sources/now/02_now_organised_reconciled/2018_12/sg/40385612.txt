<p> LELYSTAD , NETHERLANDS ( AFP ) - Dutch ranger Andre Donker sighs as he looks out at the rippling grey waters of the Markermeer , one of Europe 's largest freshwater lakes . <p> " Once upon a time it was teeming with fish here , " he says . <p> This vast 700 sq km expanse of water , which regulates the level of water in the rest of the Netherlands , had until recently been nothing more than a cloudy mass devoid of aquatic life . <p> Now , the hope is that a new artificial archipelago of five islands will bring nature back to the area via a typically ambitious engineering project for a low-lying country that has battled the sea for centuries . <p> It is " one of the largest rewilding operations in Europe " , says Mr Donker . <p> Standing on a wooden bridge over a pond in the middle of experimental plots of different kinds of reeds , he says he has been able to see the first signs of increasing biodiversity . <h> ' WE HAD TO INTERVENE ' <p> The lake was once part of the Zuiderzee , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ which closed off a huge expanse of water to keep out the North Sea and combat flooding . <p> Vital in a country where 26 per cent of the land is below sea level , the scheme created an inland lake and polders , land reclaimed from the sea , but at a cost to the environment . <p> Over the subsequent decades , sediment used to create a dyke separating the Markermeer from a neighbouring body of water , the Ijsselmeer , washed away and sank to the bottom of the lake . <p> " We had to intervene , " says Mr Donker , wearing a woollen hat to brave the storms from the North Sea . <h> COMBATING VULNERABILITY <p> The solution was a bold one , in keeping with a country whose people like to boast that " God created the world , but the Dutch created the Netherlands " . <p> Eight kilometres from the port of Lelystad , the ranger walks down the side of an artificial sand dune .
