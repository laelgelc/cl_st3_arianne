<h> OPINION : Climate ambition and common sense <h> Renato Redentor Constantino and Steven Herz <p> With scientists issuing increasingly dire warnings about the impending dangers of climate change , the diplomats assembling in Katowice , Poland to decide how to implement the Paris Agreement face a critical challenge . They must persuade countries to substantially increase their actions to keep climate change within relatively safe limits , as they pledged in Paris . <p> Too often , countries have resisted taking these steps because they see climate change as a " tragedy of the commons " problem , in which the costs are borne by countries that act , while the benefits are shared by future generations and citizens of far-off lands . The result is predictable : countries do much less than is needed , leaving global goals far out of reach . <p> But this understanding is hopelessly out of date . <p> After years of technological and policy innovation , climate solutions are now often cheaper and provide greater economic returns than higher-carbon alternatives . As a result , a large and growing portion of the actions needed to contain climate change are " socially @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the countries that take them , even before climate impacts are considered . <p> We may call these climate actions , but they are really just smart policies and sound investments . The climate benefits are an added bonus . <p> Opportunities to increase social welfare while reducing emissions are readily available in all countries . Eliminating wasteful fossil fuel subsidies could reduce fossil fuel emissions by over a fifth , while more than halving deaths related to fossil-fuel combustion and freeing up almost US$3 trillion in public revenue that could be used to fund badly needed investments in education , health , and infrastructure . Revitalizing degraded forests and planting new ones can capture enormous amounts of carbon while generating local benefits that exceed their costs 20-30 fold . Restoring farmlands , shifting to more sustainable agricultural practices and reducing food waste will improve food security , create millions of jobs , and generate trillions of dollars in surplus benefits , while providing as much as a third of the necessary emissions reductions . The list goes on . <p> All of these actions are in countries ' self-interest @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ emissions . <p> Truly dangerous climate impacts can not be avoided unless countries can capture as many of these socially beneficial opportunities as possible , as quickly as possible . Yet while seizing these opportunities is critical to the Paris Agreement 's success , the ongoing work to implement the Agreement has not adequately addressed them . <p> The diplomats meeting in Katowice have two critical opportunities to encourage countries to seize these opportunities . First , they will conclude the ear-long " Talanoa Dialogue " , which is considering recommendations on how to quickly scale up mitigation efforts . They will also write the " rulebook " for implementing the Paris Agreement , which will govern issues such as the information Parties will include in their pledges , how Parties will report on meeting those pledges and how collective progress will be assessed through " global stocktakes " . <p> The Talanoa Dialogue should highlight the vast potential of these socially beneficial opportunities , and promote them as a priority source of increased ambition . In so doing , it would expand countries ' understanding of what they can @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ expectation that countries will try to take those actions that are in their interests for non-climate reasons as part of their contribution to the global effort . <p> Meanwhile , the rulebook should reinforce this expectation by encouraging countries to share how they are capturing these opportunities in their national pledges and periodic reporting , and ensuring that this information informs the global stocktakes . This would both promote understanding of what the reporting country is doing , and help other countries learn from their experiences . <p> Opponents of climate progress may object that this is too prescriptive . But given the urgency of the challenge and the grave consequences of responding inadequately , it hardly seems onerous to ask countries to try to do what is in their own interest anyway .
