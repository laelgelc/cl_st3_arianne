( Photo by John Gunn ) <p> By John Gunn <p> Abandoned mine sites represent problems we are all too familiar with when dealing with " common rooms " and " shared kitchens " -- " Who is going to clean up this mess ? " and " Who is going to pay for the damages ? " <p> Public lands have the same problem when people dump trash in the bush to avoid paying landfill fees . However cleaning up industrial " brown fields " , like the mercury-laden sediments of an abandoned industry that caused such suffering for an Indigenous community on the English Wabigoon River near Dryden , is a far bigger problem than litter . <p> In the mining sector alone , there are more than 5,000 abandoned sites where the taxpayer is on the hook for the cleanup , ecosystem repairs and dealing with the downstream community miseries . <p> Kam Kotia 's mine site rehabilitation effort in Timmins is probably the largest of these efforts in Ontario ( with about $75 million spent to date @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ surprises " have not been dealt with , including an abandoned gold mine site ( above photo ) south of Sudbury , where arsenic is steadily seeping into Long Lake , forcing several adjacent residents to use bottled water . <p> This mine was closed in 1939 after extracting $63 million of gold ( in today 's dollars ) , but 100 years later , taxpayers are stuck with millions of dollars in cleanup costs ( not yet started ) . Fortunately , modern mining companies are under much more stringent regulations , so we hope not to inherit too many more such sites . <p> Sharing the global atmosphere as a dumping ground for CO2 , methane and other greenhouse gases dwarfs the challenges presented by abandoned mine sites , but is the same issue ( tragedy of the commons ) . <p> This time , it is hard to know who the culprits are when it comes to the effects of climate change : Who trashed the air we breathe ? Or who triggered the extreme climate events we suffer ? Who is responsible for increases in asthma @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ insurance costs . It is even harder to find who to blame when the smog and damages to the atmosphere are caused by wildfires , or other so-called " natural processes " ( thawing permafrost , greater decomposition during warm extended summers ) . <p> However , lately it seems that we are finally starting to connect the dots , and beginning to accept that humans caused these problems and now we need to pay the bill ( Note : 2018 will probably be one of the most expensive wildfire years ever ) . <p> Well , who should pay ? Clearly if we can identify the culprits ( illegal dumpers , polluting companies , inefficient users of fossil fuels ) , they should pay first ( polluter pays ) . But as citizens who already are familiar with covering the costs of shared services ( hospitals , highways , schools , etc. ) , and know the wisdom of investing in our own our future ( e.g. home and auto insurance , RSPs , etc. ) the answer is obvious . <p> Is there a silver lining ? For @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ a party , you might actually find change beneath the cushions or be able to cash in the leftover beer bottles . <p> Sometimes , there is actually lots of value in waste , if you are smart enough to harvest it . For example , the Greater Sudbury Utilities Innovation group is harvesting enough natural gas from the landfill site to heat 14,000 homes , and there are millions if not billions of dollars of left over valuable metals in tailing piles around Sudbury . <p> The economic benefits ( i.e. green jobs ) and health care cost savings from investments in " clean tech industry " can also be enormous as we move into the post-fossil fuel world . The alternatives are not pleasant . <p> Lately report after report has delivered the same message : time is short .
