<p> The Angus Reid polling company has released a survey that appears puzzling , at least on the face of it . <p> Among respondents who voted Conservative in the last federal election , 35 per cent believe climate change has nothing to do with human activity and a further 21 per cent think it 's a theory that has yet to be proven . In short , more than half these respondents are skeptics . <p> When you turn to Liberal and NDP voters , however , the numbers reverse . Among Liberals , 81 per cent believe climate change is real and human-caused . Among New Democrats , 85 per cent agree with that view , and almost no one in either group thinks climate change is an unproven theory . <p> I call this puzzling because we 're talking about a matter that lies in the realm of science , not politics . Admittedly , this is not the first time controversy has swirled around a scientific thesis . <p> In the 1600s , the Italian astronomer Galileo was forced by the Catholic church to abandon his claim that the Earth orbits the sun . He @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ some cardinals refused to look through his telescope , for fear he might be right ( though this could be as much legend as fact ) . <p> And during the modern era , the theory of evolution has been rejected in certain quarters . <p> Yet those are both instances where the opposition was based on largely religious grounds . Setting them aside , I ca n't think of another scientific claim provoking such marked disagreement along strictly political lines . <p> It 's no secret that political preferences determine how voters approach various matters of public policy . <p> Climate change is not , however , any sort of policy . It 's a scientific theory based on meteorological observation and temperature measurements . So why the breakdown along political lines ? <p> The answer I think is that to date , scientific advances have generally created benefits that outweighed their costs . I 'm thinking in particular of medical discoveries , such as antibiotics and MRI machines , but numerous other fields of research can make this claim . <p> However , acceptance of climate change entails @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Their benefits are uncertain and lie well in the future , while their costs are immediate and far-reaching . <p> It 's natural that voters who are comfortable with more government regulation , higher public spending and appeals to supra-national co-ordination can buy into these prospects . They tend to support such policies in any case . <p> Likewise , it 's understandable that individuals who dislike this prospect are suspicious of the science that gave rise to it . They fear the economic consequences that come with it . <p> Nevertheless , there is a warning here for both sides . While the majority of voters might accept global warming as a reality , there are limits to how far and how fast governments can go . French President Emmanuel Macron discovered that last week , when he had to suspend a new carbon tax on gasoline after violent protests in Paris turned into nationwide uproar . <p> Equally , denying that the theory lacks a factual basis invites derision , even if more catastrophic prognostications come up short . In a matter with existential implications , moderation is essential on both sides of the issue .
