<p> Warmer winters from climate change could hit British blackcurrants by delaying the growing seasons and reducing yields . <p> Like many fruit crops , blackcurrants required a period of chilling over winter before they start to grow in spring . This reduces the risk of frost damage to new buds and ensures that buds burst rapidly and flower together when pollinators are abundant . <p> Speaking at the British Ecological Society 's annual meeting in Birmingham , a research group based at the James Hutton Institute discussed how milder winters could threaten the blackcurrant 's cycle , predicting crops to flower later in the year causing reduced yields , and a reduced plant lifespan . <p> " Blackcurrants have particularly high chill requirements and so are already seeing the effects of milder winters " , said Dr Katharine Preedy from Biomathematics and Statistics Scotland . <p> " Blackcurrants are like the canary in the mine . If we can understand what they need in a changing climate , we can apply our knowledge to similar crops like blueberries , cherries , apples and plums , " Preedy added . <p> A key crop worth about ? 10 million @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ processed as an ingredient and juice for major brands like Ribena , which takes around 90 per cent of the crop . <p> Many farmers coordinate processing with apple producers in shared facilities , and a delayed blackcurrant season may force them to harvest unripe fruit of poorer quality or they might miss the chance to process the fruit at all . <p> To explore the relationship between chilling period and bud opening , ecologists carried out controlled temperature experiments ( at temperatures ranging from -4 to +8 ?
