<p> An episode of extreme global warming that left ocean animals unable to breathe caused the biggest mass extinction in Earth 's history , research has shown . <p> The extinction event at the end of the Permian period 252 million years ago wiped out 96% of all marine species and 70% of land-dwelling vertebrates . <p> Scientists have linked what has become known as the " Great Dying " with a series of massive volcanic eruptions in Siberia that filled the atmosphere with greenhouse gas . <p> But precisely what made the oceans so inhospitable to life had remained an unanswered question until now . The new study , reported in the journal Science , suggests that as temperatures soared the warmer water could not hold enough oxygen for most marine creatures to survive . <p> Lessons from the Great Dying have major implications for the fate of today 's warming world , say the US scientists . <p> If greenhouse gas emissions continue unchecked , ocean warming could reach 20% of the level experienced in the late Permian by 2100 , they point out . <p> By the year 2300 it could reach between 35% and 50% @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Penn , who is a doctoral student at the University of Washington , explained : " This study highlights the potential for a mass extinction arising from a similar mechanism under anthropogenic human caused climate change . " <p> Before the Siberian eruptions created a greenhouse-gas planet , the Earth 's oceans had temperatures and oxygen levels similar to those of today . <p> In a series of computer simulations , the scientists raised greenhouse gases to match conditions during the Great Dying , causing surface ocean temperatures to increase by around 10C . <p> The model triggered dramatic changes in the oceans , which lost around 80% of their oxygen . <p> Roughly half of the ocean floor , mostly at deeper depths , became completely devoid of the life-sustaining gas . <p> The researchers studied published data on 61 modern marine species including crustaceans , fish , shellfish , corals and sharks , to see how well they could tolerate such conditions . <p> These findings were incorporated into the model to produce an extinction " map " . <p> " Very few marine organisms stayed in the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ flee or perish , " said co-author Dr Curtis Deutsch , also from the University of Washington . <p> The simulation showed that the hardest-hit species were those found far from the tropics and most sensitive to oxygen loss . <p> Data from the fossil record confirmed that a similar extinction pattern was seen during the Great Dying . Tropical species already adapted to warm , low-oxygen conditions were better able to find a new home elsewhere . But no such escape route existed for those adapted to cold , oxygen-rich environments . <p> Previously , experts had been undecided about whether lack of oxygen , heat stress , high acidity or poisoning chemicals wiped out life in the oceans at the end of the Permian period . <p> " This is the first time that we have made a mechanistic prediction about what caused the extinction that can be directly tested with the fossil record , which then allows us to make predictions about the causes of extinction in the future , " said Mr Penn .
