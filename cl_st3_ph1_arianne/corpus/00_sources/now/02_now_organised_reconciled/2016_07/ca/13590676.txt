<p> As electricity consumers in Alberta how do we sort the ' wheat ' from the ' chaff ' on power purchasing agreements ? <p> We need to understand some basic facts on electricity generation re-structuring , on emissions reductions strategies and on the workings of the Alberta power market . As seen from the current controversy over the wording of a specific clause it is easy to understand the confusion when the preamble to a PPA runs to 45 pages , has 185 clauses , and over 18,000 words , and an individual PPA for a generating station has 21 schedules that detail its terms and conditions . <p> So where did these constructs come from ? <p> The intent of the Power Purchase Arrangements ( PPAs ) was to transition Alberta 's regulated electricity sector to an open market system . Prior to 2000 , generating facilities were constructed under the regulated era and essentially belonged to the electricity consumers of Alberta . The intent was to attract new market participants to Alberta 's de-regulated market through sale by auction of the ' right to offer @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ auctions were paid to the Balancing Pool , a government agency set up to administer the PPAs and to distribute the funds to consumers as it has done for the past 16 years . <p> So why all the concern now ? <p> The buyer of the PPA was to take on the risk of transacting in the new de-regulated market through to the end of its life , most to 2020 and was responsible for paying agreed costs to the owner of the generating station . Some of these costs in the PPAs were indexed to cost escalation and other extraordinary costs could be flowed through to the buyers . From the electricity consumer perspective the key was the operational revenue and cost risks belonged to the PPA buyer -- not to us . But in 2016 times get a little tough and the buyers want ' out of the deal ! Owners can offset these requirements through implementation of reduction strategies at the generating stations , by acquiring offsets through investments in low emissions projects such as wind farms , or paying $15/ton . Known as SGER ( Specified Gas Emitters Regulation ) , it has been one of the most innovative and effective carbon reduction programs in Canada . <p> All major emitters engaged in the development of the Framework and signed on . The SGER regulations had built-in five-year reviews and a process for continuous input . In the 2013 Five-Year Review , Enmax 's comments in Support for the Alberta Framework showed full commitment , stating it " has become an integral part of our investment strategy . We have made major investments in the market which are consistent with the certainty provided ... After a year of dithering by the previous government , in June 2015 , the NDP government extended the program to the end of 2017 , increased the payment option to $20/t for 2016 and $30/t for 2017 , and increased the thresholds for emissions , as per discussions with industry over the previous two years . <p> Were the impacts significant ? <p> The impacts are , at worst , modest . If the generation owner did nothing to offset the carbon emissions through offsets or by ' cleaning up his plant ' then the increase in the potential costs is from just under $2/MWh in 2014 to $6/MWh by 2017 . All of this in a market that had an average gross revenue to the PPA buyers of $67/MWh and net revenues around $30/MWh . <p> So is there really new costs to the PPA buyers ? <p> Not all PPA owners have attempted to pass on these costs . Capital Power has neutralized its exposure to GHG regulations in Alberta ... " <p> In its August 2015 report to investors , after the increase in the SGER requirements , Capital Power indicates that " Between 2016-2020 , Capital Power 's increase in compliance costs will be partly mitigated by the higher wholesale power prices directly caused by the new regulations . " <p> So how does this ' higher wholesale costs ' work ? <p> Since 2011 , the Alberta power market has an MSA ( Market Surveillance Administrator ) sanctioned mechanism that allows sellers of electricity to withhold generation to drive up prices . This ' economic withholding ' was successfully deployed over the past five years during periods of high demand or outages resulting in prices close to $1,000/MWh in some @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . Essentially , the holders of PPAs have the ability to increase prices to cover added costs , such as the potential increase in SGER . <p> Ok . So what does all that mean for the controversy ? <p> Let 's ask the hard questions -- why did the PPA buyers choose to use a potential Change in Law provision in 2016 , but not in 2007 when SGER was first enacted ? <p> And if they choose not to influence prices through ' economic withholding ' , why does this make them " more unprofitable " when they have been able to extract profits from $25 to $30/MWh for the first 15 years of the PPAs ? <p> It appears the answer is that the PPA buyers no longer want the ' risk ' they took on acquiring the PPAs and want to ' give them back ' to consumers .
