<h> The penguins have all but abandoned what was once a thriving breeding site at Halley Bay <p> Halley Bay has long been home to one of the largest emperor penguin colonies in Antarctica , second only to Coulman Island in the Ross Sea . Over the past 60 years that researchers have been observing the Halley Bay colony , between 14,300 and 23,000 pairs have flocked to the site 's sea ice to breed . But since 2016 , breeding failures have been " catastrophic " and the penguins appear to have abandoned what was once a reliable haven , according to a new study published in Antarctic Science . <p> The trend was reported by Peter Fretwell and Philip Trathan of the British Antarctic Survey , who studied high-resolution satellite images of Halley Bay , looking for clues -- like poop stains and shadows -- that point to population size . The researchers think the trouble started in 2015 , after the strongest El Ni ? o in decades began disrupting Halley Bay 's " fast ice , " or sea ice that is anchored to the shore or ocean floor . " And now , the colony at Halley Bay has largely disappeared . <p> It seems that many of the adult emperor penguins have travelled elsewhere to find more reliable breeding ground . Satellite data shows that a colony of emperor penguins at the nearby Dawson-Lambton Glacier suddenly experienced a " massive increase " in numbers starting in 2016 , the study authors write . <p> In some respects , this is good news .
