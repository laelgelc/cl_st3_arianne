<h> The accident that led to the discovery of climate-change-proof coffee <h> Extreme weather could wipe out existing breeds of coffee plants in Central America . But researchers are making genetic improvements . <p> The round plastic container in William Solano 's hands holds the coffee blend of the future . It 's not a novel macchiato or a whole-bean medium roast from a boutique shop : it 's a new hybrid variety of coffee that might fare better in our changing climate . <h> This story is part of our May/June 2019 issue <p> " This one is called Centroamericano , " says Solano , a coffee breeder at the Tropical Agricultural Research and Higher Education Center ( CATIE ) , in Costa Rica , as he shows me a petri dish with dozens of plant embryos that look like tiny popcorn . Experts like him create hybrids by combining two genetically distant and complementary coffee strains , hoping to get the best characteristics from each parent . <p> Centroamericano was not created with climate change in mind . Its breeders were aiming for disease resistance , yield , and taste . On the night of February 6 , 2017 , at a trial site in Laos where dozens of coffee varieties were being tested , temperatures dropped so dramatically that frost blackened and severely injured most of the trees in the plot . By 7 a.m. , only three rows still survived : those with Centroamericano and two other hybrid varieties from Central America . <p> Scientists are now realizing that such hybrids might be better at withstanding the temperature extremes likely to be brought on by climate change . A 2015 study by Christian Bunn and colleagues at the International Center for Tropical Agriculture ( CIAT ) calculated that under a midrange estimate of how severe climate change will be , the global area suitable for coffee will shrink about 50% by 2050 , even as demand increases .
