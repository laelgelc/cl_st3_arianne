Scientists count the number of young corals that attach themselves to the tiles to gauge the reef 's recovery.PHOTO : ARC COE FOR CORAL REEF STUDIES/GERGELY TORDA <p> Large parts of Australia 's Great Barrier Reef are struggling to recover from damage caused by climate change , and baby corals needed to restock and rebuild the reef are in dramatic decline , a study shows . <p> Published today in the journal Nature , the study found that new corals settling on the reef declined by 89 per cent following the loss of about half of all adult shallow-water corals between 2016 and 2017 . <p> Prof Hughes and his colleagues from the ARC Centre of Excellence for Coral Reef Studies at James Cook University in Queensland found a direct correlation between the loss of adult corals and the dramatic decline in young corals . <p> Co-author Andrew Baird said : " The number of coral larvae produced each year and where they travel to before settling on a reef are vital components @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ The Great Barrier Reef , the world 's largest , comprises hundreds of different coral species . Some produce tiny larvae directly , others ( produce ) eggs and sperm in annual mass-spawning events . Ultimately , the tiny larvae settle on the reef and begin to grow , taking several years to become young adults . <p> But global warming is threatening this natural cycle . Sudden spikes in ocean temperatures can literally cook the corals , badly damaging or killing them . This leads to the images of ghostly white coral skeletons caused by bleaching events , which are triggered by marine heatwaves often lasting weeks . <p> Scientists say the gap between bleaching events is shrinking , greatly reducing the reef 's capacity to recover . <p> Prof Hughes and his team measured the decline in baby corals by counting the number of juveniles that settled on experimental tiles placed across 17 reefs last year , following the 2016 and 2017 bleaching events . They then compared these figures with measurements of young coral numbers in the years prior to 2016-17 . <p> The biggest decline in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ corals , called Acropora , that are key to the reef 's structure . " <p> He said the mix of species on the Great Barrier Reef was changing very rapidly for two reasons . <p> " One is due to the bleaching , which is very selective - we found that some species are more susceptible to extreme temperatures in 2016 and 2017 than others . <p> " The other is the radical shift in the mix of baby corals we saw in 2018 . <p> " So the reef is moving to a new configuration , with a greater proportion of the species that are resistant to bleaching , or that are capable of bouncing back the fastest .
