<p> A rifle resting on his shoulder , Tatji Arara looks despondent as he steps over the trunks of huge trees felled by timber traffickers in the heart of Brazil 's Amazon rainforest , now the scene of numerous land conflicts . <p> " Every day , we find new trees cut down . I 've never seen anything like this , " laments the 41-year-old , a leader of the Arara indigenous people in the northern state of Para . <p> He says illegal logging on Arara lands -- an area equivalent to 264,000 football fields -- has intensified since President Jair Bolsonaro came to power in January . <p> Bolsonaro , a far-right champion of agribusiness , vowed during last year 's election campaign that he would not give up " one centimeter more " of land to indigenous communities in Brazil , home to around 60% of the Amazon rainforest . <p> According to Para-based conservation group Imazon , deforestation in the Amazon increased 54% in January -- the first month Bolsonaro was in office -- as compared with a year earlier . <p> Some 37% of the devastated areas are in Para . <p> The @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ has been under government protection since 1991 . <p> But there are fears that could change under Bolsonaro , a climate change sceptic . <p> " Bolsonaro is poisoning the spirit of the people .
