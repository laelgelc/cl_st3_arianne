<h> Agriculture 's wicked problem <p> A wicked problem : that 's how the chief science advisor to the Ministry for Primary Industries describes the issue of greenhouse gas ( GHG ) emissions as they affect agriculture . <p> Speaking at the NZ Agricultural Climate Change conference last week in Palmerston North , Dr John Roche said climate change is a wicked problem lacking a simple solution . <p> He told 200 scientists , farmers , policy makers and rural professionals that while there are levers you can pull to solve the problem , doing so often has unintended , unwanted consequences elsewhere in the system . <p> " There is not an easy solution . These are political , scientific , social , and ethical problems . All of these combine to be a complex problem without one simple solution , " he said . <p> Roche says a mish-mash of information is available on climate change , some from people not qualified to speak on the subject . This confusion makes it hard for farmers to get to grips with the issue . And social media have advantages and disadvantages in the climate change debate . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ primarily farming based , with about 50% of our GHG emissions coming from agriculture , Roche says . <p> " In most developed countries agriculture would generate closer to 10% of their emissions . Their major GHGs tend to be from industry or transport , for which there are solutions , e.g. alternative sources of power or electric vehicles , etc , " he said . <p> " These are not simple solutions but they are solutions . Whereas with our agricultural emissions , especially methane from enteric rumen fermentation , we are dealing with 60 million years of evolution . " <p> This is a difficult problem because of the near impossible task of trying to fight nature . NZ scientists are trying to find solutions and are making progress on various fronts such as developing better genetics , breeding forages that reduce methane emissions , and vaccines and feed supplements that can make a difference . <p> Roche says despite all the good work NZ is doing it still tends to give itself a bad rap . <p> " I call it self-flagellation , whipping ourselves . We @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ in a lovely peaceful country free from political and social turmoil , " Roche says . <p> " But we often do n't celebrate our successes and tend to be very critical of ourselves . Often we do n't put that criticism in the context of the wider world and do n't celebrate the things we do very well , such as producing milk and meat with relatively low carbon footprints . <p> " We are not as bad as we think we are and we need to celebrate that and continue to do good work to improve further , " he says . <p> Do n't villify dairy , meat <p> Roche says in NZ and other countries some people vilify meat and dairy products . <p> Various interest groups would like us to believe that these products are bad for us and that their production harms the environment . <p> " NZ ca n't ignore the fact that all food production has effects on the environment and as a country we are working hard to correct any negative effects . But people need to look at the wholesome , high quality food we produce and eat a balanced diet . " <p> As for dealing with agricultural GHG emissions , Roche insists there are no silver bullets coming soon . But several technologies are being worked on and if they come to fruition they could be change agents . <p> He says NZ research into mitigating the ruminant animal problem is world class and capable of yielding a striking breakthrough .
