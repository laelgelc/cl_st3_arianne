<h> Telling a story - the global rise of artisan craft chocolate <p> As Easter approaches and we reach for that chocolate bunny , chocolate researcher Kristy Leissle looks at the explosion in craft chocolate . <p> Globally , chocolate and its source , cocoa , are in a moment of dynamism and change . <p> In some cases it 's for the worse , as the industry faces the realities of climate change . Other changes , however , are for the better , as cocoa producers , chocolate makers , researchers and even retailers offer creative new ways to interact with this beloved food and the people who bring it to us . <p> Kristy Leissle has been researching and writing about all things chocolate for 15 years , from its politics and geography to its history and culture . <p> Here are three trendsshe has been following that are reshaping the industry . <p> One of the biggest changes has been the rise of craft chocolate . <p> For most of the 20th century , a few major brands such as Hershey , Mars , Cadbury and Nestle dominated the market . <p> That began to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Berger opened its doors in Berkeley , California , and became the first new bean-to-bar maker in decades . <p> To see how much things have changed , look no further than the Northwest Chocolate Festival , held every autumn in Seattle , which recently celebrated its 10th year . When Leissle became education director for the festival in 2010 , she knew maybe a dozen craft chocolate makers to invite . In 2013 , the last year Leissle held the role , she surveyed the market and was able to locate 37 bean-to-bar makers operating commercially in the US . <p> Today , there are more than 200 . <p> In comparison , New Zealand is where Cadbury ( the company 's New Zealand roots were in Dunedin ) and Nestle dominated the market for decades , and the only homegrown alternative was Whittakers Chocolate Company which made the peanut slab and only started making 250g blocks of chocolate in 1990 . <p> It was in the 1990s when the first ' ' craft ' ' chocolate makers began in New Zealand with Devonport Chocolates starting in Auckland in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ in Kerikeri in 1998 . <p> But it was in the 2000s when craft chocolate making began to take off with figures now showing 120 chocolate makers in the country , many of them craft chocolatiers such as Hogarth Craft Chocolate in Nelson , Honest Chocolat in Matakana , The Remarkables Chocolate Co. in Auckland and Dunedin 's very own Ocho . <p> In 2017 New Zealand got its very own Chocolate Awards and last year New Zealand Chocolate Week in October was launched . <p> The NZ Food and Grocery Council has also identified chocolate as having high growth potential with consumer-ready chocolate exports already worth $81million in 2016 - 82% of that went to Australia . <p> The rise of craft chocolate has meant a true renaissance . These makers often take an artisanal approach , getting to know their materials well in this case , cocoa and sugar and shaping them carefully from bean to finished product . The results are lovingly crafted bars , many of them single origin that showcase cocoa 's natural flavour range . <p> With this artisanal market shift has come rising @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ analysis of the craft market shows that consumers now want more than just a piece of chocolate . They expect makers to also share a story , from who grew the beans to the flavour profile of the finished product . <p> But this surge in craft chocolate makers and stories can also create confusion at the grocery store . <p> A visit to a typical sweets section these days reveals dozens of new and attractive chocolate bars stretching down the aisle , bearing a dizzying array of labels : Fairtrade , direct trade , Rainforest Alliance , IMO Fair for Life , bean to bar , raw , handmade , craft and artisan to name just a few . <p> Is there any real difference among these claims , or are they all simply marketing hype ? And what does it really mean to be artisanal ? <p> By and large , labels do one of two things : say something about a chocolate maker 's ethics or its process . <p> Fairtrade , for example , sets a price floor for cocoa . An artisan once was a person who spent long years as apprentice to a master , training in a craft , and ' ' graduated ' ' only when that master said the trainee was ready . <p> Opportunities for apprenticeships vanished in the US because the few chocolate companies that dominated the 20th century guarded their manufacturing secrets so closely . Furthermore , her findings suggested that consumers translate passion into good flavour . So the word ' ' artisan ' ' seems to sell a delicious chocolate-eating experience , which may or may not be true . <p> Leissle also concluded that terms like artisan are meant to do more than sell a product . The term , and the storytelling that accompanies it , is intended to educate consumers about what makes this chocolate different from mass-produced candies . <p> For consumers eager to get a story with their chocolate , these labels provide plenty of information . Leissle 's advice is not to try to learn every story but to follow the ones that you find compelling .
