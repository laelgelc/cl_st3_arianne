Here 's what we can do about it <p> Some regions across Canada are seeing an increase in floods due to heavier and more frequent rainfall . It 's high time that governments at all levels figure out how to mitigate the costly impact , experts say . <h> Social Sharing <h> It 's high time that governments at all levels figure out how to mitigate the costly impact , experts say <p> Flood waters from the Ottawa River cover a street in Gatineau , Que . ( Justin Tang/The Canadian Press ) <p> For most people , the early days of spring conjure pleasant thoughts : blue skies , singing birds , sunshine , warmth and blooming flowers . But for many , the spring thaw is a time of dread . It 's a time of floods . <p> Over the past few weeks , communities in New Brunswick , Ontario and Quebec have made headlines as severe flooding forced many people to abandon their homes . <p> The spring flood season is n't anything new to Canada : the Red River consistently @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Alberta . <p> But what 's different , experts say , is that spring flooding is becoming more common and will likely get even more severe in some parts of the country . <p> " The extreme rain , precipitation events that are occurring now are going to continue and indeed are going to get worse going forward for sure , " said Blair Feltmate , head of the Intact Centre on Climate Adaptation at the University of Waterloo . <p> " Because a certain proportion of that which is driving these events is climate change itself . So we 're getting more storms of greater magnitude , of greater intensity and higher volumes of water coming down over shorter periods of time . " <p> But there are other factors to consider , including how we 're using our land . <p> In cities , for example , all that concrete helps prevent water from being absorbed quickly . <p> Rural areas may look greener , but land used for agriculture has changed the landscape and prevented natural vegetation that might have otherwise absorbed melting snow and rainwater . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Climate Adaptation at the University of Waterloo , works with groups across the country on adapting to climate change . ( uwaterloo.ca ) <p> " Here in Ontario -- and the trend is the same right across Canada -- we 've lost 73 per cent of the original , natural infrastructure that was here 100 years ago : forests , fields , wetlands , marshes , " Feltmate said . " That 's disappeared . That 's either been paved over or turned into agricultural development such that when the big storms hit , water is n't absorbed quickly on these new surfaces . " <p> David Phillips , senior climatologist at Environment and Climate Change Canada , said that while Canada has experienced these kinds of floods before , for some areas , it 's been the level of extended cold that 's made all the difference . <p> In Ottawa , for example , the city has had six months that have been colder than usual . For example , along riverbanks , it 's wiser to keep trees , bushes and grasses that have been there historically to hold back the water rather than to clear it for development . <p> Residents in low-lying areas of Rigaud , Que. , are dealing with floods for the second year in a row .
