<p> Before spring began , Canadian political leaders were already debating how to respond to the profound challenge of climate change . Now , they 're doing it while neighbourhoods in Ontario , Quebec and New Brunswick are under water . <p> In theory , those rising waters ought to bring a new sense of urgency to the debate . But this spring 's floods still seem unlikely to wash away the stark partisan differences that have emerged over climate policy in this country . <p> The flooding does at least put climate change front and centre . An abstract threat has been made tangible . A problem for the future is suddenly immediate and apparent . <p> And beyond the expressions of concern for the people directly affected , the conversation has quickly moved to necessary and important questions about adapting to climate change . Is this the new normal ? Among those who support Justin Trudeau 's Liberal government , 81 per cent consider it a top five issue . Eighty-six per cent of NDP supporters and 84 per cent of Green supporters agree . But only 46 per cent of Conservative voters consider climate change a top-five concern . <p> That roughly lines up with an earlier finding from Abacus on Canadians ' belief in global warming . Seventy-four per cent of Liberal and NDP supporters said there is " conclusive " evidence that the planet is getting warmer . Just 49 per cent of Conservative supporters agreed . <p> A left/right split on climate change is not unique to Canada and it 's less pronounced here than in the United States .
