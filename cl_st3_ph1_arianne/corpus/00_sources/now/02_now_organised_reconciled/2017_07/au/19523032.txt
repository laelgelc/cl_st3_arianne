<h> Smart grid to address balance of power <p> This article featured in Swinburne 's 2017 ' Research Impact ' magazine , produced in association with Nature Publishing Group . <p> A Swinburne researcher is part of a team that has come up with a plan to upgrade Australia 's electricity network and save taxpayers billions of dollars . <p> The ageing electricity grid is one of the largest pieces of infrastructure in the country . But , it was built in an era when electricity flowed one way -- from power stations to the consumer . <p> The system is no longer linear , as homes and business-owners increasingly install solar panels and supplement their network supply . Some feed excess electricity back into the grid , creating a two-way flow of electrons . <p> Distinguished Professor Qing-Long Han , Swinburne 's Pro Vice-Chancellor ( Research Quality ) says the grid sometimes struggles with this intermittent , two-way supply and demand . " <p> He says using a probabilistic approach allowed them to get around the challenges of intermittent supply . But Professor Han says their system will be more cost-effective and would allow increased use of renewable energy . This would prevent the need for new power stations , realising further savings , and help to address climate change . <p> Peter Wolfs , one of Professor Han 's collaborators from Central Queensland University , says even if the system is not adopted by the grid , the system has application on smaller scales . " Universities have electricity bills in the millions of dollars each year . With advanced networked control and battery storage , they could make better decisions about when to sell their electricity into the grid . "
