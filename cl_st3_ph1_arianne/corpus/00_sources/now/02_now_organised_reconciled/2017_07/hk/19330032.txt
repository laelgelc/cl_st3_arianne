<p> China has entered the game in fighting climate change and with support of the government 's ambitious carbon reduction plan , the private sector has become more active and motivated to bring low-carbon living into Chinese cities . <p> Given 6% of China 's carbon emissions1 came from the transportation sector , and among that a large proportion was from roadside transportation , developing a market for low-carbon and less polluting transportation has become both a priority and an immense opportunity . <p> Enter the bike-sharing industry - perhaps most phenomenal , economically and social disruptive example of China 's low-carbon economy boom . In just half a year 's time , millionsof colourful , GPS tracked bikes have hit the streets of more than 50 major cities across 20 provinces in China . The two major market players , Ofo and Mobike are said to be valued at US$1 Billion , reflecting the interests of some deep-pocketed investors like Tencent and Temasek . <p> What makes bike-sharing companies so disruptive is that they are highly technology-driven . Taking advantage of the high connectivity and smartphone penetration of Chinese consumers in Chinese cities , and the adaptability of mobile payment ( through platforms like Alipay and WeChat Pay ) , uptake has been quick as urban dwellers are happy to try new options that cope with the traffic congestion for practically no cost . For most bike-sharing apps , the first 30 or 40 minutes is free , and users may pay as little as RMB1-2 for the next hour . With the acute demand and immense population in cities , bike-sharing companies are able to collect capital and scale up very rapidly by collecting a deposit of RMB 100-300 . <p> Some innovative practices to engage riders and help control the flow of the bicycles include offering the option to check if a nearby bike is in good condition on the app , without having to walk to the bike itself . No official data is available yet regarding achieved carbon reduction , but in an April 2017 ( Chinese only ) report , Mobike claims through their survey and operation data that in some cities the scheme has reduced private car use by as much as 50% and unlicensed taxi use by 70% . <p> This whole new economy is not without its downsides , however . Without pressure from the government , investors and users , the well-intended scheme could turn into an environmental disaster . One must bear in mind that the bike-sharing model is not currently a circular economy . Unlike Uber , bike-sharing companies produce ( if not overproduce to win the upper hand in the market ) , own and transport/ move their own bicycles . They are also responsible for maintenance and disposal , and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ ( from the GPS parts ) , not to mention end-of-life disposal , remains an unsolved and overlooked problem . Also , due to ongoing cut throat competition , there is already an oversupply of bicycles . Some cities are already dealing with the headache of having bicycles piled up on street corners , taking up private space , given that users can simply park a bike anywhere . <p> Bike riding also increases exposure to the polluted air and smog -- an ever present concerns for Chinese citizens . It is unclear how the government or bike-sharing companies are addressing this problem , but it is their responsibility if they want to brand themselves as ambassadors of smart , green lifestyles . The Mobike report also shared that 70% of their users are younger than 50 years old -- which is fitting for bike-sharing clientele . Users need to be physically fit enough to ride a bike , own a smartphone and know how to use the mobile app to rent a bike .
