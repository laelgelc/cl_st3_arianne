The David Suzuki Foundation began its Butterflyway Project earlier this year , recruiting more than 150 residents in five Canadian cities as the first Butterflyway Rangers . <p> These volunteers learned how to help local pollinators flourish . They returned to their neighbourhoods with a mission : create a local Butterflyway by planting at least a dozen pollinator patches filled with native wildflowers that support these essential critters . <h> Butterflyways blooming Canada-wide <p> What happened next is inspiring . Rangers in each city connected with local gardening and horticulture groups , businesses , municipal councillors and parks staff , teachers and daycares . Photo courtesy of the David Suzuki Foundation <h> Pollinating species in serious danger <p> Reproduction for about 90 per cent of flowering plant species depends on pollinators , from bees and butterflies to hummingbirds and bats . We have pollinators to thank for one of every three bites of food we eat . Sadly , threats like development , pesticides and climate change are dramatically reducing pollinator diversity and numbers . <p> A 2016 United Nations report found 40 per cent of all insect pollinators worldwide are under threat . More than 50 butterfly and moth species and a quarter of all bumblebee species in North America are threatened , and six species of native bees await protection under Canada 's Species at Risk Act . <p> Although Canada 's more than 300 butterfly species are n't as diligent pollinators as other species , they play other essential ecological roles , like becoming bird food . The plight of perhaps the most iconic butterfly in North America , the monarch , is well documented .
