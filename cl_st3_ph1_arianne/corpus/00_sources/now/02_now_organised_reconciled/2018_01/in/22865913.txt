RakeshJan 22 , 2018 00:00 IST <p> Peter Baker <p> Bangalore : Climate change is brewing a crisis for coffee , which could join the cocoa as an endangered crop in 50 years unless governments wake up and , well , smell the coffee , a global expert said on Friday . <p> " Climate change has already had a big impact on coffee-growing , ( with ) droughts and floods affecting flowering and fruit-setting , " Peter Baker , scientific director with the UK-based agricultural research enterprise Climate Edge , told The Telegraph . <p> Experts say that coffee is a delicate plant that requires a particular amount of rain at a specific time of the year , and is therefore sensitive to even small changes in the climate . <p> But what 's worsened matters and triggered a vicious cycle , Baker said , are global efforts to avert a price rise by constantly increasing the plantation area through deforestation - itself a key cause of climate change . <p> " We should @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ cost should be going to reduce deforestation . Instead , there is massive deforestation to add new coffee plantations , which is only contributing to more global warming , " Baker said on the sidelines of the India International Coffee Festival 2018 . <p> " Between 100,000 and 150,000 hectares of new coffee plantations are cropping up every year ( globally ) . This almost translates into one football pitch of forest cleared every five to ten minutes . " <p> Baker said the planters invest in the new plantations the money earned by selling the forest wood , " thereby increasing the yield , which keeps prices low " . <p> His grouse is that governments are neither controlling deforestation nor trying to solve coffee growers ' problems . " There 's a lack of global leadership that should be taking a lot more interest in this sector , " he said . <p> His solution : " The farmers ( planters ) need to get more organised and even be militant . At the moment , the farmers are very independent and do n't aggregate well . " @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Board of India , agreed that climate change was affecting India 's coffee plantations . <p> " The southwest monsoon , which used to come on June 1 , arrives a week late . Coffee plants need timely showers . Nor do we get sufficient summer rain nowadays , " he said . " Climate change is spreading diseases and pests too . " <p> Indian scientists have developed more resistant strains of coffee plants but few farmers are ready to switch from their traditional arabica and robusta varieties just yet . <p> Every new coffee plant needs three to four years before it starts yielding the crop annually for the rest of its productive life . So , the planters want to wait till their current plants reach the end of their productive age , which is 10 to 15 years . <p> Some Indian coffee farmers have switched completely to pepper , which commands a higher market price .
