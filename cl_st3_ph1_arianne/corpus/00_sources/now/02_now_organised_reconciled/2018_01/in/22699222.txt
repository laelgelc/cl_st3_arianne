<h> Poisoned by air and water <p> There is a breathless quality about Sunita Narain 's prose . It is not on account of the fact that India is choking to death on its polluted air , a hazard that Narain examines with insight . ( Two and a half million pollution-related deaths were recorded in 2015 , says The Lancet . ) The breathlessness of the prose mirrors both the indignation and exasperation that Narain , one of India 's noted environmentalists , has experienced in the course of her many battles to stall the slide towards an ecological catastrophe . Here , she peels off the layers of these conflicts , forcing readers to confront the many culpabilities - those of corporations , of governments , and their own - that are pushing the country towards a dystopian future . <p> Narain 's adversaries are numerous ; they are also resourceful . The book , pertinently , opens with a reference to a conference organized to announce that Tata Motors , affiliated to one of India 's most respectable business houses , had served a defamation suit worth @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ for its report on the toxins released by diesel . Narain uses this incident to highlight the oscillations in the fate of her decades-old campaign against air pollution . CSE 's investigations and reports led to a brutal retaliation from the aggrieved automobile industry that was manifest in the form of legal notices , personal vilification and even disinformation campaigns , till the Supreme Court intervened to improve fuel and emission standards and bring about the historic , but contested , switch to CNG . Narain shows that the key to the battle over air lies in resilience and in providing alternative solutions . But opposition from those with vested interests is , like some malignancy , bound to return . For instance , in 2017 , the automobile industry - its representatives can include influential former ministers-lawyers - resisted the move to switch over to BS-IV emission norms . So the only option open for embattled environmentalists is to sustain the pressure , usually through the good offices of the court . The spoils of victory , if there are any , can also be crumb-like . Delhi 's affluent classes remain vehemently opposed to lowering the number of private vehicles ; the war against diesel is far from over as well . <p> This pattern , of setbacks and small victories , is repeated in Narain 's recounting of the conflicts over pesticides , carbonated drinks , climate and conservation . Of these , even though each battle is equally important , there is an additional - indeed fascinating - element that Narain explores in the context of conserving the tiger . The contests regarding the environment , Narain argues , are essentially fought over conflicting ideas . The chapter on man-animal conflict reveals how , at times , co-warriors can drift apart because of differential perceptions . A fellow member of the task force on the tiger , which Narain was asked to chair , was adamant that people and tigers should be treated as exclusive categories . The success of Periyar 's conservation model that employed former poachers as protectors bolsters such a claim . <p> Narain describes herself as the sootradhar of a rather grim tale . But she is more of a whistle-blower , exposing , for example , the duplicity of and connivance among nation states as they go about diluting the principle of equality in international climate negotiations . The Paris climate treaty , hailed as a landmark in some quarters , was another occasion in which the world let go of the opportunity to uphold the idea that each nation , big or small , should be given an equal share of the planet 's atmospheric space . <p> Experts do not just raise questions : they are also expected to answer them . Narain , too , provides a ' blueprint ' to manage the exigencies brought about by a crisis of unprecedented scale . But this is also a race against time . Is there enough time left to chip away at the culture of denial or the plain ignorance about climate change in the vast , growing , iniquitous economy that is India ? Given the monumental apathy of the government , as well as the resistance to meaningful interventions from certain lobbies , how does one begin to transform personal , everyday choices within social spaces which , Narain correctly infers , resemble disconnected bubbles ? <p> Narain 's book is , admittedly , a bit dry in parts . But it makes it clear that she is not willing to give up this unequal battle just yet .
