<h> Clothes that nobody wants <h> New pattern needed as secondhand trade frays in face of cheap ' fast fashion ' <p> For decades , the donation bin has offered consumers in rich countries a guilt-free way to unload their old clothing . In a virtuous and profitable cycle , a global network of traders would collect these garments , grade them and transport them around the world to be recycled , worn again or turned into rags and stuffing . <p> Now that cycle is breaking down . Fashion trends are accelerating , new clothes are becoming as cheap as used ones , and poor countries are turning their backs on the secondhand trade . <p> Without significant changes in the way clothes are made and marketed , this could add up to an environmental disaster in the making . <p> Nobody is more alert to this shift than the roughly 200 businesses devoted to recycling clothes into yarn and blankets in Panipat , India . <p> Located 88km north of Delhi , the dusty city of 450,000 has served as the world 's largest recycler of woollen garments @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ for the US$4 billion ( S$5.2 billion ) used-clothing trade . <p> Panipat 's mills specialise in a cloth known as shoddy , which is made from low-quality yarn recycled from woollen garments . Much of what they produce is used to make cheap blankets for disaster-relief operations . <p> It has been a good business : At its peak in the early 2010s , Panipat 's shoddy manufacturers could turn out 100,000 blankets a day , accounting for 90 per cent of the relief-blanket market . <p> In the early 2000s , though , cash-flush Chinese manufacturers began using modern mills that could produce many times more blankets per day than Panipat 's , and in a wider variety of colours . <p> Mr Ramesh Goyal , the general manager of Ramesh Woollen Mills , told this writer that Chinese manufacturing has become so efficient that a new polar fleece blanket costs a mere US$2.50 retail - compared to US$2 for a recycled blanket . This has made China the preferred manufacturer of relief blankets worldwide , costing Panipat most of its export market . <p> So Panipat is @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ new fleece blankets . Today , about 50 mills do . <p> Ramesh Woollen Mills added a Chinese-built line in 2016 , boosting its production from 7,000kg a day to 12,000 , two-thirds of which is polar fleece . Consumers appreciate the quality , variety and fast production times . <p> But what 's good for Panipat and its customers is bad news for donors and the environment . <p> Even if Panipat were producing shoddy at its peak , it probably could n't manage the growing flood of used clothing entering the market in search of a second life . <p> Between 2000 and 2015 , global clothing production doubled , while the average number of times that a garment was worn before disposal declined by 36 per cent . In China , it declined by 70 per cent . <p> The rise of " fast fashion " is thus creating a bleak scenario : The tide of secondhand clothes keeps growing even as the markets to reuse them are disappearing . <p> From an environmental standpoint , that 's a big problem . By raising temperatures and intensifying droughts , climate change could substantially cut cotton yields , making garment production less predictable and far more expensive . <p> Industry executives are clearly concerned . The question is what to do about it . Some brands , such Hennes &amp; Mauritz ( better known as H&amp;M ) and Patagonia , are experimenting with new fibres made from recycled material , which could help . But longer term , the industry will have to try to refocus consumers on durability and quality - and charge accordingly . <p> Ways to do this include offering warranties on clothing and making tags that inform consumers of a product 's expected lifespan . <p> To satiate the hunger for fast fashion , meanwhile , brands might also explore subscription-based fashion rental businesses - such as China 's YCloset - or other more sustainable models . <p> None of these options can replace Panipat and the other mill @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ clothes for the poor .
