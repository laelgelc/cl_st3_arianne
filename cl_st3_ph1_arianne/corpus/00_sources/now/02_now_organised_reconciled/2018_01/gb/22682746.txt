<p> Image copyrightBas van de SchootbruggeImage caption One of the scales under the microscope <p> Newly discovered fossils show that moths and butterflies have been on the planet for at least 200 million years . <p> Scientists found fossilised butterfly scales the size of a speck of dust inside ancient rock from Germany . <p> The find pushes back the date for the origins of the Lepidoptera , one of the most prized and studied insect groups . <p> Researchers say they can learn more about the conservation of butterflies and moths by studying their early evolution . <p> They used acid to dissolve ancient rocks , leaving behind small fragments , including " perfectly preserved " scales that covered the wings of early moths and butterflies . <p> " We found the microscopic remains of these organisms in the form of these scales , " said Dr Bas van de Schootbrugge from Utrecht University in the Netherlands . <p> Intriguingly , they show that some of the moths and butterflies belonged to a group still alive today that have long straw-like tongues for sucking up nectar . <p> Image @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ " These finds push back the evolution of this group with proboscises - with a tongue - by about 70 million years , " said Dr van de Schootbrugge . <p> " Our finds show that the group that was supposed to co-evolve with flowers is actually much older . " <p> The Jurassic was a world dominated by gymnosperm plants , such as conifers , which produced sugary nectar to capture pollen from the air . The primitive insects may have fed on this nectar , before flowering plants came along around 130 million years ago . <p> Dr Russell Garwood of the University of Manchester , who is not connected with the study , said it had always been assumed that coiled mouthparts had evolved alongside the flowers that these animals pollinate . <p> " This new evidence suggests that perhaps the coiled mouthparts had another role , before flowering plants evolved , " he said . <p> Image copyrightBas van de SchootbruggeImage caption The rock was drilled from the ground east of Hannover <p> The study , published in the journal Science Advances , gives clues to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ every continent except Antarctica . <p> The early Lepidopterans survived the mass extinction at the end of the Triassic , which wiped out many other living things . <p> This knowledge will help inform modern conservation efforts , said Dr Timo van Eldijk , also of Utrecht University , the lead researcher on the study . <p> The information is " paramount to help us piece together how current manmade climate change might affect insects and their evolution in the future " , he said . <p> Image copyrightHossein RajaeiImage caption The scales are similar to those seen on this moth
