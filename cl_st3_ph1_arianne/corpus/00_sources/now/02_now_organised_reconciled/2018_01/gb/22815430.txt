<h> Secrets of sudden death of 200,000 antelopes in Central Asia <p> Saiga calf <p> 18 January 2018 <p> In May 2015 the sudden death of more than 200,000 saiga antelopes in Kazakhstan baffled the world . <p> In just three weeks , entire herds of tens of thousands of healthy animals died of haemorrhagic septicaemia . The deaths took place in the Betpak-Dala region of Kazakhstan , across a landscape equivalent to the area of the British Isles , and the number dead represented more than 60 per cent of the species ' global population . These deaths were caused by pasteurella multocida bacteria . We know that this pathogen was probably living harmlessly in the saigas ' tonsils up to this point , so what caused this sudden and dramatic mass mortality event ? <p> Scientists funded by a NERC urgency grant - funding which allows scientists to respond rapidly to unexpected and transient events affecting the environment - have carried out research to find out . By studying previous die-offs in saiga antelope populations , the researchers were able to uncover patterns and show that the probability of sudden die-offs increases when the weather is humid and warm , as was the case in 2015 . <p> The research also shows these very large mass mortalities , which have been observed in saiga antelopes before , including twice during the 1980s , are unprecedented in other large mammal species . <p> These sudden mass deaths also tend to occur during calving . The saiga antelope invests a lot in reproduction , so it can persist extreme continental environments where temperatures plummet to below -40 celsius in winter or rise to above 40 celsius in summer , with food scarce and wolves prowling . In fact , it bears the largest calves of any ungulate species . High levels of poaching since the 1990s have also been a major factor in depleting the species , while the development of railways , roads and fences threaten to fragment their habitat and interfere with their migrations . With all these threats , it is possible that another mass die-off from disease could reduce numbers to a level where recovery is no longer possible .
