<h> Time to get more of our youngsters into science <p> Chances are that it will be the same stereotype that has existed for time immemorial : a white male with white hair in a white coat . <p> Growing up with that perception , children who do n't fall into the ' white male ' category may feel that " science is n't for them " and are less likely to become a scientist . <p> And it 's true , not just for science but also technology , engineering and maths , collectively known as STEM subjects . <p> Indeed , only 12 per cent of those working in engineering occupations in the UK are women ( Engineering UK 2018 : The state of engineering ) , and BME men are 28 per cent less likely to work in STEM than white men ( Case Diversity in STEM report , 2014 ) . <p> A diverse STEM workforce is not only socially important but also crucial for the quality of research output ; the narrower the breadth of experience of scientists , the slower their scientific progress , whereas a wide range of people with different perspectives broadens out the possibility to find solutions and to make more exciting discoveries . <p> Additionally , if you 're only drawing from a limited pool of applicants , the skill set will also be narrowed . <p> At a time when we have a national skills shortage in STEM industries this is something that needs to be addressed . <p> It 's therefore a no-brainer that we need to encourage more people from more diverse backgrounds , genders and ethnicities into STEM . But how ? <p> Providing role models that highlight people in STEM who come from a range of backgrounds and have a host of experiences is essential for encouraging a more diverse representation in STEM , shattering stereotypes of these careers . <p> Therefore we need to find ways that enable children , and adults , to meet scientists and engineers and be able to relate to them , finding out about their jobs and their qualifications . <p> That is what STEM Festivals ( or STEMFests for short ) are doing . STEMFests , coordinated by STEM Sussex , take place across the region in areas where you do n't normally find science , technology , engineering or maths . <p> They are fun opportunities to get stuck into STEM and see classroom learning in context ; whether that 's through becoming a bee using virtual reality , building paper bridges or spelling your name with DNA . <p> Exploring STEM outside of school is n't just for children and these festivals are also a chance for adults to re-discover and enjoy science in practice . <p> Once we leave school , subject labels are lost and we do n't realise that baking the perfect cake is a chemical experiment , or winning at crazy golf involves some serious physics . <p> STEM subjects are fundamental to our lives ; from small everyday uses we take for granted to shaping the future of our changing world . Science is n't just for scientists , it 's for everyone , and STEM Festivals are guaranteed bring back child-like curiosity . <p> STEM festivals are not the only way that the public can interact with scientists and engineers ; there are increasing opportunities for people to get involved in science . <p> That may be through helping to collect data that will feed into a scientist 's project , termed ' citizen science ' , or through working with engineers to define a specific problem in the city . <p> There are a range of projects like these taking place in Sussex , for example : the ' Buzz Club ' run by the University of Sussex works with gardeners , allotment holders and bee enthusiasts to collect data on bees and other pollinators ; The University of Brighton runs a community-university partnership programme ( ' CUPP ' ) , creating relationships with the local community to see where university research can support the local area . <p> With the increase in ' fake news ' and debates around the legitimacy of climate change , data storage , etc , it 's more important than ever that scientists and engineers are communicating with the public . <p> Surveys show that the UK public think it is important to know about science given the importance of it to their daily lives , but just over half of people do not feel informed about STEM research and development , with a similar number thinking they see and hear too little from these professionals ( Public attitudes to science survey , 2014 ) . <p> Organisations have taken this on board and are opening up their work to the public , and there are an increasing number of volunteer groups who are supporting this . <p> The British Science Association has two such groups in Sussex : the Brighton and Hove branch and the Lewes branch . <p> Their remit is to increase the number of people who are actively engaged and involved in science and they do this by organising community events that get people thinking , talking and taking part in STEM-related activities , such as film screenings or debates . <p> As STEM professionals , we realise we need to do more to get people enthusiastic about science , technology , engineering and maths , and we 're gradually finding ways to do that around Brighton and Sussex . <p> l Dr Katy Petherick who is the Public Engagement Coordinator at the School of Life Sciences from the University of Sussex <p> Today , Saturday , April 28 , Lewes STEMFest is taking place at Lewes Town Hall , 12 to 3pm . <p> It 's a free event open to all , and everyone is encouraged to visit and meet workers , including those at the universities of Brighton and Sussex .
