<h> NZ 's $2 million a year search for an anti-cow burp vaccine <p> Livestock has directly caused about one-quarter of Earth 's warming in the industrial age . <p> This story was published on Newsroom.co.nz and is republished with permission . <p> In a cream-coloured metal barn a few minutes ' drive from Palmerston North , a black-and-white dairy cow stands in what looks like an oversize fish tank . <p> Through the transparent Plexiglas walls , she can see three other cows in adjacent identical cubicles munching their food in companionable silence . Tubes sprout from the tops of the boxes , exchanging fresh air for the stale stuff inside . <p> The cows , their owners say , could help slow climate change . <p> Livestock has directly caused about one-quarter of Earth 's warming in the industrial age , and scientists from the US departments of agriculture and energy say bigger , more resource-heavy cattle are accelerating the problem . <p> Contrary to popular belief , cows contribute to global warming mostly through their burps , not their flatulence . So about a dozen scientists here at AgResearch Grasslands , a government-owned facility , are trying to develop a vaccine to stop those burps . <p> " This is not a standard vaccine , " says Peter Janssen , the anti-burp programme 's principal research scientist . " It 's proving to be an elusive little genie to get out of the bottle . " <p> Janssen and his team are trying to purge cow stomachs of methanogens , the microbes that convert hydrogen into methane , a potent greenhouse gas . <p> It 's an unexpectedly delicate and difficult task , because cows rely on a host of other bacteria , fungi , and protozoa in their guts to digest the grasses they eat . Researchers have tried feeding them oregano , tea extracts , probiotics , antibiotics , seaweed ( too toxic ) , coconut oil ( too expensive ) , chloroform ( too carcinogenic ) , and even leftover grains from beer brewing ( which made cows pooh more nitrous oxide , another greenhouse gas ) . <p> No one knows how low-methane a cow can go without hurting its health or productivity . <p> So far no vaccine has progressed far enough to be given to the cows in the cubicles , where methane output can be measured . The vaccine must first be successfully tested in the lab and on sheep . <p> Although the scientists have figured out how to produce the desired antibodies in the cows , the animals continue to merrily burp . Janssen 's team is looking for proteins they can use to concoct a stronger vaccine , one that will better prime the cows ' immune systems to attack methanogens . <p> A single methanogen genome has 2000 proteins , so they 've narrowed their search to a handful of candidates , which they think could knock out the gassiest microbes . <p> The $2 million a year hunt <p> The effort is n't entirely altruistic . Grasslands is dedicated to boosting New Zealand agriculture and biotech , and Fonterra has vowed to increase its milk exports without increasing carbon emissions . <p> But 2017 is set to be the third-hottest year on record - the top two were 2016 and 2015 - so the globe can use all the help it can get , business-minded or not . <p> " It 's essential to reduce global livestock emissions in order to reduce climate change consistent with what countries signed up to under the Paris Agreement , " says Andy Reisinger , deputy director of the New Zealand Agricultural Greenhouse Gas Research Centre . <p> The hunt for a vaccine costs about $2 million a year , part of a $12m annual pool for curbing farming gases . The research money is meant to address our status as the world 's highest per capita methane emitter as well as curbing nitrous oxide . About two-thirds of the funding comes from the New Zealand Government . Industry supplies the rest . <p> Janssen says it may take five years or longer to create the right vaccine , but it will do much more to reduce bovine emissions worldwide than a treatment that Dutch company DSM is developing for bucket-fed cows . That 's because the vaccine will work just as well for grazers . <p> " There are n't too many ruminants in the world where the animals never get to eat grass , " he says , noting that even cows fattened in pens with buckets of feed typically start out in pastures . <p> DSM used computers to create a methane-blocking molecule called 3-nitrooxypropanol , or 3-NOP , that appears to cut burped methane by about a third when sprinkled on a cow 's food . <p> The company , whose annual research and development budget is US$500 million , is waiting for approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration , which is likely to take at least two more years . <p> " For developed countries , this is the most promising technology at this point , " says Alexander Hristov , a Penn State professor of dairy nutrition who 's tested 3-NOP for DSM . <p> The New Zealanders are leading the vaccine hunt , he says , but they have n't developed a proven product they can offer to farmers . <p> Janssen , a bespectacled man with the lanky limbs of a longtime mountain explorer , says his team is also working on substances similar to 3-NOP that could be given in pill form . <p> A complicating factor : No one knows how low-methane a cow can go without hurting its health or productivity . Trials suggest cows that burp less seem to cope fine , but scientists want to make sure there are no unintended consequences , such as reduced milk quality or quantity . <p> " We need to understand where that tipping point is , " Janssen says . <p> Humans are the final hurdle . Canadian scientists created low-polluting pigs almost a decade ago , but people would n't buy the genetically modified pork . <p> " Farmers will produce what the consumer demands , " says Tim McAllister , whose colleague Karen Beauchemin has been conducting trials of 3-NOP and other methane-reduction techniques for the Canadian government at the Lethbridge Research and Development Centre in Alberta . <p> Soaring global demand for meat makes climate concerns pressing . In Palmerston North , the cows seem content in their tanks , turning to watch as Janssen strides between their boxes . <p> For now , their burps are packed with methane , but they may not have to be . <p> This article first appeared in Bloomberg Businessweek and was reported with support from the UC Berkeley-11th Hour Food and Farming Journalism Fellowship . <p> This story was published on Newsroom.co.nz and is republished with permission .
