<h> Chemically altered soil could be used to build sustainable homes , scientists say <p> Adding chemicals to soil could create a strong and environmentally-friendly construction material to build homes in developing countries , scientists say . <p> The material , known as geopolymer-stabilised soil , is created when alkaline chemicals similar to those found in household cleaning products are added to soil . <p> Clay present in the soil is transformed into a geopolymer , a kind of glue similar to cement , which chemically binds the material today . <p> Mud has been used as a construction material for hundreds of years but adding the chemicals makes it stronger , more durable and potentially far more resistant to breaking down due to moisture . <p> Scientists at the University of Bath are examining whether geopolymer-stabilised soil could become a viable and robust construction solution for building homes in some of the world 's poorest areas . <p> They say modern-day building materials , such as cement and bricks , can be environmentally damaging and unaffordable for people living in such countries . <p> Geopolymers can typically be fired at 80C , whereas cement and fired bricks usually require more than 1,000C . <p> Alastair Marsh , postgraduate researcher in civil engineering at the University of Bath , said : " The construction materials we have used so far in the 20th and 21st centuries have been very good , but they 've come at a high energy and carbon cost . <p> " We 're trying to find more environmentally friendly alternatives . We 're using a process called geopolymerisation to make sustainable bricks out of soil . <p> " In this reaction , we produce a natural kind of cement that glues the soil together , making it stronger and more durable . <p> " The advantage of this reaction is that it has a far lower energy cost . I 'm hoping that through this , we can make new construction materials that are practical , affordable and environmentally sustainable . " <p> Tomorrow , we share news about @BathArchandCivE research into soil-based construction which could provide sustainable and environmentally friendly housing in developing countries . " <p> Depending on the source materials and required properties , geopolymer-stabilised soils could have as little as half the carbon emissions impact of concrete , and a quarter that of fired bricks , he said . <p> Mr Marsh added that a typical family home could be built using approximately 10 tonnes of soil by adding 5-10% sodium hydroxide . <p> There is a significant knowledge gap in understanding how the geopolymerisation reaction works for different soil types and Mr Marsh is physically and chemically testing a range of soils to improve this . <p> Professor Andrew Heath , of the University of Bath 's Department of Architecture and Civil Engineering , added : " This research is key to the development of sustainable housing around the world . " <p> The project has already led to a joint project looking at developing sustainable housing in India , in collaboration with the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore .
