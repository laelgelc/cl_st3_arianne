But what if there were an alternative that could reduce our emissions , create good jobs in Manitoba and use the savings to reinvest in our transit system ? <p> When the Pallister government cancelled the 50-50 cost-sharing agreement for transit , working families , students and seniors got stuck with a 25-cent fare hike to cover the shortfall . Despite widespread protests , the recent provincial budget failed to reverse this decision and the effects are felt by lower-income citizens . <p> The provincial budget also failed to deliver any of the 100 new electric buses promised in the provincial climate plan and left no blueprint as to how the government would ever pay for them . Now we have learned recently that transit services will have to pay the upcoming carbon tax , even though transit is a far greener alternative than individual automobiles . Winnipeg Transit estimates the carbon tax will add a further $1.2 million burden per year on an already stressed system . <p> Evidently , the outlook for the future of public transit is not positive . However , the good news is that there is an alternative . Manitoba should be using a proven and innovative financing tool to generate huge future savings for transit operations . These savings could be used to improve transit services , add additional buses to the fleet while improving service for routes on our roads and free up resources to provide better safety for transit operators and riders alike . <p> Instead of increasing fares , we could reduce them to make public transit more affordable and accessible to everyone . And all of this could be done without costing the Pallister government a single dollar . <p> The tool the province could be using is called pay-as-you-save financing , or PAYS . These loans are most effective when a bit more money up front makes it possible to acquire a more expensive technology that is far cheaper to operate over the long run . The operational savings are first used to pay back the loan , but then they are funnelled back to the purchaser in operational savings once the loan is paid off . <p> We have to look no further than to Manitoba 's Indigenous owned and operated Aki Energy as a great example of how PAYS financing can work . This Indigenous social enterprise trained 40 people to become certified installers of geothermal energy systems . <p> To date , more than 400 homes in First Nations communities have been switched to this green alternative , with PAYS loans provided by Manitoba Hydro covering the entire upfront cost . Hydro has verified the average savings in each home is 12,000 kW-h per year . The financial savings involved are split between the home 's occupants and paying back the loan , with all the savings going to the occupants once the loan is paid off . <p> By using PAYS , we can reduce our energy consumption and reduce our greenhouse gas emissions profile while also saving money . <p> A similar opportunity exists right now with electric buses . They are more expensive upfront than diesel buses , but deliver significant operating savings estimated at $60,000 per bus per year , according to University of Manitoba professor Nazim Cicek . <p> Cicek 's argument is further enhanced by the fact that the estimates provided were calculated before the carbon tax -- which will add $2,000 cost each year to run a diesel bus on our roads . With more than 600 buses in Winnipeg Transit 's fleet -- and only four of them electric so far -- the potential savings of switching the whole fleet to electric is a staggering $38 million per year . <p> A July 2016 report , which was released only recently by the Pallister government , called for the city of Winnipeg to purchase an additional 12 to 20 electric buses . The report identifies higher upfront costs and the need for more electric charging infrastructure as two key obstacles . <p> Here 's where a PAYS loan could really make a difference . <p> The Pallister government could provide an interest-free PAYS loan to the city to cover the roughly $350,000 additional cost of each electric bus . A $7-million PAYS loan would enable the city to acquire 20 new electric buses at no more cost than they would have paid for the diesel option . At $62,000 of savings per electric bus , the payback period to the province is less than six years , or a return on investment of 18 per cent . <p> The Pallister government easily could avoid all borrowing costs by financing the PAYS loan using upcoming carbon tax revenues . The operating savings even could be used to help build the required electrical charging infrastructure if federal funding or support from the Federation of Canadian Municipalities Green Fund are not available . And let us not forget that Manitoba Hydro would be responsible for selling electricity as fuel to transit , which could lessen the potential increase in prices for ratepayers . <p> The premier 's relationship with city hall clearly could use improvement . The province 's confused messaging around the carbon tax and why revenues are not going to be used to address climate change could also benefit from a success story .
