Yet the possible impediments are many -- from funding to political will , bureaucracy and a recent safety reports scandal . <p> The completion of the Genoa bridge was hailed as a sign of renewal for Italy , where over 28,000 people have died in the coronavirus pandemic and millions risk losing their jobs due to an economically-crippling nationwide lockdown . <p> The hi-tech flyover " is a symbol for the whole of Italy . An Italy that can rise again , that will roll up its sleeves , that will not allow itself to be beaten , " Prime Minister Giuseppe Conte said at the unveiling ceremony . <p> As ships in the maritime city 's port sounded their horns to mark the completion , Conte paid homage to the 43 people who plunged to their deaths when the former span , the Morandi flyover , collapsed in 2018 . <p> Other such tragedies could be averted by funding roads , bridges , schools , hospitals and climate change adaptation , which would create jobs and fuel short- and long-term growth . <p> Almost 750 public works worth 62 billion euros ( $68 billion ) were on hold at the end of last year , from large bridges to small-town schools , according to Italy 's construction lobby ANCE . <p> Kick-starting them would create 962,000 jobs , it said . <p> Sceptics wonder where the money will come from -- with Italian GDP forecast to contract by between eight and 10 percent this year -- and whether the political will exists in a fragile coalition government . <p> -- Disasters in the making -- <p> The Morandi -- one of the world 's longest concrete bridges when it opened in 1967 -- was a symbol of rebirth for postwar Italy , which was modernising rapidly , throwing up thousands of concrete viaducts , tunnels and bridges . <p> But by 2018 , Italy ranked 22nd out of 28 EU countries for the efficiency of its transport network . <p> While its ancient Roman cement structures still stood , it discovered that modern versions had much shorter life-spans . <p> The Morandi collapse followed a string of similar incidents and it finally set alarm bells ringing . <p> Most of Italy 's 6,500 kilometres ( 4,000 miles ) of motorway are managed by private companies , which must ensure their bridges are safe . <p> A post-disaster probe found evidence of poor maintenance by Autostrade per l'Italia , which is among Italy 's biggest toll-road operators and owned by the powerful Benetton family . <p> It also unearthed allegedly falsified safety reports by the motorway unit and its sister company for several other bridges , in what prosecutors suspected were attempts to cut costs . <p> Autostrade per l'Italia denies any wrongdoing and widespread calls for it to be stripped of its concession have as yet come to nothing . <p> It is not the only operator failing to keep its network safe . <p> The latest bridge to crumble in Italy , managed by state-owned ANAS , fell last month into a river between Liguria and Tuscany . It would have been heavily used at the time , had it not been for the virus lockdown . <p> There are 20 more bridges at risk of collapse across the country , and 200 road tunnels that do not meet European safety standards , according to investigative reports seen and cited by the Repubblica daily in January . <p> -- ' Give the country a plan ' -- <p> " I am begging you on my knees , give the country a great plan -- like the Marshall Plan -- to restart it , " Pietro Salini , CEO of Italy 's biggest builder Salini Impregilo , told Conte last week . <p> Salini Impregilo , which was charged with reconstructing the Genoa bridge with shipbuilder Fincantieri , wants to create a construction hub called Project Italy by combining its assets with those of struggling building companies . <p> It says it would help restart numerous projects that are currently blocked across the country . But its biggest challenge may be generating sufficient liquidity . <p> Since the shutdown , Conte has unveiled stimulus packages worth 750 billion euros ( $830 billion ) . He has also , however , had to apologise several times for long delays in payments . <p> Last week , he lifted the lockdown early for construction projects considered to have strategic importance , from schools , prisons and council housing , to disaster risk reduction for extreme weather events . <p> Environmentalist lobby Legambiente has urged the government to make the climate crisis its infrastructure priority , after nearly 160 extreme events in 2019 -- flash floods , whirlwinds and landslides -- killed 42 people . <p> A historic high tide that washed through Venice in December , causing millions of euros of damage to Saint Mark 's Basilica alone , was blamed both on climate change and huge delays to a vast flood barrier project . <p> The country 's schools are also in an alarming state . Fifty-five percent of school buildings in Italy were built before the 1974 anti-seismic regulations were brought in , according to ANCE . <p> Some 43 percent of them are in areas classified as high-risk for earthquakes . Over 70 percent do not have fire safety certificates . Stories regularly hit the news about pupils evacuated over crumbling classroom ceilings . <p> Many have pointed out that the national closure of schools until September because of the coronavirus is the perfect opportunity for the renovation of ageing structures . <p> -- Red tape -- <p> The government has said it will widen the budget deficit by 55 billion euros , a " shock cure " which would see public debt in the eurozone 's third largest economy jump to 155.7 percent of GDP , from the already mammoth 134.8 percent in 2019 . <p> It pledged to speed up new public works that are in an advanced planning stage and maintain existing ones . <p> And it also promised to " urgently and drastically simplify administrative procedures " in critical sectors , including construction , the green economy and Internet connectivity . <p> Italy is famed for its red tape and projects often drag on for years , plagued by bureaucracy . <p> The country has at least 50 large infrastructure projects that are blocked but have been signed off on , with the money ready to spend , from a high-speed train in Sicily to the widening of a motorway in Tuscany , ANCE says . <p> Financial experts caution however that some of those funds may be withdrawn and channelled to projects considered more essential due to the crisis , or with immediate prospects of a payoff . <p> The National Council of Engineers ( CNI ) is one of many bodies to have sent the government proposals on how best to use infrastructure to reboot the country and shape it along different lines . <p> He suggested investment could be made to encourage a return to the countryside , revitalising hundreds of abandoned little towns across Italy , and improving internet connections to encourage working from home . <p> -- Mafia -- <p> Zambrano acknowledged , though , that he had little hope the government would actually do the necessary tape-cutting . Reforms aimed at reducing bureaucracy in construction , in 2018 and 2019 , were disappointing . <p> " There are always elements of the government that are distrustful , who equate big projects with corruption , ever since the Mani Pulite ( Clean Hands ) scandal , " he said , referring to widespread political corruption in the 1990s . <p> The crisis has sparked a fierce debate over whether projects are being obstructed by overly-strict corruption and mafia controls , with some even calling for them to be scrapped . <p> Italy 's anti-corruption authority has warned the rules are essential to prevent mafia infiltration . <p> " Organised crime groups would certainly be interested in getting a slice of public contracts , construction is where they thrive , " criminology expert Anna Sergi told AFP . <p> The powerful ' Ndrangheta in Calabria and Camorra in Campagna have infiltrated road and airport construction , while the Cosa Nostra in Sicily prefers lower-key infrastructure projects which are easier to control , she said . <p> -- Politically controversial -- <p> Political economy expert Erik Jones from Johns Hopkins University said the greatest problem lay with the anti-establishment Five Star Movement , which governs Italy in coalition with the centre-left Democratic Party . <p> The Movement ( M5S ) is the largest party in government and has components within it that loathe big infrastructure projects , from the high-speed Turin to Lyon train line to a Trans Adriatic gas pipeline . <p> " There are many of these products that are shovel-ready , but are politically controversial , as well as being held up for bureaucratic reasons , " Jones told AFP . <p> Pushing forward on them would " light a spark " under M5S supporters who had regularly taken to the streets to protest such projects and were already suffering " pent-up frustration " after two months of lockdown . <p> The government could pick other big , less controversial pieces of infrastructure that are not shovel-ready , but it would take time to identify them and go through the entire planning process . <p> Jones also warned of another potential flaw in the plan . <p> " The Italian state is not great at paying firms that do work for it . It has a huge stockpile of arrears that it owes . " <p> All said , the idea of restarting the economy through massive infrastructure spending is , he says , " just pie in the sky " .
