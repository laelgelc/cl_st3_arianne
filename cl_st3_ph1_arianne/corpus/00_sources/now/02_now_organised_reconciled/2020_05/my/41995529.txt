evidence that was growing too big to ignore . <p> " I think there comes a point when it 's worth being skeptical of your skepticism , " the Englishman said in an interview with AFP . <p> Canine science has enjoyed a resurgence in the past two decades , much of it extolling dogs ' smarts . <p> Titles like " The Genius of Dogs " by Brian Hare have advanced the idea that dogs have an innate and exceptional intelligence . <p> Wynne , however plays spoilsport , arguing that Fido is just not that brilliant . <p> Pigeons can identify different kinds of objects in 2D images ; dolphins have shown they understand grammar ; honeybees signal the location of food sources to each other through dance ; all feats that no dogs have ever been known to accomplish . <p> Even wolves , dogs ' ancestor species known for their ferocity and lack of interest in people , have shown the ability to follow human cues -- including , in a recent Swedish study , by playing fetch . <p> Wynne proposes a paradigm shift , synthesizing cross-disciplinary research to posit that it is dogs ' " hypersociability " or " extreme gregariousness " that sets them apart . <p> -- Williams syndrome gene -- <p> One of the most striking advances comes from studies regarding oxytocin , a brain chemical that cements emotional bonds between people , but which is , according to new evidence , also responsible for interspecies relationships between dogs and humans . <p> Recent research led by Takefumi Kikusui at Japan 's Azabu University has shown that levels of the chemical spike when humans and their dogs gaze into each others ' eyes , mirroring an effect observed between mothers and babies . <p> In genetics , UCLA geneticist Bridgett vonHoldt made a surprising discovery in 2009 : Dogs have a mutation in the gene responsible for Williams syndrome in humans -- a condition characterized by intellectual limitations and exceptional gregariousness . <p> " The essential thing about dogs , as for people with Williams syndrome , is a desire to form close connections , to have warm personal relationships -- to love and be loved , " writes Wynne . <p> Numerous insights have also been gleaned through new behavior tests -- many devised by Wynne himself and easy to replicate at home with the help of treats and cups . <p> One involved researchers using a rope to pull open the front door of a dog 's home and placing a bowl of food at an equal distance to its owner , finding that the animals overwhelmingly went to their human first . <p> Magnetic resonance imaging has drilled down on the neuroscience , showing that dogs ' brains respond to praise as much or even more than food . <p> But although dogs have an innate predisposition for affection , it requires early life nurturing to take effect . <p> Nor is the love affair exclusive to humans : A farmer who raised pups among a penguin colony on a tiny Australian island was able to save the birds from maurading foxes , in an experiment that was the basis for a 2015 film . <p> -- All you need is love -- <p> For Wynne , the next frontiers of dog science may come through genetics , which will help unravel the mysterious process by which domestication took place at least 14,000 years ago . <p> Wynne is an advocate for the trash heap theory , which holds that the precursors to ancient dogs congregated around human dumping grounds , slowly ingratiating themselves with people before the enduring partnership we know today was established through joint hunting expeditions . <p> It 's far less romantic than the popular notion of hunters who captured wolf pups and then trained them , which Wynne derides as a " completely unsupportable point of view " given the ferocity of adult wolves who would turn on their human counterparts . <p> New advances in the sequencing of ancient DNA will allow scientists to discover when the crucial mutation to the gene that controls Williams syndrome occurred . <p> Wynne guesses this happened 8,000 -- 10,000 years ago , at the end of the last Ice Age , when humans began regularly hunting with dogs . <p> What makes these findings important , beyond advancing science , is their implications for dogs ' welfare , he argues .
