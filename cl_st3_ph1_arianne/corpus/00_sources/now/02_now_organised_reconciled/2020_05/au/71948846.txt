<h> Take a deep breath of cleaner air when deciding energy and transport policy <p> Virus restrictions have encouraged walking and cycling and reduced air pollution to a level not seen since 2006 . It should be a turning point for post-pandemic transport and climate policy , argues the team who measured a significant drop in global carbon emissions under lockdown . <p> The numbers of people cycling and walking in public spaces during COVID-19 has skyrocketed . <p> Cities from Bogota to Berlin and Vancouver have expanded bike lanes and public paths to accommodate the extra cycling traffic . In Australia , the New South Wales government is encouraging councils to follow suit . <p> Mandatory social distancing under COVID-19 is disrupting the way we live and work , creating new lifestyle patterns . But once the crisis is over , will -- and should -- the picture return to normal ? <h> Advertisement <p> That 's one of many key questions emerging as the precise effect of the pandemic on carbon emissions becomes clear . <p> Our research published today in Nature Climate Change shows how COVID-19 has affected global emissions in six economic sectors . We discovered a significant decline in daily global emissions -- most markedly , on April 7 . <p> The analysis is useful as we consider the deep structural change needed to shift the global economy to zero emissions . <p> Take , for example , our quieter streets . The fall in road traffic was a main driver of the global emissions decline . So , if we encourage cycling and working from home to continue beyond the current pandemic , our climate goals will become far more achievable . <p> Global daily fossil emissions of carbon dioxide in million tonnes . Dash lines represent different future scenarios in the evolution of the pandemic and confinement levels . <h> Crunching the numbers <p> At the end of each year we publish the Global Carbon Budget -- a report card on global and regional carbon trends . But the unusual circumstances this year prompted us to run a preliminary analysis . <p> And we built an index showing the level and size of the population under confinement in each country , to extrapolate the available data worldwide . <h> The pandemic 's peak <p> In early April , the reduction in global activity peaked . On April 7 , global emissions were 17% lower than an equivalent day in 2019 . <p> Total daily emissions in early April were similar to those observed in 2006 . The fact that the world now emits as much under " lockdown " conditions as it did under normal conditions just 14 years ago underscores the rapid emissions growth in that time . <p> Road traffic contributed the most to the emissions decline ( 43% ) . The next biggest contributors were the power sector ( electricity and heat ) and industry ( manufacturing and material production such as cement and steel ) . These three sectors combined were responsible for 86% of the fall in daily emissions . <p> The peak daily fall in global aviation activity ( 60% ) was the largest of any sector we analysed . But aviation 's contribution to the overall fall in emissions was relatively small ( 10% ) because it makes up just 3% of global emissions . <p> As people stayed at home , we found a small increase in global emissions from the residential sector . <h> The 2020 outlook <p> We assessed how the pandemic will affect carbon dioxide emissions over the rest of 2020 . Obviously , this will depend on how strong the restrictions are in coming months , and how long they last . <p> If widespread global confinement ends in mid June , we estimate overall carbon emissions in 2020 will fall about 4% compared to 2019 . If less severe restrictions remain in place for the rest of the year , the reduction would be about 7% . <p> If we consider the various pandemic scenarios and uncertainties in the data , the full range of emissions decline is 2% to 13% . <p> Now for the important context . Under the Paris climate agreement and according to the United Nations Gap report , global emissions must fall by between 3% and 7% each year between now and 2030 to limit climate change well below 2 ? and 1.5 ? , respectively . <p> Under our projected emissions drop , the world could meet this target in 2020 -- albeit for the wrong reasons . <p> Stabilising the global climate system will require extraordinary changes to our energy and economic systems , comparable to the disruption brought by COVID-19 . <h> A fork in the road <p> So how could we make this byproduct of the crisis -- the emissions decline in 2020 -- a turning point ? <p> A slow economic recovery might lower emissions for a few years . But if previous global economic crises are any indication , emissions will bounce back from previous lows . <p> But it need not be this way . The recent forced disruption offers an opportunity to change the structures underpinning our energy and economic systems . This could set us on the path to decarbonising the global economy . <h> Advertisement <p> Let 's consider again the extra people now walking and riding bikes . What if governments took the chance now to support such active , low-emissions travel , and make it permanent ? What if we accelerate the rollout of electric cars , bikes and scooters , to both broaden transport options and save lives through cleaner city air ? <p> Encouragingly , the NSW government recently announced a A$15 million fund to help councils create bigger public thoroughfares and extra road crossings during the crisis . If the community embraces the changes , they may become permanent . <p> And Paris will invest ? 300 million ( A$500 million ) into a 650km bicycle network post-lockdown , including new " pop up " cycleways established during the pandemic . <p> The crisis has opened the way for other structural change . People and businesses have been able to test what travel is essential , and when alternative remote communication might be equally or more efficient . <p> Finally , energy and material consumption dropped during COVID-19 . While such forced reductions are not a long-term answer to reducing greenhouse gas emissions , lower consumption can be achieved in other ways , such as new types of energy efficiency , that allow both environmentally sustainable development and rising well-being , incomes and activity . <p> Get InDaily in your inbox .
