<p> BECCS involves growing plants which remove carbon dioxide as they grow and are then burned in power stations to produce electricity . The resulting carbon dioxide from this combustion is captured and stored underground . The result is carbon dioxide removal from the atmosphere . <p> It is the not-so-high-tech wonder many are waiting for , but it comes at a high price . It also risks delaying policies that actually reduce emissions in the first place . <h> Mapping the future , now <p> According to models , BECCS is the technology we are banking on to fix our climate disruption and safeguard our future . The models have doubled down on BECCS , but it is an unproven solution on a large scale -- and one that has significant and damaging side effects . <p> There are three choices on the table ( we will likely see a mix of at least two ) : <p> Equitable sustainability Massive amounts of low-carbon energy ( solar , wind , batteries , electric vehicles ) , huge improvements in energy efficiency , a revolution of the food systems and a transition of society towards lower growth , both in population and economy . <p> Hypothetical backstop Continue down the road we are on , and hope to " overcorrect " the problem in the future by sucking carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere . A lack of political will and intense lobbying has meant what was once a fairly manageable problem has become an exercise in inventing heroic backstops . <p> Cowboy optimism Engineer the planet ( even further ) to ease the impacts of climate disruption , but not the underlying causes themselves . <p> The first choice means we change ourselves and alter the way we do things . The second means we continue polluting as we do now , and hope to clean up later . This option is a bit like the plastic clean-up trial currently underway in the Pacific . <p> Choice three means we simply paper over the cracks , perhaps saving some aspects of human civilisation but pushing large parts of nature to extinction . <h> Bonfire of the BECCS <p> BECCS on an industrial scale needs many resources . Plants need land , water and fertilisers ( sometimes ) to grow , and infrastructure to get low-density plant matter from one place to another . We already struggle to do this sustainably . <p> Related to this , it is reasonable to think that BECCS will increase food prices . We have to produce 70% extra food by 2050 to just keep up with population and food demand increases . Can we do this while using vast tracts of land for BECCS production ? Perhaps only if we have a big change in dietary habits which frees up land ? <p> BECCS make use of thermal power plants so inherit many problems related to running them . Power plants are heat engines and need water for cooling . We already have problems with water cooling , and it is getting worse with climate change . <p> Finally , BECCS power plants will produce ash , which is a " better " version than the ash from coal plants ( it does n't take much ) , but will still need attention . <h> The role of Integrated Assessment Models <p> The origin story for BECCS has been told elsewhere , but how did we end up in a situation where the large majority of models point to this one problematic solution ? These models are called Integrated Assessment Models , and come in two main varieties : simple and complex . <p> The complex ones are mostly used for investigating technology choices . The simple ones are often used to explore what the cost of carbon could be . This year 's Nobel Prize winner in economics , Bill Nordhaus , works with these simple models . <p> The overall weaknesses of these models have been covered in compelling and entertaining ways . Given the depth of the complex models , it is difficult to be sure why BECCS dominates . Most would agree that there are three likely possibilities . <p> First , these models discount future benefits and costs to a large extent . That is , they assume that future benefits and costs are much less in the future than they are today . The default rate at which models discount is 5% per year , meaning that to avoid $100 of climate damage in 2100 is only worth $3 to us today . Many have argued that this is much too high , ethically inappropriate , and misleading . <p> I know of only one study which performs a sensitivity analysis using so-called discount rates . It finds that carbon dioxide removal is significantly reduced with lower discount rates . <p> Third , these models have a difficult job estimating the damage from climate change . The risk from emitting now and paying later is fat-tailed -- there is a non-negligible increased risk of catastrophe even if we do manage to implement choice two at a large scale . <h> Taking off the BECCS blinders <p> Are there technologies other than BECCS ? If we must hypothesise backstop technologies , then direct air capture is a possibility . As the name implies , it sucks carbon directly from the air . <p> Although it does n't generate energy in the process ( in fact it uses large amounts of energy ) , it does n't have as many of the problems faced by BECCS . A possible future consists of solar-powered direct air capture in the Middle Eastern desert pulling carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and pumping it underground into reservoirs from which oil was once pumped . <p> Fortunately , there are an increasing number of studies which take a non-backstop approach . These still use integrated assessment modelling , but investigate other options , like very low-energy demand scenarios and large-scale behaviour change ( for example to plant-based diets ) which reduce other , non-CO2 gases quickly . <p> There is nothing to be lost by committing to the first choice as fast as possible . In fact , many of the important solutions are better for our health too ( such as using bikes instead of cars , plant-based diets , and insulating houses ) . And if we end up needing BECCS , then so be it , but the earlier we start moving to low-carbon economies , the more potential catastrophes we avoid .
