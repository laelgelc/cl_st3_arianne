He 's surrounded by trees on The Overland Track -- gnarled myrtle beech , spindly snow gum -- but he 's looking for one tree in particular . <p> Time is short . Steely clouds are tearing at the dolerite peaks of Tasmania 's Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair National Park , portents of rain , but there is another reason for haste . <p> A pencil pine can live a thousand years but the ancient tree bears its seed cones only sporadically . This rare event , known as " masting " , last took place in 2015 . Now , in the midst of a global pandemic , it 's happened again . <p> The seed-bearing wo n't last long -- " If you 're not there at the right time you miss it , " Wood laments -- and nobody knows when it will next occur . <p> As Tasmania 's habitat changes , many alpine species may become extinct , says Wood . ( Supplied ) <p> So , he 's on a mission : to collect and store genetically diverse seed from the conifers most at risk from climate change in the Tasmanian Wilderness World Heritage Area . <p> Wood is not a survival expert . He 's a botanist from Kew Gardens in London , who now works in the seed bank at the Royal Tasmanian Botanic Gardens in Hobart . He 's also never been on the Overland Track . <p> His navigator is Justin Dyer , a seasoned guide with the Tasmanian Walking Company , a local operator of guided hikes along Tassie 's top trails . <p> It takes two days for Wood and Dyer to get where they want to be , near Pelion Plains . There 's a primeval specimen shaped a little like Marge Simpson 's hairdo , with green scale-like leaves and tightly cross-hatched cones in the higher branches . <p> Pencil pine cones ripe and ready to release seeds . ( Supplied ) <p> The woody capsules are ripe and ready to release seeds . <p> " If there 's a severe fire , " says Wood , " the iconic pencil pine will be one of those likely lost -- along with the King Billy pine . So there 's a critical need to take action now if we are to keep these plants in our landscape . " <h> An expected gift as the COVID-19 crisis hits <p> The coronavirus has unexpectedly given Wood and Dyer one of life 's superlative gifts . <p> Between October and May , the Overland Track usually attracts intrepid recreationists from around the world , with up to 60 hikers a day undertaking the 65 kilometre six-day trek from Cradle Mountain to Mt Ossa ( Tasmania 's highest peak ) ending at Lake St Clair , Australia 's deepest natural lake . <p> But , for now , the scientist and the outdoorsman have it all to themselves . When COVID-19 forced the closure of all national parks they were grateful to discover complete solitude on one of Australia 's most exhilarating alpine walks . <p> From the Overland Track , Wood and his guide had sweeping views of Mt Ossa , Tasmania 's highest peak . ( Supplied ) <p> " It was eerily quiet out there , " says Wood on his return to civilization at the end of a five-day odyssey in a landscape carved with glacial valleys , ancient forests , moorlands and meadows . <p> " One day we came across a platypus snuffling about in a small tarn beside the track , totally oblivious to human contact . It was very sweet . " <p> Another day , through sheets of torrential rain , the pair walked into a dense field of pandani . <p> " It 's an insane plant , " says Wood . " A type of heather that looks more like a weirdly tropical , grassy kind of palm with a single head of long leaves . It 's peculiarly out of place in this cold environment . " <p> The pair had to squeeze through a " belt " of them as they ascended Mount Oakleigh . " It was really misty and they were standing in shrouds like big cloth wipers in a car wash station , " says Wood . <p> " I looked back at this beautiful dark silhouette on the horizon . It was a single pandani with its leaves arching downwards to the earth against a grey-purple sky . It was just gorgeous . " <h> New species are still being uncovered every year <p> The final night spent in a private hut was something of a relief for Wood . <p> He 'd been menaced by a knee injury but the cabin was warm and Dyer was an artisan with lamb ragout . The guide had brought his sourdough starter from home to make bread and there was a good store of red wine . <p> Isolation in nature is not entirely unfamiliar to Wood . <p> Wood and his guide made their way through stunning wilderness and , on one day , sheets of torrential rain . ( Supplied : Royal Tasmanian Botanical Gardens ) <p> " I occasionally worked on Christmas day at Wakehurst Gardens in the United Kingdom , " he says . " I 'd have the entire estate to myself and it would be oddly quiet . Not another living soul all day . " <p> When the English seed collector moved to Tasmania in 2005 , he 'd been interested in botany since his mid-teens , but was still astonished by the difference in the landscape . <p> " I 'd worked in Mexico and Africa , but then I came out to Australia for the first time , and it felt as if I got off the plane onto on a totally different planet . Nothing looked familiar . " <p> He decided then that if the opportunity to work down under ever came up , he 'd take it . Tasmania 's floral diversity is much lower than on the mainland but new species are still being unearthed every year . <p> " This season I was out surveying and I discovered a plant that was only known from another collection that was made 30 years ago and we do n't even know what it is , " says Wood . <p> " Discoveries like that are pretty rare in Europe but are much more common out here . The opportunities to actually learn about new things is huge ... It 's immensely rewarding and very exciting to be in this sort of environment . " <h> A quest for conservation <p> Still , as Tasmania 's habitat changes , many alpine species may become extinct , says Wood -- some have already been lost and may not be able to re-establish themselves naturally . <p> " The increasing aridity and likelihood of fire in these areas is very worrying , " he says . <p> Between October and May , the Overland Track usually attracts intrepid recreationists from around the world . ( Supplied ) <p> Most stands of pencil pine are clonal -- that is , from one individual . This means seed collectors must capture genetic variation over a large area to ensure biological diversity . <p> The two men , who met as strangers , covered 300 hectares of land , picking two handfuls of cones from 46 stands of trees , eventually filling a cloth bag with 8,000 viable seeds . <p> " It 's not a bad-sized haul as an insurance policy for the future , " says Wood . <p> The collection will be stored in the Tasmanian Seed Conservation Centre and the UK 's Millennium Seed Bank . One day , the seeds may be reintroduced back into the landscape to flourish again which , for Wood , is a comforting thought . <p> " It will be nice if we can leave those who come after us with more than just a glimpse of what the world was in the beginning , " he says .
