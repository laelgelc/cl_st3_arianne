<p> " Climate change has reduced coral cover , and surviving corals are under increasing pressure as water temperatures rise and the frequency and severity of coral bleaching events increase , " Dr Buerger said . <p> With three mass bleaching over the past five years the barrier reef has not had enough time between events to recover . <p> The team isolated tiny algae which live inside coral tissue and exposed it to increasingly warmer temperatures over four years . <p> The experiments were conducted at the Australian Institute of Marine Science in a sea simulator . <p> Patrick Buerger/CSIRO <p> Called " directed evolution " the technique assisted the microalgae to adapt and survive in hotter conditions . <p> " Once the microalgae were reintroduced into coral larvae , the newly established coral-algal symbiosis was more heat tolerant compared to the original one , " Dr Buerger said . <p> Fellow University of Melbourne researcher Madeleine van Oppen , of the Australian Institute of Marine Science , said the evolved microalgae was better at photosynthesis , which in turn improved the resistance of the coral animal .
