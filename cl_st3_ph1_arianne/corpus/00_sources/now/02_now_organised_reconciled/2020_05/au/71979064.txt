<h> Opinion <h> He 's no Hawke , but he 's no Howard either : Morrison 's pragmatism may yet be his making <p> In 2005 , then-ACTU secretary Greg Combet held a secret meeting with prime minister John Howard , who wanted to reform industrial relations laws with a new policy he called WorkChoices . <p> Combet urged Howard to ditch the controversial Australian Workplace Agreements that formed the heart of the policy , and introduce a safety net of minimum pay and conditions for workers . Howard " was completely unmoved " , Combet later told journalist Christine Jackman . <p> Two years later , in the face of a huge union backlash and a successful public campaign against WorkChoices , Howard was forced to implement a " fairness test " which sounded a lot like a safety net for minimum pay and conditions . <p> It was too late . Howard lost the 2007 election so resoundingly he could n't even hang onto his own seat of Bennelong . WorkChoices was a major reason the electorate turned off Howard -- one analyst called the policy " the longest suicide note in political history " . <p> Advertisement <p> Loading <p> John should have listened to Greg . <p> This week Scott Morrison announced a plan to reform industrial relations in the wake of a pandemic that put nearly 600,000 workers out of work in April and has ruined the economy in ways that are freshly shocking every day . Morrison said he wanted to bring employers and unions together for " working group meetings " on various aspects of industrial relations law . <p> The stated aim is to assist economic recovery and create jobs under what Morrison is calling " JobMaker " , standing alongside JobSeeker and JobKeeper to complete the JobPolicy box set . <p> By now you will have noticed a theme -- what it lacks in creativity it makes up for in consistency . As a sign of good faith , Morrison announced he was ditching the " Ensuring Integrity Bill " , AKA " union-busting " legislation first introduced by Malcolm Turnbull in 2017 , and re-introduced by Morrison 's government last year . <p> This legislation , which Morrison said he wanted to ensure " thugs get the punt " , was unlikely to pass the Senate anyway , so it constituted no real sacrifice . Immediate and unfavourable comparisons were made with the Prices and Income Accord of the Hawke-Keating years , and everyone pointed out that " Scott Morrison is no Bob Hawke " , in a way that was not meant as a compliment . <p> The Labor opposition , horrified that Morrison was being mentioned in the same breath as their beloved Hawkie , chose to emphasise , instead , the rather more gruesome WorkChoices angle . It is probably the more instructive precedent , as an example of everything that can go wrong when a Coalition government attempts IR reform . <p> Illustration : Reg Lynch Credit : <p> " The idea that a Liberal government is about to engage in industrial relations reform will send a chill down the spine of every Australian worker , " said Labor 's deputy leader Richard Marles . <p> He was n't wrong . But there are some pretty solid differences between now and 2005 , even if we do n't count the intervening global pandemic . <p> This time , unlike Howard , Morrison is not only listening to Greg Combet , he has the former union boss working for him . Combet was a driving force behind the economy-saving JobKeeper program , and is now appointed a commissioner on the National COVID-19 Co-ordination Commission , which is focused on the economic impact of the pandemic and our economic recovery from it . <p> Loading <p> Morrison also talked to current ACTU secretary Sally McManus before the announcement and she has said the unions will participate in the process . <p> We live in an age of partisanship and social media-fired divisiveness , and we are a people hardened by a decade of toxic , ineffectual politics . For 10 years the prime ministership has been passed around like a plate of lamingtons ( although rather more violently than that ) and positive reform has been elusive . <p> No one could blame Australian voters for being a little cynical that a government that dishes out sports funding according to self-interest , and puts out an energy policy that fails to mention meaningful emissions reduction , has good intentions when it comes to something as swampy as industrial relations reform . But here lies the potential upside of Morrison 's policy blankness . <p> As I 've noted many times in this column , Morrison 's government was elected last year with a scant policy agenda . This was a lazy position for any political party , and scandalous when you consider the urgency of the climate change disaster we face . <p> But when it comes to an area as fractious as industrial relations , Morrison at least has little personal baggage to bring to the negotiating table . Sure , the party he leads has a full matching set of it , in the form of the ghost of WorkChoices -- famously triple-murdered by then opposition leader Tony Abbott in 2010 when he declared it " dead , buried and cremated " . <p> Loading <p> But Morrison himself is a transactional politician , a pragmatist who is socially conservative but not particularly ideological . He has probably noticed that his personal approval ratings have been one of the only things improved by the pandemic . It might well have occurred to him , and his advisers , that Australians have responded well to the bipartisanship and spirit of co-operation that has imbued his leadership throughout the crisis . <p> Morrison 's relative blankness as Prime Minister -- Australians elected him without having had much of a look at him -- also means he has to make his mark somehow . This could be one way -- leveraging the co-operation of the pandemic into political success for himself . <p> Who knows if the industrial reform process Morrison has kicked off will amount to anything substantial , let alone anything good . At the moment , all it amounts to is the promise of some talks , with no other detail . <p> But it is an interesting starting point -- the idea that non-ideological co-operation might deliver political success of the kind that wins elections . <p> Jacqueline Maley is a senior journalist , columnist and former Canberra press gallery sketch writer for The Sydney Morning Herald .
