<h> The political art of not wasting a crisis , or getting wasted by it <p> There is a misapprehension that Winston Churchill said it , but actually the saying " Never waste a good crisis " is not a quote from the big man . It is an example of " Churchillian drift " , the phenomenon where aphorisms from obscure people , or of unknown origin , are misattributed to major historical figures . <p> That 's V for victory , while it lasted ... Winston Churchill . <p> Be warned -- the International Churchill Society polices such slips very sternly . <p> Actually , it was Rahm Emanuel , chief of staff to former US president Barack Obama , who said it -- about the global financial crisis . " You never want a serious crisis to go to waste , " he told a Wall Street Journal forum in 2008 . " And what I mean by that is , it 's an opportunity to do things you think you could not do before . " <p> Australia 's jobless figures out in the past week were devastating , a statistical rendering of the stubborn Centrelink queues that now form a regular part of the urban backdrop . The people in my suburb bring their own milk crates to sit on , and have started wearing coats in the chilly mornings . <p> Advertisement <p> Things that were unthinkable mere months ago -- soup kitchens for out-of-work hospitality workers , the spike in calls to the Kids Helpline , beer being poured down drains because the pubs have been closed -- do not even make front page news . Watching the nightly bulletins requires a strong constitution or a stiff drink . <p> That 's why the idea that something good might come of all this , that the senseless waste might actually bear some fruit , is so appealing . <p> Illustration : Reg LynchCredit : <p> So , what signs do we have that the government is using the pandemic crisis , and not wasting it ? <p> Certainly , the Coalition is using the fog of war created by the crisis to continue evading scrutiny on the sports rorts affair . No wastage there . <p> Last week the Australian National Audit Office presented new evidence that Scott Morrison 's office had asked then-sports minister Bridget McKenzie to seek his authority for the sports grants program which gave away so much of its $100 million to marginal seats the Coalition was targeting in the 2019 election . Asked by a journalist if he had misled Parliament , the " daggy dad " mask fell , and it was back to the Morrison of old . <p> Loading <p> " No , " he snapped , and could n't resist adding a dig : " It 's good to see the Canberra press gallery is back to politics as usual " . <p> Home Affairs Minister Peter Dutton is n't wasting his crisis , either . He used the return to Parliament to table extraordinary legislation giving ASIO the power to question children as young as 14 , the power to put individuals under surveillance without a warrant , and a sweeping power to prevent a person from contacting a lawyer while being questioned by ASIO . <p> You have to marvel , really , at Dutton 's ambition . These are the powers typical of a military dictatorship , or a totalitarian state . Let 's wait while all the usual libertarian bloviators about personal freedoms mount their campaign to prevent this egregious erosion of civil liberties . We might need a milk crate -- we could be waiting a long time . <p> Morrison 's government came to power with very little in the way of an economic agenda , beyond tax cuts , which it legislated with laudable speed , and the promise of a surplus . Its surplus -- which Treasurer Josh Frydenberg ( a big Churchill fan ) announced last year before it existed -- is now on the slagheap of broken dreams . <p> Loading <p> Frydenberg 's ministerial statement to Parliament last week had a hole at its centre where the deficit number should have gone . Conditions are too volatile to make any reliable projections yet . <p> Frydenberg threaded the speech with what shadow treasurer Jim Chalmers called " the usual tired Liberal branch meeting spiel , pasted in there awkwardly " . Chalmers would say that , but it is true that talk of " maximising personal choice " and " unleashing the power of dynamic , innovative , and open markets " seemed odd for the moment . The government says it will not rush reform , that it must shore up confidence first . Fair enough . <p> NSW Treasurer Dominic Perrottet has long been lobbying to end stamp duty -- an inefficient tax -- and replace it with land tax , a " good " tax because it goes after wealth , not income . He is using the crisis to push the case for that worthwhile reform . <p> At a federal level , the government had no pre-pandemic plans or dreams for major reform , none it had shared with taxpayers , anyway . That was a pretty lazy position for any government to be in , and made you wonder why any of its members wanted to be in Canberra , beyond the retention of power for its own sake . <p> Loading <p> But perhaps it is a good thing the government did n't get elected on the strength of any great reform platform . Chances are it would have to be jettisoned now , anyway . Perhaps this blank slate will mean Morrison and his cabinet returns to the experts in Treasury , the Productivity Commission , and even in the Climate Change Authority , to craft a way out . <p> If it listens to the usual voices in big business , who are pushing for industrial relations deregulation at a time of horrendous employment insecurity , or who demand " cheap " energy as we stare down the reality of a climate crisis that has not been magically erased by the pandemic , then it will be wasting its crisis . <p> Likewise , if it does n't bring forth strong policy to target the women and young people who have been disproportionately hurt by the horrendous job losses , it will be wasting its crisis . <p> Here is something Churchill really did say : " Politics is more dangerous than war , for in war you are only killed once . " <p> The government faces far more danger as it seeks a graceful exit from the crisis than it ever did in the belly of it . No one knew that better than Churchill -- he got voted out after World War II . <p> Jacqueline Maley is a senior journalist , columnist and former Canberra press gallery sketch writer for The Sydney Morning Herald .
