But the attitudes and fears that have emerged in the battle against it can be equally unsettling . <p> The world is being upended by something unseen and aggressive . Is it any wonder unease and disorientation are the results ? <p> " There 's this invisible threat of a virus , but then there are visible threats of me losing my job or my livelihood or the economic shutdown or my mental health because I 'm isolated , " said Lindsey Root Luna , a clinical psychologist who teaches at Hope College in Michigan . <p> " So I look at the fact I have both a visible threat and an invisible one . And people tend to respond to the visible threat . " <p> The coronavirus is an apt adversary for our times . It 's a specific threat , yet its invisibility has made it a Petri dish where people can cultivate all kinds of misinformation . <p> Already , this was an era when entertainment and politics screamed : even if you can see it , do n't necessarily believe it . <p> Shaped by visuals and concrete things and loud volumes , the United States is today at a particularly weird juncture when people with agendas use loud volumes and sophisticated visuals to call the most concrete things into doubt . <p> On top of that , enough is coming at people already that many resist or reject storylines involving more abstract or unseen forces - virus , political manipulation , climate change . <p> " We are evolved to respond to threats that exist - a car crash , a punch in the face - that we can perceive , " said Alix Spiegel , cohost and coproducer of the NPR program Invisibilia , which tells stories that add structure to things we ca n't see . <p> " But when they exist on a physical scale or a time scale that 's hard for us to relate to then we 're much , much worse at coming up with the appropriate actions . " <p> This is evident in reactions to antivirus measures by authorities . <p> " From a visible perspective nothing 's changed : very few of us actually see someone suffering from coronavirus , ' ' said Adam Kotsko , who teaches at North Central College in Illinois . He 's the author of The Prince of This World , a history of one of humankind 's most notorious unseen adversaries - the devil . <p> " Society , " Kotsko said , " has been thrown into upheaval for no visible , apparent reason . ' ' <p> It has meant many things over the years as new forms of the invisible emerged , such as the harnessed atom , the radiation it produced and the Cold War terror it spread . <p> But invisibility often stands in for a menacing adversary , as with Invasion of the Body Snatchers , the mid-1950s novel and movie about aliens undetectably replacing humans that evoked postwar fears of unseen threats . <p> At the same time , the invisible threat of lethal contagious disease was receding as vaccinations became commonplace . Smallpox was eradicated , polio conquered . Measles , mumps and rubella were largely vanquished . So their effects - disfigured children , firsthand knowledge of the dead - naturally became less visible . <p> Today , given that many people 's connections to Covid-19 victims are still indirect , the unseen nature of virus unease seems more analogous to the fear of ghosts , said Marc Longenecker , a film scholar at Wesleyan University in Connecticut who has written about invisibility in movies . <p> " The chasm between seeing and knowing is ever-widening in the 21st century , ' ' Akiko Busch wrote in How to Disappear : Notes on Invisibility in a Time of Transparency . <p> One thing , though , is both seeable and knowable at this important moment : to push this threat away will require thorough research and the microscopic vaccine we hope it produces . <p> Until then the Walter Johnson rule still prevails . Even knowing precisely what you ca n't see does n't mean you 'll be able to hit it .
