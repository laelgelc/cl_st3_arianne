<p> Over the past few weeks there have been many reports of localised air quality improvements as the world has locked down to combat the coronavirus pandemic . <p> However , recent data from the United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Association ( NOAA ) shows global carbon dioxide ( CO2 ) levels rising sharply . <p> In April 2020 the average concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere was 416.21 parts per million ( ppm ) , the highest since measurements began in Hawaii in 1958 . <p> Furthermore , ice core records indicate that such levels have not been seen in the last 800,000 years . <p> The United Nations Environment Programme 's ( UNEP ) World Environment Situation Room shows a sharp increase in CO2 concentrations of more than 100 ppm since March 1958 . <p> The curve shows expected seasonal fluctuations : the northern hemisphere has a greater land mass than the southern hemisphere and more vegetation-absorbing CO2 during the summer . Global CO2 concentrations peak in May at the end of the northern hemisphere winter . <p> Then , as photosynthesis takes place and new foliage appears , it absorbs CO2 , lowering concentrations by about 7.5 ppm until October . During the northern hemisphere winter , the Earth has less photosynthesis activity , so CO2 concentrations go up until the next cycle . <p> Source : UN NOAA <p> However , owing to anthropogenic CO2 emissions ( emissions from human activities ) , CO2 concentrations are not only increasing , but accelerating . <p> The following graph shows the difference in CO2 concentrations between one month and the same month one year before ( e.g. + 2.88 ppm between April 2020 and April 2019 ) . <p> Source : UN NOAA <p> It shows that while the increase over one year was around 0.9 ppm during the 1960s it rose to 2.4 ppm on average during the 2010-2019 period . There is a clearly accelerating upward trend . <p> The long-term view <p> Using ice-core records , it is possible to measure CO2 trapped in deep Antarctic ice going back 800,000s years . We have never in the last 800,000 years reached 416 ppm . <p> Given that the Homo sapiens appeared about 300,000 years ago , and the first trace of Homo sapiens sapiens ( also called anatomically modern humans ) dates to 196,000 years ago , no individual of our species has experienced such high levels of CO2 . <p> " This is , of course , of great concern for our climate , and demonstrates , yet again , that urgent action is needed to reduce our greenhouse gas emissions . To keep average global warming to 1.5 ? C we need to achieve net zero emissions by 2040 ( 2055 at the latest ) , " said Pascal Peduzzi , Director of UNEP/GRID-Geneva and Programme Manager of the World Environment Situation Room . <p> These results may come as a surprise to those who optimistically assume that Covid-19 will reduce total global emissions . <p> While it is true that vehicular and air traffic , as well as industrial activity , has reduced sharply in most parts of the world since January 2020 , this is not the case with our electricity supply : 64% of the global electricity energy mix comes from fossil fuels ( coal : 38% , gas : 23% , oil : 3% ) , according to the World Energy Outlook 2019 . <p> Heating systems have been functioning as before Covid-19 . None of the fundamentals have changed ( such as the shift to renewable energy , public transport , deforestation ) . <p> Forest fires and wildfires that are increasing in likelihood and severity due to climate change continue to affect swathes of Brazil , Honduras , Myanmar , Thailand , and Venezuela , each fire emitting large amounts of additional CO2 . <p> " Without fundamental shifts in global energy production , we should have no reason to expect a lasting reduction in emissions , " said UNEP climate change expert Niklas Hagelberg . <p> " Covid-19 instead provides us a chance to take stock of the risks we are taking in our unsustainable relationship with our environment and seize the opportunity to rebuild our economies in more environmentally responsible ways .
