<p> PAT O'Doherty has seen a world of change in his four-decade career with the Electricity Supply Board - and believes the greatest change of them all is coming in the wind . <p> O'Doherty , ESB 's chief executive since 2011 , joined the then-State monopoly straight out of University College Dublin as an engineering graduate in 1981 , when Ireland 's power plants mostly generated electricity by burning oil . In those days , coal and gas beckoned as the fossil fuels of the future - with coal the cheaper alternative , gas the cleaner . <p> Fast-forward to 2020 when , for the first time , following years of investment , wind farms have overtaken gas-fuelled plants as the top source for electricity in the State . <p> Solar panels in bogland fields are planned to replace the industrial cutting and burning of turf , which officially ends on New Year 's Eve . Reliance on coal has fallen nearly to zero . Scientists and engineers are working to develop a carbon-free successor to non-renewable gas - possibly hydrogen , the most common chemical in existence . <p> " How we generate electricity in this country is going to change beyond recognition within the coming decade , " O'Doherty says from his Castleknock home , where he 's been working since mid-March , when most of the ESB 's 7,000-strong workforce switched to remote working . <p> " The ESB was established to modernise Ireland through electrification starting in 1927 . That deep sense of purpose is part of our DNA , and it shows in our ambition to lead Ireland to clean energy , " he says . <p> " We want to de-carbonise electricity and through it large swathes of the economy . " <p> The ESB spends more than ? 1bn annually maintaining and modernising the State 's electricity connections and on developing new sources of power . The biggest of its 23 wind farms - Grousemount , midway between Kilgarvan , Co Kerry and Coolea , Co Cork - joined the grid in the first quarter of 2020 . <p> By no coincidence , that quarter marked the first time wind overtook gas as Ireland 's top source of electricity . <p> Grousemount 's final work on eight of its 38 turbines was halted until May 18 because of Covid-19 disruption to construction . The full power from the site - billed as enough for 70,000 homes , farms and firms - should be plugged into the grid by mid-June . <p> " Way out off the west coast of Ireland , the wind resources out in the Atlantic are - if we can harness them - ten times what Ireland needs . So Ireland could become an exporter of renewable power , " he says . <p> ESB engineers currently are learning how to construct and operate offshore turbines in Scotland , where ESB International is partnering with the UK 's EDF Energy to build the Neart na Gaoithe 448 MW wind farm . <p> " That will become the generation technology of the future in Ireland , " he says . <p> Other ESB engineers are learning how to produce power from waste wood in a joint venture with the UK government 's Green Investment Bank at Tilbury Port in Essex , although the ESB 's five-year investment in that biomass fuel project has not gone smoothly , producing a ? 34m writeoff last year . <p> " It 's new technology and we ran into plant difficulties in terms of the technology . We 're close enough to getting them resolved now , " O'Doherty says . " It 's up and running . We 're working our way through a recovery plan there . " <p> Technical challenges have been a passion of O'Doherty since he was a boy - the eldest of five brothers - growing up on the Navan Road in a home beside the local GAA pitch . <p> " I always wanted to be an engineer . I was always taking things apart and putting them back together . Sometimes I had parts left over , " he says with a laugh . " The truth be told , I always wanted to become an engineer - not knowing what engineering was . " <p> He remembers a family home with no central heating , only open fires , including in the bedroom - " Mad now , when you think about it ! " - and an extremely personal experience of how utilities worked . <p> " You 'd put a shilling into the gas meter for cooking . When the meter reader would come , he 'd unlock the tray with all the coins on it , sit down at the kitchen table and count out the coins to make sure that the money and meter reconciled . Then I always remember he gave a few bob back to my mum . " <p> He met his future wife , Therese , while still studying at St Declan 's College in Cabra , at an Irish debating competition . <p> " She went to the local convent , I went to the local Christian Brothers , and we were debating against each other . That 's how we met . " When asked how things progressed , he exclaims with a laugh , " Ah here , c'm on ! " and explains : " She asked me out to her debs , and it went from there . " <p> The young couple soon were sharing the bus each morning to Hanlon 's Corner , when he would hop the number 10 bus to UCD and she would walk on to St Patrick 's teachers training college in Drumcondra . <p> He spent the summer of 1980 in San Francisco on a J1 visa , where he split time between playing GAA and pulling four jobs on a construction site , in a department store , in an insurance company , and as an electrician . <p> " America was a great experience , but I 'm a home bird , " he says . " Anyway , I had to come back with money in my pocket for my final year fees . " <p> He and Therese married in 1984 , by which time he was working as a telecommunications engineer at an ESB that had a workforce of 13,500 , nearly double the size of today . <p> He recalls the ESB then as an environment of huge projects , deepening a tradition dating to the ESB 's roots in harnessing the power of the Shannon at Ardnacrusha . <p> His early ESB memories include construction of Ireland 's only coal-fired plant at Moneypoint in Co Clare and its first gas-burning plant in Co Cork to exploit the first offshore methane gas finds . <p> " We had a huge mobilisation of people and machinery in West Clare , then the building of transmission lines up to Dublin for Moneypoint . <p> " We were supposed to build an extension at Poolbeg in Dublin and instead we moved all the power station kit to build Aghada power station in Cork , to take the gas coming ashore from Kinsale . We then converted two stations to gas , North Wall and Poolbeg . " <p> At the time , such moves away from oil were designed to boost energy security following the oil crises and price spikes of the 1970s . " Gas and coal was diversification away all from putting all the eggs in one basket , predominantly oil . This was forward thinking . " <p> O'Doherty has tried to maintain that one-step-ahead approach as he worked his way up the ESB ladder , holding in turn the top spots at three divisions : Networks , Power Generation and International . <p> At Networks he experienced the early 21st-century opening of the energy market here to new entrants needing regulated access to ESB infrastructure . <p> At International he oversaw engineering teams working worldwide , including in Middle East power plants . <p> He says the primary value of overseas work is to build engineering expertise " that we might not be able to maintain only in our own facilities . It 's a window for us into emerging technologies through clients . We form relationships with big suppliers and work in partnership with them in plants . " <p> The ESB first drafted plans for coping with a pandemic following the 2003 spread of the Sars virus . It conducted a company-wide pandemic drill in 2018 . Even before the first Covid-19 case was confirmed here , the ESB already had been planning in February for potential disruption and large-scale remote working . <p> O'Doherty says the ESB always took the threat seriously and ordered critical supplies , including PPE , early . <p> " Everything depends on electricity . Without it you do n't have your mobile , broadband , communications or TV . Above all you do n't have working hospitals and medical facilities . " <p> Its Covid-19 plans keep staff in power plants round the clock and engineers in the field , but work shifts and supply runs to depots are staggered and critical staff are " hermetically sealed " from others , he says . <p> " We 've managed all of that to make sure that this virus did not spread through ESB . " <p> When asked whether he thinks it 's possible to produce a carbon-free energy supply here , as the Government and EU expect should happen by 2050 , he says the virus crisis offers an unlikely lesson - that what seems far-fetched can quickly become reality . <p> " Look at how the whole of society has changed in just eight weeks . Had somebody described this to me at the start of the year , I would n't have believed them . If the past eight weeks has taught us anything , it 's that we need to look at what 's possible with a new set of lenses . " <p> O'Doherty hopes the Government wo n't be forced down the road of austerity again , despite the ballooning deficit , and keeps financing the Climate Action Plan 's 2030 goals . He thinks of the future for his two children and first grandchild born nine months ago . <p> " We need to find a way to keep investment going in energy even though we 're heading into a recession . We need to look at the lessons from the last recession , at cutbacks done out of necessity . If you delay investment , it only becomes a bigger and more expensive hill to climb . <p> " Just look at what Covid-19 has wrought . It 's an early look at what climate change could do to the world . "
