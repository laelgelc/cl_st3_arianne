<h> Nature of hope <p> The fate of fish can speak volumes about what 's happening to our environment , says a leading biodiversity spokesman , Canadian David Suzuki , but most of it is n't good news . <p> Salmon . They feature a bit in David Suzuki 's conversation . This is probably not surprising , given that the fish is in decline , and he is , after all , one of biodiversity 's best-known spokespeople , and also he 's Canadian . He uses the real-life salmon situation to illustrate a couple of parables that neatly sum up his world-view . <p> The first is the fish 's role in its greater ecosystem . Sure , it 's worrying enough that salmon themselves are spawning in ever-declining numbers , but the great Pacific north-west forests are also affected , as it turns out salmon are one of the major sources of nitrogen for the trees . Bears catch , eat and excrete the salmon , spreading the love around . Fewer salmon - or bears - means less nutrition for trees . So , do we need to work harder to understand " the interconnectedness of all things " ? Well , does a bear shit in the woods ? <p> Second , the story of the sockeye salmon speaks volumes about Suzuki 's optimism - or lack thereof . After normal runs of 30-40 million of the fish in the Fraser River dropped to a record low of one million last year , the Canadian Government set up an inquiry . This year , the run was 35 million , the highest in years . Great news , surely ? " I do n't believe for a minute sockeye salmon have recovered , " the geneticist says . After so many years ' decline , this is only a blip , he suspects . <p> So do not be fooled by the title of Suzuki 's latest book . From The Legacy : An Elder 's Vision for Our Sustainable Future , " Elder " is about all a reader could take at face value . At 74 , Suzuki refers to himself unsentimentally as being " in the death zone " . But as a summary of his life spent devoted to speaking out for the environment , the title is misleadingly optimistic . This slim volume , based on his last lecture as professor at the University of British Columbia in 2009 , is full of gloomy signs . So when he says , " All I have left is hope " , it does n't sound very ... hopeful . <p> Still , if he 's in the death zone , he has hardly succumbed to querulousness . On the phone from Melbourne , where it 's 8.00am , Suzuki 's familiar warm broadcaster 's tones are as vital as ever . Indeed , his great strength has always been as a talker - although his writing ( some dozens of books ) has always been important , speech is his medium . And this morning it 's peppery with anger at the people who 've stood in the way of progress on his life 's work - protecting biodiversity and arresting climate change . <p> His visit to Australia and New Zealand this month was to be his last - he has amended that , he says now , to " latest " . " I do n't want to be like Cher , always announcing my last tour . " Australia , especially , would have been hard to say goodbye to . He has visited the country most years since 1988 and refers to it as a " second home " after Canada . <p> Australia is the seat of his climate-change activism . " In ' 88 , I still called it a slow-motion catastrophe . I really thought it was going to take 100 years or more , so we had time to do something and I was more focused on issues of deforestation and ocean pollution . I came here ... and met scientists who showed me what was going on , and that was the first time I realised we could n't wait around , we had to do something . " <p> Suzuki writes he was named David by his father " who feared I 'd be a small man surrounded by Caucasian Goliaths " . As a second-generation Canadian born to Japanese parents , Suzuki was interned with the rest of his family during World War II in a paradisaical area that is now British Columbia 's Valhalla Provincial Park . Yet although the scene of his prison , such as it was , has remained unscathed , the swamp near his childhood home , where he discovered a fascination with insects , is now paved , turned into a parking lot . Environmentalism is probably the only choice when your life turns into a Joni Mitchell song , but the die had been cast anyway . <p> Alongside his academic career as professor of genetics , Suzuki made his television debut in 1970 , as host of the kids ' show Suzuki on Science . Since then , a series of shows made for public broadcasting companies in the US and Canada has emphasised biodiversity and sustainability . <p> The same year as his first visit to Australia , a group of climatologists gathered in Ontario and called for emissions reductions of 20% in 15 years . " A big call in 1988 , " Suzuki notes , but nothing compared with the kinds of cuts we 're facing more than 20 years down the track , as greenhouse gases have only soared . <p> Then , though , Suzuki was largely untroubled . " I really thought we could get that to be accepted as the big target . " It 's barely 10 past eight in the morning and Suzuki is approaching full thunder at this point - he lays the blame for the lack of action at the feet of the oil companies . " I never imagined the fossil fuel industry itself would begin a massive multimillion-dollar campaign of deception ... They 've been unbelievably successful . " <p> It is , he says , " unforgivable ... an intergenerational crime " , and he holds out hope that those oil companies will eventually find themselves facing the kinds of lawsuits Big Tobacco has had to grapple with . <p> His anger at his own country 's leadership is likewise plain . Canadian Prime Minister Stephen Harper has reneged on the country 's Kyoto commitments , which Canada ratified in 2002 , instead ramping up investment into extracting oil from oil sands , about the filthiest method of getting black gold as is possible . " We have had , " Suzuki says , " the three amigos - John Howard , George Bush and Stephen Harper - two of the amigos are gone , but there 's still Mr Harper carrying on in the Bush tradition . He 's just as bad , or worse , than George Bush . " Howard and Bush both refused to ratify the Kyoto Protocol - whereas Howard 's successor , Kevin Rudd , ratified the Protocol in 2007 , Suzuki believes the Kyoto process to be irretrievable broken , anyway . <p> " Kyoto is dead . Clearly dead ... We 've had , for four years , a government that said we do n't give a damn about Kyoto , we 're not even going to try to reduce our emissions . There 's no way to enforce Kyoto , so it 's got no teeth . And when you have an economic downturn , everyone says , well , we could n't possibly do that now , it 's too expensive . " <p> But , he writes , he can see why science communication may have failed to ? capture the public imagination , too . So many of his colleagues , he says , came into science out of wonder at their surroundings , yet " as we write up our reports , we expunge all trace of passion and emotion in the name of objectivity . Yet it is more important than ever to maintain that sense of wonder , awe and reverence as we approach the natural world ... " <p> His rejection of scientific language can lead to some slightly fruity writing at times , but he also has a gift for bringing the big picture together , as he makes the links in chains such as his salmon-in-the-woods example . His Suzuki Foundation pays as much attention to biodiversity as it does to climate change , for the very reason you ca n't separate the two . <p> As the UN Convention on Biodiversity meets in Japan throughout October , members of the Convention , including New Zealand , will be working towards targets to protect areas of land and sea . But , says Suzuki unsurprisingly , " I 'm not optimistic about that " . The destruction of the world 's biodiversity is " terrifying " , and should be screamed about from banner headlines the world over . Indeed , even conservative figures are frightening : no more fish in the sea for human consumption by 2050 ( fancy jellyfish ? ) . Forty per cent of all plant species becoming extinct . A third of all amphibians , likewise . At the top of the food chain , our correct reaction should be worry . <p> " By 2050 , " he says , " my grandchildren are still going to be young . I was just in Japan and I was saying can you imagine Japanese people without fish ? Why is n't Japan up in arms and leading the global fight to protect the oceans ? And they just say well , look at the markets . The biggest market in the world is in Tokyo and there 's lots of fish there . So they do n't feel there 's any problem . Of course , 100 years ago , that market was filled with fish that were from Japan . Now that market is filled with fish that have come from all over the world . There 's still lots of fish there , so the Japanese do n't see it . " <p> Suzuki has a habit of pre-empting any criticism - he almost immediately acknowledges his guilt over his travel carbon footprint - in the same way he pre-empts any attempt to glean some good news from him . He talks about Walmart , for instance , and that company 's efforts to become a green retail giant . It has pledged to be 100% renewable in its energy use , and waste-free in its packaging . And " good on them " notes Suzuki . But - there 's always a but - Walmart 's drive will always be growth , and " we know Walmart ca n't continue the way it is , it 's just unsustainable " . <p> You have to wonder , given his decidedly gloomy take on the state of the world , whether The Legacy , with its veneer of optimism , is a way to fake it until he makes it - a kind of " if you write it , it will come " attempt to force a happy ending . If so , he 's not the only one . Australian environmentalist Tim Flannery 's latest book , Here on Earth , is a marked departure from his critical The Weather Makers , focusing on the wonders of nature , rather than the destruction we 've wrought , as in his previous books . <p> Then again , says Suzuki , he 's currently reading Australian ethicist Clive Hamilton 's Requiem for a Species . It , too , is a step away from Hamilton 's Scorcher , a climate-change polemic that took politicians and industry to task , instead looking at why we 've failed to act as we should . Hamilton still , in the face of all his own evidence , makes the argument for hope . " This is a very depressing book " is Suzuki 's verdict . Boy , is it . " But I have not found anything that I disagree with so far . I just saw Clive in Canberra last week and he really thinks , as does Jim Lovelock , that it 's too late , that we 've passed too many tipping point , to go back . And there 's a lot of support for that . " In fact , most climate scientists believe we 're long past a safe level of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere , 350 parts per million , now fast approaching 400ppm . At that point , at warming of over 2 ? C , creating runaway changes in the climate , is a real risk . And no major emitter is committed to the kinds of cuts needed to avert that . <p> Super . But that , at least , is where Suzuki parts company from his colleagues ever so slightly and finds a tiny nugget of hope in stories like the sockeye salmon 's return . He may not believe they 've bounced back , but it shows that nature is unpredictable - and it just might - might - occasionally be unpredictable in good ways . <p> " I believe we do n't know enough to say it 's too late . There are many surprises nature can offer us . A lot of them will be bad , but I think there would be good surprises as well . If we were able to slow down and give nature a chance , I think she would be far more generous than we deserve . So that 's the hope that I cling to . We do n't know enough to say with certainty that it 's too late , so we have to just act now , as heroically as we can . " <p> At nearly 75 , he says it 's time to admit this really is a life 's work , hence his insistence that this is no " last " tour - " my wife has convinced me to stop using the word ' retirement ' . Who knows how much longer I 'll last - but I want to be able , if I 'm conscious as I 'm dying , to look at my grandchildren and say I did the best I could . And that 's all I can do . It 's not about retiring - as long as I 'm healthy , I 've got to keep trying to carry the message on . "
