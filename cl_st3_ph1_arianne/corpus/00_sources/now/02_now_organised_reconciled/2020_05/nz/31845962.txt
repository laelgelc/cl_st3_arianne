<p> The Transport Agency analysis estimates the benefits per year at $24m , three-quarters of that from cutting vehicle operating costs , and the rest split evenly between cutting vehicle emissions and the health benefits to cyclists . <p> The analysis puts the cost of footpath crash deaths at $4.4m and injuries $9.5m , or $14m in total per year - so $10m less than the benefits . <p> It reviewed several studies , most from overseas . <p> " There is little published data available regarding the effects of footpath cycling on pedestrian safety , " it concluded . <p> Then added : " Permitting cycling on footpaths is not likely to lead to a substantial increase in casualties . " <p> Blind Citizens New Zealand lobby group said all this implied it was " acceptable to have injuries " . <p> A transport planner , Bevan Woodward of the national lobby group Movement , said the analysis failed to account for the advent of e-scooters and e-bikes , which are heavier and faster than regular bikes . <p> Blind Citizens president Jonathan Godfrey was dismissive of the analysis but worried at its influence on the reform of road rules . <p> It failed to account for the deterrence effect on those who had no choice but to use footpaths , he said . <p> " If our needs as people who ca n't choose to go somewhere else off the footpath are not adequately catered for , then New Zealand is losing . <p> " We should have learned over the eight weeks of lockdown how important footpaths are for people walking their streets , " Dr Godfrey said . <p> " And when the rest of New Zealand gets back in their cars , disabled people will still be using those footpaths and we want to use them safely . " <p> The Transport Agency said the analysis assumed footpath riders obeyed the speed limits and took due care . <p> It belatedly added the analysis last month to its list of public documents , available as part of the consultation around its Accessible Streets reform proposals . Consultation closes on Wednesday 20 May . <p> Dr Godfrey said the reforms were good in parts , but the footpath plan starkly illustrated the lack of direct consultation with disabled people . <p> However , the agency said the disability sector was consulted , with its views included in the preliminary regulatory impact statement , which was also publicly available . <p> The government said the proposals responded to parents ' calls for children to be allowed to ride on footpaths . <p> The consultation was to see if footpath riding should continue to be banned , limited to children , or open to all at low speeds , and with give way rules clarified , it said . <p> The Transport Agency has had more than 1000 submissions , and says it will analyse them all . <p> " This will include putting together a disability impact statement which will summarise the views of the disability sector and how the proposals may impact them , an update to our regulatory impact statement , and a re-write of our cost benefit analysis informed by the feedback , " it said . <p> The NZTA cost-benefit analysis was done last August and cites a dozen studies - 10 from overseas , and eight of them more than a decade old . <p> One done in New Zealand in 2016 , echoed several other studies in pointing out there are probably many more footpath crashes than are reported , and that there is a lack of footpath crash data overall . What data there was showed " pedestrians typically sustain more injuries than cyclists in a pedestrian/cycle crash " . <p> An Australian study found just four deaths from bike-walker crashes from 2001-2006 , and all were of pedestrians , not cyclists . <p> Another Australian study , in 2018 , found there were " risks of negative safety impacts " for pedestrians . <p> However , a 2009 study - yet another in Australia - said the " perception of danger " was much greater than the actual risk , after recording only five near-misses having observed 50,000 walkers and 12,000 cyclists . <p> The cost-benefit analysis concluded from the studies that " cycling on footpaths is likely to have a very minor effect on pedestrians as cyclists travel at slow speeds and injuries are likely to be low " . <p> " The literature review indicates that given the low speeds involved there are likely to be no fatal injuries , " it said . <p> Even so , it said it would adopt a conservative crash estimate : That one cyclist a year would be killed in a footpath crash , and 12 would suffer a serious or 57 a minor injury ; and that five pedestrians would suffer serious injuries and 10 minor injuries , but no pedestrians would be killed . <p> Bevan Woodward rejects that . <p> " NZTA do n't have the research on allowing e-bikes on our footpaths and the research they 're relying upon looked at schoolchildren , up to 12 years old , cycling on our footpaths . " <p> The analysis estimates cyclists would ride an extra 2.3 million kilometres , with a health benefit , and an $18m benefit in lower vehicle operating costs as cars made fewer trips . <p> But Woodward said that could only come at a cost to garages and service stations , which should cancel out any benefit . <p> Such analyses worked for businesses set on maximising profit , but were not for government agencies measuring impact on societies , or on climate change , he said . <p> " It 's fundamentally flawed . <p> " The government 's road safety policy Road to Zero states that no deaths are acceptable . However , this cost benefit analysis trades off the predicted deaths and injuries to pedestrians . "
