<h> Climate change : coronavirus exposes flaws in recycling scheme <p> Reporters Chloe Ranford and Alice Angeloni look at what types of plastic can and ca n't be recycled in Marlborough . <p> Coronavirus exposed the cracks in New Zealand 's recycling system , but it also provided a taste of what would happen if New Zealand had to deal with its own waste inside a national bubble . <p> The lockdown threw the system into disarray and exposed how much our cities vary - something waste researchers say must change to keep recycling viable . <p> Wellington City Council last emptied recycle bins on March 25 and will resume on May 12 . Despite some initial confusion , Auckland Council recycled throughout the lockdown . Meanwhile , Christchurch City Council collected recyclables under alert level 4 , but the material went to landfill . Hastings , Napier and New Plymouth councils resumed kerbside pick-ups on April 27 . <p> Just as each council chooses whether they accept aerosols , bottle tops and soft plastics , each had to decide in two days whether to maintain or suspend recycling services , from kerbside collections to sorting and processing . <p> Christchurch City Council resource recovery manager Ross Trotter says collections continued during lockdown to avoid the health risks of stockpiling . " The quality of some recyclable material will also degrade if not appropriately stored or stockpiled for extended periods .. " <p> But in Wellington , collection and processing methods put staff at too great a risk of contracting Covid-19 , Wellington Mayor Andy Foster says . " Much of the sorting of the items is done by hand -- either at the truck for glass , or at the Oji sorting plant in Seaview , " he says . <p> Wellington City Council 's waste operations manager , Emily Taylor-Hall , fears the capital is developing bad habits , as recycling bins left on streets during the lockdown have been spotted with some concerning additions : " they now have glass in them , or grass clippings or dog poo bags " . <p> These would likely contaminate the recyclable materials in their bin and any neighbours ' -- a whole truckload may need to be dumped at the landfill , she says . " There will be lots of people who 've been very diligent about keeping their recycling really clean and it would be a shame for their efforts to go to waste . " <p> Stacy Squires/Stuff <p> Though some paper and light card is recycled in New Zealand , approximately 60 per cent is shipped to other countries . <p> PAPER THIN <p> Even if clean and lovingly sorted , some plastics and paper in your kerbside bin may end up in landfill , but only partly thanks to Covid-19 . <p> All the paper and light card collected by Auckland Council in April was dumped in landfill -- not for health and safety reasons , but because the council could n't find anyone to take it . <p> Christchurch is currently storing paper , but warned some may have to go to landfill . <p> For the capital , Taylor-Hall says the issue with paper and light card " has been brewing for a while " . <p> Since late 2019 , international paper recyclers have tightened their standards . Previously , a bale of paper was accepted as long as there was less than 5 per cent soiled or non-paper items ( known as contamination ) . Now that number 's down to 0.5 per cent , she says . <p> " You 're looking at introducing five or six extra steps , just to get to that low contamination level , " she says . <p> Auckland Council waste solutions general manager Parul Sood says the additional sorting comes at an extra cost to ratepayers . " People tend to underestimate the impact of lazy recycling , " she says . <p> There are sorting machines able to scan each item ( with a laser or camera ) as it moves along a conveyor , determine what it 's made from , and sort it accordingly . These are capable of meeting the new contamination standards , but do n't come cheap . <p> Recycling company Green Gorilla recently opened a $9.5 million sorting plant in Auckland with this optical sorting technology . The government put nearly $3.1m towards the facility . <p> There was a small hitch with the opening , with operations beginning as the country went into lockdown , chief executive Elaine Morgan says . " We were probably operating at one-fifth of our capacity . " <p> The system sorts dry waste so thoroughly customers can use one bin for all recyclables and non-recyclables , Morgan says . " They do n't have to have all these different bins . " <p> However , Green Gorilla only sorts commercial and industrial waste -- not residential . Morgan says commercial waste is often cleaner and more desirable to recyclers than the material coming from residents ' kerbside bins . <p> Even before lockdowns cut off international markets , onshore recycling facilities were floated as one much-needed solution to New Zealand 's recycling woes . <p> To help fund new equipment and facilities , the Ministry for the Environment is proposing to increase the waste disposal levy -- currently $10 per tonne of rubbish -- to a maximum of $60 per tonne of waste sent to dumps , plus expand the number of landfills it applies to . <p> Andy Jackson/Stuff <p> Due to international lockdowns and tightening standards , some recyclable paper and light card is being sent to landfill . <p> That 'll make it costlier to throw stuff away , boosting the financial viability of recycling , and put more money into central and local government coffers to spend on projects like the Green Gorilla sorting plant . <p> Associate Minister for the Environment Eugenie Sage ? says the current levy is used to boost onshore recycling volumes , but more cash is needed . " There 's a big infrastructure hole because of underinvestment in the past . " <p> Sage says Covid-19 has caused some delays , but Cabinet committees are now meeting regularly . " I will be asking Cabinet colleagues to make decisions on the landfill levy before the end of July , " she says . <p> In the meantime , Wellington City Council is currently paying a fibre processing plant to recycle the paper from the city 's kerbside bins to avoid it going to waste . <p> Chris Harrowell/Stuff <p> The government is proposing to increase the waste levy , which will make it more expensive to send rubbish to landfill . <p> OILY SITUATION <p> There 's a similar problem with plastics , now exacerbated by Covid-19 . <p> The cost of oil turned negative in May -- meaning producers paid companies to take it off their hands -- as international and domestic travel waned . <p> As long as packaging plants are open to take advantage of the oil price , virgin plastic has become that much cheaper . <p> But , even before the Covid crisis recycling certain types of plastic wasfinancially unviable . Taylor-Hall says the plastics known as 1 ( PET ) , 2 ( HDPE ) and 5 ( polypropylene ) are more easily recycled . <p> But outside the soft plastic recycling scheme , Taylor-Hall says there 's no demand for " low-value " plastics -- including 4 , but also 3 ( PVC ) , 6 ( polystyrene ) and 7 ( all others ) -- from national or international recyclers . <p> It 's a confusing system for residents , so last year the government kicked off some work helping councils to standardise collections . " If across the country we 're collecting everything the same way , then there is certainty for the onshore processors -- in that they know the product they get would be the same regardless of where it comes from , " Taylor-Hall says . <p> Eli Hartman/AP <p> After worldwide travel slowed to a crawl , the supply of oil dramatically outweighed demand , so the price went negative . <p> The associate minister says the government " has flagged " that it 's looking to phase out hard-to-recycle plastics such as PVC ( number 3 ) and expanded polystyrene ( number 6 ) . " Alternatives are readily available , " Sage says . <p> Once all councils have aligned what they will and wo n't accept , it will be much clearer to manufacturers whether their products and packaging can be recycled or must be binned . Recycling information labels could be the first step to ditching low-value plastic . <p> WasteMINZ chief executive Janine Brinsdon ? says waste in some areas dropped during the lockdown . " People were n't able to go out and consume . Organic waste went down dramatically because people did n't want to go to the supermarket , people were using that broccoli or freezing their bread and not wasting it . " <p> The classification of waste collection as an essential service is making residents think about the human beings who pick up their rubbish and recycling each week , Brinsdon says . <p> " I think people are actually taking the time to rinse the milk or to rinse out the cat food before they stick it in the bin . That can get revolting after two weeks , " she says . " There were loads of stories about people leaving little notes on the bins to the bin man saying thanks . " <p> The hope is manufacturers will increasingly design all products to be as repairable and recyclable as possible , Sage says . <p> " We 've got a very long way to go in getting to a genuinely circular economy where people are thinking about what happens to products at the end of their life when they 're designing and manufacturing them . " <p> 123RF <p> The government is designing a bottle return scheme , so consumers get a small payment when they return a container . <p> RECYCLING TIPS <p> - Reduce . It 's energy-intensive to recycle something , so it 's best to say no to recyclable materials if you do n't need them . For example , you could refuse a paper bag or put a no junk mail sign on your letterbox . <p> - Clean . Leftover and spoiled food , drink or cosmetics in containers can leak through and send a whole load of recycling to the dump . Give all items a quick rinse before placing them in your recycling bag or bin . <p> - Toss small items . Even if made from recyclable materials , tiny things such bread tags and paper scraps ca n't be recycled , but they can jam sorting machines . Anything smaller than the palm of your hand is better off in the trash.
