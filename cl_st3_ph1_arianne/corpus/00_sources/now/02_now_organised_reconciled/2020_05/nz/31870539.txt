<h> Covid-19 recovery phase should be transparent and equitable <p> Developing countries like Burkina Faso would may extra support for recovery ( UNICEF photo by Vincent Tremeau ) <p> Since the Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004 , disaster recovery plans are almost always framed with aspirational plans to " build back better . " <p> It is a fine sentiment . We all want to build better societies and economies . <p> But , as the Cheshire Cat tells Alice ( in Alice in Wonderland ) when she is lost , where we ought to go depends very much on where we want to get to . <p> The ambition to build back better therefore needs to be made explicit and transparent as countries slowly re-emerge from their Covid-19 cocoons . <p> Asian Development Bank Criteria <p> The Asian Development Bank attempted last year to define build-back-better aspirations more precisely and concretely . It described four criteria : build back safer , build back faster , build back potential and build back fairer . <p> The first three are obvious . We clearly want our economies to recover fast , be safer and be more sustainable into the future . <p> It is the last objective -- fairness -- that will inevitably be the most challenging long-term goal at both the national and international level . <p> Disproportionate effects <p> Economic fallout from the pandemic is already being experienced disproportionately among poorer households , in poorer regions within countries , and in poorer countries in general . <p> Some governments are aware of this and are trying to ameliorate this brewing inequality . <p> At the same time , it is seen as politically unpalatable to engage in redistribution during a global crisis . Most governments are opting for broad-brush policies aimed at everyone , lest they appear to be encouraging class warfare and division or , in the case of New Zealand , electioneering . <p> In fact , politicians ' typical focus on the next election aligns well with the public appetite for a fast recovery . We know that speedier recoveries are more complete , as delays dampen investment and people move away from economically depressed places . <p> Developing countries like Burkina Faso would may extra support for recovery ( UNICEF photo by Vincent Tremeau ) <p> Speed with safety <p> Speed is also linked to safety . As we know from other disasters , this recovery can not be completed as long as the Covid-19 public health challenge is not resolved . <p> The failure to invest in safety , in prevention and mitigation , is now most apparent in the United States , which has less than 5% of the global population but a third of COVID-19 confirmed cases . Despite the pressure to " open up " the economy , recovery will not progress without a lasting solution to the widespread presence of the virus . <p> Economic potential also aligns with political aims and is therefore easier to imagine . <p> A build-back-better recovery has to promise sustainable prosperity for all . <p> The emphasis on job generation in New Zealand 's recent budget was entirely the right primary focus . Employment is of paramount importance to voters , so it has been a logical focus in public stimulus packages everywhere . <p> Fairness , however , is more difficult to define and more challenging to achieve . <p> While a rising economic tide does not always lift all boats -- as the proponents of growth-at-any-cost sometimes argue -- a low tide lifts none . <p> Many low-wage workers also work in industries that will be experiencing longer-term declines associated with the structural changes generated by the pandemic : the collapse of international tourism , for example , or automation and robotics being used to shorten long and complicated supply chains . <p> Poorer countries hit hard <p> Poorer countries are in the worst position . <p> The lockdowns hit their economies harder , but they do not have the resources for adequate public health measures , nor for assisting those most adversely affected . <p> Worryingly , the international aid system that most poorer countries partially rely on to deal with disasters is not fit for dealing with pandemics . When all countries are adversely hit at the same time their focus inevitably becomes domestic . <p> Very few wealthy countries have announced any increases in international aid . <p> WHO under attack <p> If and when they have , the amounts were trivial -- regrettably , this includes New Zealand . And the one international institution that should have led the charge , the World Health Organisation , is being defunded and attacked by its largest donor , the US . <p> Unlike after the 2004 tsunami , international rescue will be very slow to arrive . <p> One would hope most wealthy countries will be able to help their most vulnerable members . But it looks increasingly unlikely this will happen on an international scale between countries . <p> Without global empathy and better global leadership , the poorest countries and poorest people will only be made poorer by this invisible enemy . <p> Ilan Noy is Professor and Chair in the Economics of Disasters and Climate Change at Victoria University of Wellington .
