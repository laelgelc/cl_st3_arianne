<h> Matakana retiree gave away 141kg of fruit during lockdown <p> Trish Allen fed her Matakana community over 140 kilos of fruit during lockdown , from the 50 trees she planted on her property when she moved there . Trish is a permaculturist and set up the Matakana farmer 's market . <p> When Trish Allen moved to Matakana Village , she planted 50 fruit trees on her quarter acre . <p> During the lockdown , the 71-year-old widow gave away 141kg of fruit , thanks to " a bumper year of apples , pears , bananas , feijoas , persimmons , grapes , tomatoes , zucchinis and greens " . <p> Her front gate became a produce lucky dip , as neighbours dropped off unsolicited macadamias , field mushrooms , eggs , seedlings , hot cross buns , and seaweed , and horse poo in return : " You never knew what you were going to get , it was brilliant ! " <p> Abigail Dougherty/Stuff <p> Trish Allen planted 50 fruit trees on the empty the quatrer-acre she bought after her husband Joe Polaischer passed . <p> Allen , who taught her first zero waste workshop in the ' 80s , said she was a permaculturist and Matakana local long before either became " trendy " . <p> Trish was able to share 141kilograms of free produce with her neighbours in lockdown thanks to what she describes as " a bumper year of apples , pears , bananas , feijoas , persimmons , grapes , tomatoes , zucchinis and greens " . <p> " We found this spot , just up the road from where I am now . It was 50 acres of weed and pest-infested land . And it was cheap . " That land became the Rainbow Valley farm , home to 800 fruit and nut trees , sheep , cattle and pigs . <p> " We arrived here in a house truck like a couple of hippies . The local farmers really looked down on us , but it did n't take very long before they realised that we were serious about growing food , " she said . <p> The couple supplied small shops with their produce , had a lucrative roadside stall , and a table at the popular Matakana Village Farmers Market , which they started , for many years . <p> Now Allen gives or swaps all her produce away . <p> She was one of the first members of the weekly Greenswap , now going strong in its 13th year , where locals trade and gift the excess from their gardens . <p> The Greenswappers put their success down to informality . The weekly meeting ( now limited to 10 people at a time ) is just half an hour and open to everyone . " People can fit it into their day " , after dropping kids to school or on their way to work . <p> Trish often finds other ' green swappers ' have stopped by to drop off their surplus produce at her front gate . Everything is gratefully accepted . <p> No one could meet over lockdown , but they devised an informal exchange . " Someone on the phone would say they 'd really love some apples , so I 'd put them in a box out by the gate and they 'd come and get them , " said Allen . <p> Abigail Dougherty/Stuff <p> A big basket of fresh fejoas . <p> She has made many friends in the community over the years . <p> " I 'm supposed to be retired , " she said , " but I get myself involved in all sorts of things . " <p> Most recently she started the Mahurangi Wastebusters , a charity that has turned two transfer stations into community recycling centres . Allen 's role is engagement and education . She believes the best way for individuals to oppose mega-landfills , like the one proposed for Dome Valley , is to stop creating waste . <p> Abigail Dougherty/Stuff <p> Trish spends more money on her cat 's food at the supermarket than she does on her own . <p> Food waste does n't get the same publicity as plastic , but it hurts our climate just as much . According to Auckland Council , Aucklanders put about 100,000 tonnes of food waste into their rubbish bins each year . <p> All of that food sits in our landfills , emitting methane into the atmosphere . Then we go out and buy more food because we think we do n't have enough , using up more precious resources in the developing countries where it 's produced . <p> It 's a little-known fact that diverting food from landfill is one of the biggest climate change interventions an individual can make . <p> In her own supermarket shop , Allen said she spends more on her cat 's food than she does on her own . <p> Abigail Dougherty/Stuff <p> Trish says she was a zero waster long before it was trendy . She helped to start up the Matakana Village Farmers Market , the local community garden , and the Mahurangi Wastebusters charity . <p> She made quick work of the field mushrooms gifted to her by the nearby farmlands , making them into soup , pasta , and frying them in butter . <p> " Matakana is thought of as a tourist place , but the established residents have a strong culture of growing and sharing food around here , " she said . <p> Allen 's time in lockdown " was n't too bad " , because there was plenty to do in a community garden that she started . " I could ride my bike up there by myself and do my reading and planting . But I did miss my flat whites . My home coffee just did n't cut the mustard . " <p> Asked why she planted so many fruit trees , Allen said because they are perennial : " You plant the tree once , nurture it for the first year of its life and you have food for the next 20-25 years . It pays itself back in spades . <p> " When they all started coming on I actually thought ' oh god , what have I done ? ' I do n't need all this . " <p> But in a friendly and connected community , nothing ever goes to waste .
