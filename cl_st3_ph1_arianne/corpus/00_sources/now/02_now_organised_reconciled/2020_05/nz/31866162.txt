<h> The big picture <p> ' As we hurtle haphazardly through life in the 20th century without consideration as to where we are headed , a red flag has been raised , " writes photographer Jane Dove Juneau in her newest book . We 're familiar with that red flag and the images that go with it - stories about climate change are inevitably illustrated with pictures of parched landscapes , stranded polar bears , extreme weather events , smoke stacks . <p> Listen to the Heartbeat of the Earth , Juneau 's collection of thoughts and images about humans ' impact on the planet , takes a different tack entirely . Her beautiful photos , from New Zealand and elsewhere , instead celebrate the natural landscape and remind us of what we have to save , from Lucy 's Gully in Juneau 's native Taranaki to Yosemite Valley in California , where she lived for many years . <p> She says people often ask her why she did n't choose pictures of pollution to make her point , but " I thought no , you 've got to think of the positive aspect . We have to protect what we have ; we have to look at the beauty that 's here and be aware that things could be changing . " <p> Juneau began her career as a newspaper photographer in New Zealand , before spending 17 years in California 's Eastern Sierra as a freelancer for news agencies and companies like Nike . The idea for Heartbeat began to germinate in 2002 , when two of her friends , National Geographic photographer Galen Rowell and his writer wife , Barbara , were killed in a plane crash on their way back from an assignment in Alaska . <p> At Rowell 's memorial service , Juneau says , she was struck by how much he 'd done , including his book My Tibet with the Dalai Lama . " And at that time I thought it 's time I turned my life around and did something meaningful . " The blank looks she got from people when she talked to them about climate change were a further spur - " they did n't care or they did n't know " - and after seeing the Dalai Lama speak in Auckland in 2007 , she sealed the theme for the book . <p> Though she avoided the usual bad-news pictures for the book , Juneau says that after returning to New Zealand in 2003 after so long in the US , she has noticed changes in the Taranaki landscape . " I live right by the beach at Oakura , and there 's been a definite increase in erosion in the last year or so along the beachfront here . We 've probably lost two or three metres of beachfront in parts of the beach . " Locals who 've been around longer say they 've noticed more erosion in the past six months than in the -previous 20 years . <p> Juneau sent copies of the book to Al Gore , Prince Charles , Barack Obama and Environment Minister Nick Smith , among others - Gore and the Prince of Wales responded with thanks , Obama 's copy was returned for security reasons and , as yet , there 's no word from Smith . When she was writing the book , Juneau was dismayed at the Government 's decision to review the emissions trading scheme ; she 's still waiting to hear what will happen . <p> " Politicians are so afraid to come out and do too much - well , some are . President Obama 's great but it has to have the support of the people . And unless people are vocal and speak out about what they want , perhaps the Government 's not going to act . " <p> But as her pictures suggest , she 's an optimist . " We 've got all the brilliant people in the world with great brains , great ideas - they 've just got to understand they 've got to aim them at the environment instead of making money . " The recession may provide just the opportunity to do that . <p> And as the book shows , water buffalo still cool themselves in Sri Lanka 's Arugam Bay , snow still falls on the Sierra Nevada and the sun still shines on Lucy 's Gully . " I think people have to remember that what we do does matter , " Juneau says . The Dalai Lama 's message in 2007 was just that : change comes from everyone . Juneau 's book is her example . " Every person has a responsibility on the Earth and the choices that you make in your life are important . "
