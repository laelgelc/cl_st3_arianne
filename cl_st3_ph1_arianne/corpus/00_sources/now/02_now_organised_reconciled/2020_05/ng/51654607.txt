<h> Why is part of the Amazon burning ? <p> 08/24/2019 <p> Tell your friends <p> The thousands of fires burning in the Amazon do n't look like the major forest fires of Europe or North America -- instead , they are fueled mainly by branches , vegetation and other byproducts of deforestation in cleared areas , experts say . <p> The dramatic scale of this year 's fires is the result of a significant acceleration of deforestation for the lumber industry , for agriculture or for other human activities . <p> " In the tropics , fire is used extensively in a land-use environment , " said Jeffrey Chambers , a scientist at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and a specialist in rainforests . <p> " It 's how you get rid of your agricultural waste products ... And part of the reason why that works is because those fires do n't generally move into the forest , " he explained . <p> " A tropical rainforest is generally not flammable " because it 's so humid . <p> California has the opposite problem : burning waste is prohibited because the forests are so dry , they could go up in flames at the smallest spark . <p> But in the Amazon , when an area of forest is cleared , the tree trunks are removed and the rest of the vegetation is burned on the spot during the dry season , which lasts from July to November . <p> For farmland , or for prairies , brush and weeds alike are heaped together , waiting for the dry season . That 's what is burning right now . <p> Even when the fire manages to penetrate the dense forest -- called " primary " when it is still untouched -- it usually stays in the vegetation at ground level and generally does not reach the treetops , about 100 feet ( 30 meters ) up . <p> The effect is just as devastating , though , because the tree trunks are damaged at ground level , but the overall image differs vastly from the massive fires that Europeans or Americans are used to seeing . <p> Human use of fire to manage land explains the astronomical number of fires -- more than 75,000 -- recorded by Brazilian authorities since January . <h> Trend reversal <p> The fires have made the deforestation visible , according to Paulo Brando , an assistant professor at the University of California , Irvine and a scientist at the Woods Hole Research Center . <p> " You can see the fires as the final phase of deforestation , " he said . <p> Amazon deforestation began in earnest in the 1970s , reaching its peak rate at the end of the 1990s and the start of the 2000s . <p> In 2004 , about 11,000 square miles ( 28,000 square kilometers ) of forest had been cleared in Brazil alone ( the Amazon spreads over nine countries , but 60 percent of the rainforest is in Brazil ) . <p> Deforestation then slowed down significantly . It picked up again in 2014 , but it never reached the same level as the previous decade . Last year , about 2,900 square miles disappeared , according to Brazil 's National Institute for Space Research ( INPE ) . <p> But the trend reversal is worrisome . In July alone , more than 870 square miles were cleared . <p> Additionally , the dry season is n't over . Will the fires bite into intact forest ? <p> " Right now , we are seeing mostly increases in deforestation-related fires , which may or may not escape into primary forests , depending on how dry it 's going to get in the next few months , " said Brando . <p> And how will that affect climate change ? <p> Forests contain carbon , stored in the trees and vegetation -- to the tune of 459 tonnes per hectare in the Amazon , said Diego Navarrete , a carbon specialist at the NGO The Nature Conservancy . <p> When a tree is cut , the carbon inside will reenter the atmosphere years later , at the end of its use cycle when it decomposes . When vegetation is burned , as is happening now , the carbon enters the atmosphere immediately . <p> In both cases , the carbon will be released . It just takes some simple math to realize that the total for the past few months has already reached hundreds of millions of tonnes of carbon .
