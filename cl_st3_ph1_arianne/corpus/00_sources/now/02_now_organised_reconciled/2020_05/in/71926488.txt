<h> The sound and fury of silence <p> Anti-noise pollution activist Sumaira Abdulali on what the trill of birdsong replacing the noise of traffic says about our degradation of the environment <p> One day , just like that , I wake up unexpectedly to the Bombay of my childhood . So familiar , yet unfamiliar . In 2020 , a dystopian , distorted dream . The full moon sets behind a swaying coconut tree in my window , and lights fluffy clouds red-gold . As summer approaches , the intense fragrance of clustered champa and song of the dayal , sweetest of the songbirds , greets me . A bulbul 's call drowns it out , piercing the still-dark dawn . <p> Forty years ago , my father-in-law , ornithologist Humayun Abdulali , on whose recommendation the Borivali National Park was carved out from the rapidly urbanising city of Bombay -- and who mentored some of today 's best-known environmentalists -- chose this flat in Bandra for the trees and birds surrounding it . For the first time in decades , the now everyday cacophony of Mumbai , one of the noisiest cities in the world , reverts to the sounds of nature , and birdsong dominates once again . This is the dreaded world of coronavirus , where close at hand , my brothers and sisters who make up our workforce , began long marches home , away from the city , in unbearable heat . Hunger and fear . Uncertainty . <p> A car headed nowhere is washed by a security guard who lives here now . The splash of water , the sound of a broom sweeping leaves . Dawn breaks and silhouetted trees come to life , bursting golden peltophorum and dark green leaves . <p> The sound and fury of silence <p> My car has n't been washed in weeks . When the lockdown began , I called my driver , Sanjay Salunke . He has n't honked in the 15 years he has driven for me . He has assisted me in recording noise levels in Mumbai for years . Soft-spoken and gentle , he loves the quiet , too . But now he is shouting over the phone . I struggle to hear him , with the crowd drowning out his voice . In his slum , everyone lives in close proximity , jostling for space , unable to get out . I hear crying children , loud TVs from several directions . " Nothing to do but watch TV , " he says . " The news is so depressing . When will it end ? " <p> I walk down to the kirana shop on the corner . My bag is heavy . I cross the street when a cyclist cautiously approaches , fearful of proximity . I remember my cough from months ago , when coronavirus was still a faraway disease , in faraway Wuhan . The World Health Organisation ( WHO ) now says that many more people have it than is reported , all over the world . Back then , it was just a cough . <p> The sound and fury of silence <p> No one could imagine this dystopian world , calm , beautiful and closed . All is still and peaceful here at this corner , where I once met ornithologist Salim Ali , my uncle , every day after school , binoculars around his neck . Mangalore-tile roofed bungalows , gardens without walls , a twisting narrow road , mangoes and gulmohar . A kingfisher flashes past , jewelbright . " There ! " he raises his binoculars . <p> At this same corner where Ali once lived , surrounded by trees where he birdwatched , I hold my decibel meter , and measure noise . It is well within 45dB now in the daytime , which is the legally permissible level for noise at night . This noisy street , where cars constantly honk , where the daytime decibel level in last month 's world was constantly above 85dB and sometimes beyond 100dB . The WHO says exposure to noise above 85dB has serious affects on our health . <p> The sound and fury of silence <p> In my Mumbai home , I listen to leaves rustling ; watch as my friends , without domestic staff , experience housework for the first time ever . I sympathise with those whose family were stuck in places all over the world , and who had to be evacuated by special flights . I know some of the women who captained those essential flights . They are heroes . <p> But non-essential flights have stopped now . My sister in England can not come home indefinitely . My planned visit to see her this summer will not happen . I wonder what will happen to airline travel and whether it will ever resume as before . Carbon emissions from air travel were a large contributor to climate change . Still , our work meetings were important . Our holidays were important , too . In the never-ending cycle of work , we needed a break to experience other places , and to meet friends . <p> We meet our work colleagues , friends and relatives online now , talk to them more than before , without travel . We conduct meetings and submit work assignments from home ; there are no cars on the road . The Mumbai Police , who just last month released a viral video of deafening noise from honking in Mumbai and threatened punishment to indiscriminate honkers , released a video of the Mumbai of today asleep , beautiful . There is no traffic and no honking . <p> Air and noise pollution have both declined . A friend sends pictures of flamingos in record numbers in the mangroves surrounding Mumbai , a living sea of pink . Will the new technologies we are embracing now , meeting people online and working from home , keep noise and air pollution levels permanently lower ? Will they help with mitigating climate change ? <p> I call the woman who cared for my children 20 years ago . She lives in Dharavi where coronavirus has spread widely after someone returned from a religious gathering in Delhi . Once again , there 's intolerable noise in the background . Everyone is home , crammed together . I do n't need a decibel meter to know it is above 85dB . <p> I always knew every day noise pollution disproportionately affects the poor . Just like the coronavirus , slum dwellers have no windows to block out noise . Complaints to me over the last 20 years tell of their inability , unlike ' buildingwalas ' to block honking , noise from construction , religious places and every day recreational activities . " When our sleep is disturbed at night , we can not work in the day . But we depend on them for work every single day " . I receive a phone call from a village bordering a river pleading for help to stop increasingly vigorous activity of sand mining during lockdown . " I 'm a student . I do n't want to tell you my name , but I ca n't bear to see how this river is being destroyed " . Looking at the cost of delays in other countries , the call to lock down India was swift and forceful . But some say the coronavirus pandemic will continue to spread worldwide for the rest of the year . What will happen to the economy and to people 's livelihoods ? <p> Mining , power plants and urbanisation destroy natural habitats , forests and wildlife . With their village homes destroyed , many migrant labourers come to Mumbai , often to fulfil our lifestyles . Many migrants live unendurable lives here at the best of times , ten to a room , with staggered sleep time , ' hot-bunking . ' Now , they struggle to get back to their homes , where nature has been destroyed by GDP-driven development , ruination of natural habitats and escalating conflict with wildlife . <p> This is that moment when people and nature look deep into each other 's souls , as the virus threatens our entire modern civilisation . This is the moment to strike a sustainable balance between our need to travel to office or work from home , as we become more familiar with video conferencing ; our need for holidays in faraway exotic locations versus those involving sustainable travel forms ; to choose whether we will rashly embrace unsustainable consumerism at the risk of other future calamities , or reduce luxuries obtained at the cost of destroying nature . <p> This suspended reality provides the opportunity to choose our next steps and the course of our future , to recognise the real costs of destruction of nature , to choose investment in research of time and money in a circular economy . <p> Last year , it was apparent that climate change is one of the biggest challenges to the future of our world and of life , as we know it , even as world leaders gave climate change mere lip service . In the midst of a city , we live in nature . Development and environment are intertwined and the survival of each depends completely on the well-being of the other . <p> Many in today 's cities , which are under lockdown , are experiencing the extreme beauty and vengeance of nature for the first time . The Covid-19 virus mutated to infect humans through exploitation of wildlife , and rampant destruction of natural habitats and forests , leading to unnaturally close proximity of animals and humans . Scientific reports record other potentially lethal viruses trapped under the melting ice of the poles , which may be released as climate change continues . <p> Only the voices of people , including each and every one of us , will goad real action for decision-makers to realise the inter-connectedness of all things , respect nature as it deserves and embrace a more sustainable development model benefitting rich and poor , humans and wildlife alike . <p> A coppersmith barbet pecks at the tree . Son-champa and mogra scent the air . The Mumbai skies are clear , bluer than I have seen them in decades .
