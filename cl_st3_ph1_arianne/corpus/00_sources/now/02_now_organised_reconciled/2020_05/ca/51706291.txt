<h> You can make a difference . <p> Canadian governments need to invest in sustainable building retrofits in response to the COVID-19 financial crisis , said Bruce Lourie , president of the Ivey Foundation and adjunct professor at the School of Policy Studies , Queen 's University . <p> The recommendation came during a live-broadcast conversation between Lourie and Linda Solomon Wood , CEO of Observer Media Group and founder and editor-in-chief of Canada 's National Observer . <p> Video of Bruce Lourie on a green stimulus package for Canada <p> Bruce Lourie , President of the Ivey Foundation , and Linda Solomon Wood , editor-in-chief of Canada 's National Observer , talk about the future of Canada 's green economy amid COVID-19 on Zoom . <p> The talk with Lourie was the latest in a series of weekly deep-dive discussions that allow audience members to ask questions to top experts on climate change and global sustainability issues . <p> Lourie told the virtual audience of more than 400 people that federal and provincial governments should invest in environmentally sustainable and socially equitable construction projects . <p> " There are buildings in every single town and city in this country that require building upgrades , there are local tradespeople in every single community that could be put to work helping with this , " Lourie said . <p> " It gets people to work in every single community in the country , " he said . <p> He said a key priority within this effort should be housing for Indigenous communities . But convincing decision-makers to invest in energy-efficient building upgrades can be difficult , because it does n't create a compelling photo opportunity for those in power . <p> " You 're not going to see a politician cutting a ribbon in front of insulation ( that 's hidden ) behind a wall , " Lourie said . <p> Electrification will also be important in rebooting the economy , he said . This means switching to electric heating in homes and using electricity for manufacturing materials such as steel . Powering transportation with hydrogen fuel cells should also be prioritized , and Alberta is well-suited to help with that transition , Lourie said . <p> " Right now , Alberta is among the lowest-cost producers of hydrogen in North America . " <p> During the second half of the event , audience members had the opportunity to ask Lourie questions . A few people wanted to know whether hydrogen could actually be called sustainable if it 's being extracted from natural gas . <p> " It 's very rare that you go from a bad state to a perfect end state in one go , " Lourie said , of the transition to clean energy . While hydrogen that 's produced from natural gas might not be ideal , there are sustainable ways to produce it that Lourie thinks will eventually win out . <p> " The end game is a really great , clean system with clean electricity getting clean hydrogen out of water and zero emissions anywhere . " <p> Alberta has a workforce ready to handle the tasks of storing and transporting hydrogen fuel cells , too , he said . <p> " In the early transition , much of the hydrogen will be coming from natural gas , so this to me seems like a very obvious opportunity for Alberta to look at what is a future role for energy for them , as opposed to bailing out ( the ) traditional oil sector , which really , I think is going to be a very short-lived economic activity . " <p> This live interview is part of Canada 's National Observer 's Conversation series featuring topics around COVID-19 , the economy and Climate Change .
