<h> As COVID-19 lockdowns lift , let 's build a more sustainable and environmentally resilient Canada <p> The sun sets as people cross over the pedestrian bridge over the Gardiner Expressway to the Western Beaches as people are encouraged to physically distance to help slow the spread of COVID-19 in Toronto . Steve Russell/Toronto Star <p> Across the world , we are all trying to survive an unprecedented health , social and economic crisis . We could be forgiven for overlooking -- amid the despair over lost lives and livelihoods , the sheer strangeness of enforced isolation , and the upsetting chaos of ever-changing rules -- that the pandemic has also opened a door . <p> It 's just a crack , but we have the power to push it all the way open and provoke some urgently needed changes . We 're talking about a rare and historic chance to pivot from our pre-pandemic priorities -- rooted in unsustainable , outdated development models -- to embrace long-term change through spending that prioritizes ecosystems and biodiversity , green infrastructure and regenerative food systems . <p> Prosperous countries are responding to the COVID-19 crisis with economic stimulus packages worth trillions of dollars . This is an unexpected opportunity to use the power of spending to shape a better , greener , more resilient society . As we move forward , we should make deliberate , thoughtful choices that set us up to live in greater harmony with the natural environment and develop the resilience we need to better survive future health and climate crises . <p> And we already have the roadmap : the UN 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development . The 17 Sustainable Development Goals ( SDGs ) remind us that to build a better future for all , we must consider how our economies , societies , environments and cultures interact . The goals focus not only on environmental stewardship ( clean water , climate action , biodiversity and ecosystem protection ) , but on eliminating poverty , reducing inequality and mobilizing sustainable cities . They push us to adopt circular economic systems , so everyone 's basic needs are met without depleting our planet 's finite resources . <p> But how exactly do we do this ? We suggest that the solution is to rebuild our economy on five key pillars : strengthened and cooperative governance , inclusion of Indigenous pathways , protection and restoration of ecosystems and biodiversity , vibrant food systems and green infrastructure . <p> The first pillar , better governance , is about all levels of government -- local to federal -- cooperating to establish the foundations of socio-economic resilience and well-being . That means supportive , community-minded policies , programs and services . It means post-crisis initiatives that regenerate and protect our environment and ecosystems . It means ensuring reliable access for all to healthy food , clean water and air , health care , Internet connectivity , adequate income , and green energy and transportation . It is also about caring for the most vulnerable groups . <p> Logo for the Building Back Better task force . Supplied <p> Indigenous pathways , in keeping with the Truth and Reconciliation Commission 's Calls to Action , represent opportunities to learn from and incorporate world views and systems that have proven durable over centuries , including ecosystems and food systems . We should also address how crises play out where vital infrastructure -- even access to clean water or basic health services -- may not exist . <p> Third , we must invest massively in ecosystems and biodiversity to make sure present and future generations can benefit from them . Our lands , forests , lakes , rivers and oceans are fragile strategic assets with the power to mitigate the impacts of future pandemics and natural disasters . We need to preserve them for our long-term well-being and economic development . As the world 's populations flock to cities , we must also do more to protect a vital web of urban ecosystems . Let 's seize the opportunity to increase the size and number of green and protected areas across Canada . We can do this by leveraging territories of resilience , including UNESCO-designated sites , such as biosphere reserves , geoparks and World Heritage sites . <p> Food systems are critical to solving socio-economic and environmental challenges . Local , ecological food systems can help Canadians withstand future shocks to global supply chains . They can loop money back into regional economies , provide fair working conditions for everyone in the food system , protect ecosystems , biodiversity , soil and water quality , and ensure social justice through affordable access to healthy food . <p> Finally , green infrastructure is about recognizing the major economic , social and political changes that are being fuelled by the pandemic . For example , we must seize the opportunity to move away from fossil fuels and transform current and future projects that involve them . The more we know about the links between climate change and socio-economic vulnerabilities , the clearer it is that reducing our carbon footprint is urgent and imperative . <p> As we rebuild our communities and economies , we have a unique opportunity to sculpt a future that is proactively green , economically robust and inclusive . In short , we should be thinking about living within a biosphere that we cultivate with care and foresight -- not resurrecting our misguided efforts to dominate and exploit an external environment . <p> COVID-19 has dealt the world a severe and unwelcome shock . But we can choose to see it as the Earth giving us one last chance to get it right for present and future generations . Let 's not squander it . <p> S ? bastien Goupil is Secretary-General of the Canadian Commission for UNESCO .
