<h> Tool Could Improve Accuracy of Disease Modeling <p> An international team of researchers has developed a new mathematical tool that could help scientists to deliver more accurate predictions of how diseases , including COVID-19 , spread through towns and cities around the world . <p> Rebecca Morrison , an assistant professor of computer science at CU Boulder , led the research . For years , she has run a repair shop of sorts for mathematical models -- those strings of equations and assumptions that scientists use to better understand the world around them , from the trajectory of climate change to how chemicals burn up in an explosion . <p> As Morrison put it , " My work starts when models start to fail . " <p> She and her colleagues recently set their sights on a new challenge : epidemiological models . What can researchers do , in other words , when their forecasts for the spread of infectious diseases do n't match reality ? <p> In a study published in the journal Chaos , Morrison and Brazilian mathematician Americo Cunha turned to the 2016 outbreak of the Zika virus as a test case . They report that a new kind of tool called an " embedded discrepancy operator " might be able to help scientists fix models that fall short of their goals -- effectively aligning model results with real-world data . <p> Morrison is quick to point out that her group 's findings are specific to Zika . But the team will soon try to adapt their methods to help researchers to get ahead of a second virus , COVID-19 . <p> " I do n't think this tool is going to solve any epidemiologic crisis on its own , " Morrison said . " But I hope it will be another tool in the arsenal of epidemiologists and modelers moving forward . " <h> When models fail <p> The study highlights a common issue that modelers face . <p> " There are very few situations where a model perfectly corresponds with reality . By definition , models are simplified from reality , " Morrison said . " In some way or another , all models are wrong . " <p> Cunha , an assistant professor at Rio de Janeiro State University , and his colleagues ran up against that very problem several years ago . They were trying to adapt a common type of disease model -- called a Susceptible , Exposed , Infected or Recovered ( SEIR ) model -- to recreate the Zika virus outbreak from start to finish . In 2015 and 2016 , this pathogen ran rampant through Brazil and other parts of the world , causing thousands of cases of severe birth defects in infants . <p> The problem : No matter what the researchers tried , their results did n't match the recorded number of Zika cases , in some cases miscalculating the number of infected people by tens of thousands . <p> Such a shortfall is n't uncommon , Cunha said . <p> " The actions you take today will affect the course of the disease , " he said . " But you wo n't see the results of that action for a week or even a month . This feedback effect is extremely difficult to capture in a model . " <p> Rather than abandon the project , Cunha and Morrison teamed up to see if they could fix the model . Specifically , they asked : If the model was n't replicating real-world data , could they use that data to fashion a better model ? <p> Enter the embedded discrepancy operator . You can picture this tool , which Morrison first developed to study the physics of combustion , as a sort of spy that sits within the guts of a model . When researchers feed data to the tool , it sees and responds to the information , then rewrites the model 's underlying equations to better match reality . <p> " Sometimes , we do n't know the correct equations to use in a model , " Cunha said . " The idea behind this tool is to add a correction to our equations . " <p> The method worked . After letting their operator do its thing , Morrison and Cunha discovered that they had nearly eliminated the gap between the model 's results and public health records . <h> Being honest <p> The team is n't stopping at Zika . Morrison and Cunha are already thinking about how they can deploy their same strategy to try to improve models of the coronavirus pandemic . <p> Morrison doubts that any disease model will ever be 100% accurate . But , she said , these tools are still invaluable for informing public health decisions -- especially if modelers are up front about what their results can or ca n't tell you about a disease . <p> " This epidemic has revealed how difficult it is to model a real system , " Morrison said . " But I hope that people do n't take that to mean that we should n't trust our scientists .
