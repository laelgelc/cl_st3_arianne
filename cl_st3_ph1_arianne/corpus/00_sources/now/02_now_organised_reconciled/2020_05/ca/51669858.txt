<h> COVID-19 Response Should Include Distancing from the US <h> Our dependence on our increasingly erratic and protectionist southern neighbour is risky . We need new allies and greater self-sufficiency . <p> By Philippe Lagass ? and Srdjan VuceticYesterday The Conversation Canada <p> Philippe Lagass ? is an associate professor and Barton Chair in the Norman Paterson School of International Affairs at Carleton University in Ottawa . Srdjan Vucetic is an associate professor in the Graduate School of Public and International Affairs at the University of Ottawa . This article originally appeared in The Conversation here . <p> Donald Trump and Justin Trudeau : ' As COVID-19 radicalizes the already radical presidency of Donald Trump , Canada may be forced to confront its dependence on the US more directly and with greater urgency . ' Photo : US government . <p> Canadian foreign policy has long embraced both a deep continental relationship with the United States and a devotion to liberal internationalism . In light of the COVID-19 pandemic , the time has come to re-evaluate our approach . <h> Announcements , Events &amp; more from Tyee and select partners <p> Your new routine . Your fridge . Whatever 's changed in these pandemic times -- draw it with us . <p> While Canada has been able to manage the coronavirus crisis so far , our ability to continue to keep the pandemic at bay and successfully rescue the economy will likely be even more difficult . <p> If the U.S. can not get a handle on the virus , and if its leadership chooses a protectionist route to economic recovery , Canada 's return to normalcy will be that much harder . That 's especially true if the Canadian government is not able to secure exceptions from Washington 's protectionist measures , as it has recently . <p> Similarly , if international trade and movement are slow to re-establish themselves , and if protectionism becomes a worldwide response , Canada can expect a cumbersome recovery . <p> Canada 's dependence on the United States has been mostly benign . Yes , American decisions on softwood lumber and steel/aluminum tariffs , and limitations on other free trade agreements found in the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement hurt Canada , but Canada also greatly benefits from its commercial relationships with its neighbour . <p> But as COVID-19 radicalizes the already radical presidency of Donald Trump , Canada may be forced to confront its dependence on the U.S. more directly and with greater urgency . <p> Short-lived tensions -- including Trump 's unsuccessful attempt to halt exports of masks to Canada and his musings about stationing troops near the border -- may be harbingers of longer-term restrictions , disagreements or spillover effects that slow or stifle Canada 's attempt to rebound from the current crisis . <p> Worse , the rise of nationalism and geopolitical competition points to the likelihood of a fragmented international order built around a handful of large protectionist or self-sufficient power blocs . Liberal principles of free trade and movement may come under increasing pressure , leaving Canada particularly vulnerable to the whims of protectionist powers . <p> The ' Third Option , ' COVID-19 edition <p> So , what , if anything , can be done ? In 1972 , following the shock to the world economy brought about by former U.S. president Richard Nixon 's decision to effectively end the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates , Pierre Trudeau 's government introduced a policy idea called the " Third Option . " It was essentially a call for more self-sufficiency at home and stronger ties with the rest of the world to lessen dependence on the United States . <p> Though the Third Option dissipated after a few years , the idea behind it never quite died . COVID-19 renders Third Option thinking not only respectable but also responsible again . Witness , for example , increasing appeals for a more self-sufficient Canada , , and Foreign Minister Fran ? ois-Philippe Champagne 's efforts to co-ordinate Canada 's pandemic response with both " traditional " and " new " international partners . <p> Whether general or issue-specific , multilateral or " plurilateral " ties with partners in Africa , Asia , the Americas and Europe are in Canada 's best interest , simply because they constitute a counterweight to the United States . <p> True , some of these ties will always be shallow , others short-lived , and still others both . Yet , some ties might well lead to the establishment of deeper and closer strategic relationship with the rest of the world . <p> More diverse trading relationships will be essential to ensure Canadian resilience . In light of the recently concluded Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement , the European Union , for all its shortcomings and uncertainties , is one good candidate for such a relationship . <p> A shift to self-sufficiency <p> The principal challenge of the COVID-19-era Third Option , though , is not finding new partners . Rather , it 's Canada 's ability to do things on its own without relying too heavily on allies and partners . <p> We may see Canada invest in national manufacturing of medical goods as a result , akin to the munitions supply program that ensures the Canadian military has the ammunition it needs . <p> But the pandemic has also highlighted that Canada 's COVID-19 response has arguably been too reliant on international assessments . Canada will need to strengthen its own ability to assess and craft effective responses to global crises , not only in the area of public health , but finance , security and defence , climate change and migration , among others . <p> While global problems require global solutions , the pandemic has highlighted that national responses remain vital and should not be overly dependent on allies and international bodies . <p> Building the capacity to pursue the Third Option will take time , money and , most importantly , a political culture willing to reconsider the fundamentals of Canadian foreign policy . To get there , Canadians must be willing to think harder about Canada-U.S. relations and an increasingly fractured international order . <h> Facts matter .
