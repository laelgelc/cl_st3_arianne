<h> The Importance of Psychology in Addressing Climate Change <h> An interview with Dr. Susan Clayton about psychology 's impact . <p> Addressing climate change is one of the largest tasks of our time . However , there is an often-underestimated aspect of fighting it : psychology . The human tendencies , behaviors , and attitudes towards climate change have a big impact on how it is grappled with . <p> Susan Clayton is Whitmore-Williams Professor of Psychology at the College of Wooster in Ohio . Her Ph.D. , from Yale University , is in social psychology . Her research and writing focus is on the human relationship with nature , how it is socially constructed , and how it can be utilized to promote environmental conservation . In addition to her six books , she is a lead author on the upcoming 6th assessment report from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change . <p> JA : Why did you set out to write your book ? <p> SC : I 've been working for more than a decade to attract attention to the role psychology can play in addressing the climate crisis . Although there 's a great deal of relevant research that can be useful , I still hear this comment : " What does psychology have to do with climate change ? " Not only the general public but also many psychologists , fail to see the connection . But climate change is a result of human behavior and it will affect human wellbeing . To effectively respond to climate change , we need to examine the human dimension , and we need more people to be aware of these links . <p> I want to acknowledge that there are , certainly , plenty of people who are aware of this aspect of climate change . This includes all the psychologists who have been doing research in the area , as well as a number of publishers . In fact , it was because such publishers approached me about the possibility of a book like this that I finally decided to move forward with it . <p> JA : What is the primary takeaway you hope readers will learn from reading your book ? <p> SC : Simply put , the primary message is that there is a psychological aspect to climate change . Within that , different audiences may focus on different things . Three more specific messages are : <p> There are some human tendencies that create barriers to the accurate perception of climate change , and understanding these barriers can help craft more effective messages about it . <p> There are a variety of techniques that can be used to encourage more sustainable behavior in order to mitigate the impacts of climate change . <p> JA : What are some lessons from your book that can help people live more resiliently ? <p> SC : Living more resiliently has implications not only for individual health but also for social structure . In order to be resilient , we need to protect the natural and social systems that support our physical and mental health . So , the most important way to be resilient is to become engaged with these systems in some way that is meaningful to the individual . For example , get involved in a community network to support local adaptation . Become politically active . Form an in-person or online discussion group to help people grapple with this issue . Find ways to change your own lifestyle to reduce your carbon emissions . <p> JA : What are some insights from your book that help readers support a friend or loved one ? <p> SC : We need to be informed , connected , grounded , and optimistic . The anxiety associated with climate change is partly associated with uncertainty . So , I encourage people to find a source of accurate information that helps them understand some of the specific effects that might impact their own life . For example , what are the risks to their local community ( heat waves , droughts , storms ) ? What kind of changes can be expected in the next 10-20 years ? Then , connect with other people -- talk with them about your feelings and concerns so that they feel validated , and talk about specific things you might do together to prepare so that they feel empowered . Remind people of the sources of support they have in their lives -- the social support , but also the things they enjoy . Remind them that nature is a source of enjoyment and restoration , as well as worry -- go outside and experience it ! Finally , stay hopeful . Look for positive stories of change and resilience . Do n't rely on the media that often overemphasize the bad news . Change can happen quickly . <p> JA : What are you currently working on these days ? <p> SC : I have been looking more closely at the phenomenon of " climate anxiety . " How worried are people about climate change ? Is it beginning to affect their quality of life ? I 've developed a climate change anxiety scale and found that close to 20% of respondents describe their worries as at least sometimes impairing their sleep , their work , or their ability to concentrate . I need to do further research to look at a nationally representative sample as well as demographic differences . And I intend to look further into the relationship between climate anxiety and climate action -- what enables some people to be motivated by anxiety , while others may be paralyzed by it ? <p> JA : Anything else you would like to share ? <p> SC : Climate change is happening . It 's time for us to move past deciding whether or not we acknowledge it and start to incorporate it into our decisions as individuals , communities , and societies . Responses to COVID-19 show just what changes societies can make when they have to . If we make deliberate plans to address climate change , our lives will be less disrupted and less negatively affected than if we wait until we are forced to change . Resilience is enhanced by preparation .
