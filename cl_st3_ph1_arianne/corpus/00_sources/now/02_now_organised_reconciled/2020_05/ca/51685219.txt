' <p> Forests were nothing new to Muc , an avid explorer of coastal temperate rainforests -- but this one was different . Shrouded in lichen and dappled sunlight , the island was peppered with some of the biggest life forms on Earth : British Columbia 's big trees . <p> Muc was star struck and humbled in the presence of trees considered old a thousand years ago , and was launched on a life-long journey of big tree hunting . <p> " I ca n't imagine what a 1,000- or 2,000-year-old tree has gone through , " said Muc , now in his late 40s . " When I 'm in front of a big tree I feel very small , and not just in size , but my position in life .... Here 's a tree that is 2,000 years old and could live another 500 years . It 's humbling and precious . " <p> Muc is n't alone in his passion for tracking down B.C. groves where giants grow , just to measure and record the trees . He estimates about 25 enthusiasts actively hunt big trees , with " young bucks " swelling the ranks even as some of the original trackers have passed away . <p> The group has been compiling their findings in a central database that traces its roots to a tree registry initiated by famed outdoorsman Randy Stoltmann in the 1980s . Muc got involved in hunting in 1995 , one year after Stoltmann 's sudden death , which cast the registry 's paper records into disarray . <p> Many maps and photographs were lost , and other material moved around until finally settling at the University of British Columbia 's faculty of forestry , which digitized the records in 2010 with the BC BigTree Registry . ( The registry is temporarily offline , but you can read about it here . ) <p> In the 1990s , Muc hunted for trees using old forestry maps , which rated groves out of 10 depending on their height and size . If the grove scored high -- an 8.9 or 9.2 , for example -- Muc would set out to explore it on foot . <p> " The young bucks now get by with lidar , which is where you shoot lasers down to the ground from a drone and find the tallest trees . It 's literally that easy . I did n't have Google Maps , I had what were called maps , paper maps , " Muc said . <p> Originally the registry was used to track the " champion trees , " or the 10 biggest specimens of each species , but the UBC faculty of forestry wanted to create a public database for all big trees . Today anyone can nominate a tree -- so long as the species is native to B.C. -- and a credited hunter will verify the measurements before adding the information to the database . <p> TJ Watt found ' Big Lonely Doug , ' measured him and added the tree to the BC BigTree Registry after the surrounding forest had been logged . Photo by TJ Watt . <p> Collecting data on these giants is important so scientists can understand the sizes that trees can reach before they disappear , said Sally Aitken , a professor and associate dean at the faculty of forestry who helps run the BigTree Registry . <p> Aitken is a climate change and conservation scientist and monitors the rate of ancient tree mortality , which she points out has doubled in recent years . The increase in tree deaths is a result of climate change , logging and other factors not yet understood , Aitken said . <p> " It 's important to track these trees , because you ca n't protect something if you do n't know it is there , and you ca n't monitor it if you do n't know what was once there , " she said . <p> What constitutes an " old " or " giant " tree depends on the species , but the registry has still mapped many behemoths , Aitken said . They include a Western red cedar nearly six metres in diameter , a Douglas fir stretching 95 metres high , and a yellow cedar logged at 1,853 years old . <p> The Sitka spruce in Carmanah Walbran Provincial Park average 80 metres tall , which Aitken 's student analyzed to be the same as " three blue whales put end-to-end . " Big trees dwarf all other life forms and are the largest living organisms other than certain fungi , she added . <p> And B.C. is logging them . That 's because there is no legislative protection to stop loggers from cutting down these giants . " Nothing is protected , everything is fair game , " Muc said . <p> Some of the most devastating stumps of big trees have been found in the Edinburgh Mountain clear cut , said Ancient Forest Alliance co-founder TJ Watt . The organization 's executive director Ken Wu stands beside the stump of a Western red cedar 4.6 metres across . Photo by TJ Watt . This logged old-growth forest on southern Vancouver Island includes one of the most shocking stumps Ancient Forest Alliance co-founder TJ Watt has come across . The organization 's Hannah Carpendale is curled up inside the stump to show its staggering size . Photo by TJ Watt . <p> The protection of trees depends on which municipality it grows in , and contractors have been known to lose contracts when they cut down trees they were n't supposed to , explained BigTree Registry chairperson Ira Sutherland . <p> However , if a tree grows on Crown land there is no legislation that protects it based on its size , height , or age , he added . <p> That means if a tree is 95 metres tall on Crown land , it would be up to the discretion of the logger to harvest it or not , with no repercussions either way , said Sutherland . <p> Muc wants the provincial government to immediately cease the destruction of old growth forests and create a sustainable system to selectively log second-growth forests . <p> " I 'm tired of being considered radical when I 'm trying to preserve 100 per cent of the last five per cent of the province 's old growth forests , " Muc said . " I 'm tired of being angry . I just want to see something happen . " <p> TJ Watt has been hunting big trees for 15 years on Vancouver Island and co-founded the Ancient Forest Alliance in 2010 to campaign for the protection of old growth forests and for more sustainable second-growth logging practices in B.C. <p> Watt said these forests are vital to help fight climate change because they store two to three times more carbon than is stored by non-old growth trees . <p> In the Nahmint Valley on Vancouver Island , TJ Watt discovered the ninth widest Douglas fir in BC at three metres in diameter and 66 metres tall . Though trees this size are supposed to be protected , the BC government 's logging agency BC Timber Sales auctioned off the surrounding forest and , two weeks after the tree 's discovery , the area was logged . Photo by TJ Watt . <p> He added they are necessary for the survival of wild salmon and other endangered species and are part of the province 's multi-billion-dollar tourism industry . They are also crucial to the culture of many First Nations communities , Watt said . <p> B.C. 's old growth forests , which include every tree growing for more than 250 years , cover 3.4 million hectares with 775,000 hectares available for timber harvesting , according to an email from the Ministry of Forests to The Tyee . <p> But Watt calls those numbers disingenuous and says they lump together areas like the South Coast and Vancouver Island , which are only eight and six per cent protected , with areas like the Great Bear Rainforest , which is 85 per cent protected , and areas of B.C. that are boggy or high elevation and that would n't have been logged anyway . <p> " The challenge in the whole big tree hunting effort is the places where the big trees would have once been found have largely already been logged , " Watt said . " We 're really looking at the last remaining fragments of forest to see what giants can be found . " <p> The old growth forests on B.C. 's southern coast have been 75 per cent logged , including 90 per cent of valley bottoms where the largest trees grow , Watt said . <p> The government is failing to recognize forests have any value other than the price of lumber , said Sutherland . <p> " When we put a dollar value sign on timber , we fail to account for all other values , which is a nasty tradeoff , because it takes 300 years for the forest to return , " he said . <p> In the meantime , the tree hunters continue their work . <h> It 's Up to Canadians to Save the Boreal Forest -- and We 're Doing a Lousy Job <p> One passionate amateur hunter is visiting all 43 champion trees in B.C. and recording her efforts on her blog Tracking Giants .
