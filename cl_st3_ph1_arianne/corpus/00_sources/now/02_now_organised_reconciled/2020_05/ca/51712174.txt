<h> Social Sharing <h> Pandemic presents ' a great opportunity ... to engage with the nature that 's actually all around us ' : U of M prof <p> A University of Manitoba researcher is looking into whether the decrease in human activity and noise is benefiting bird populations , like that of this northern saw-whet owl . ( Juan Pablo Medina ) <p> A University of Manitoba professor is leading a global effort to study the impact scaling back human activity due to COVID-19 has had on wildlife . <p> Fewer cars may decrease traffic mortality and quieter streets may allow animals , such as birds , to better communicate and find mates . <p> However , decreased traffic also may lead to increased activity of species such as rats and domestic cats , which could harm native wildlife , said Nicola Koper , a professor of conservation biology at the U of M 's Natural Resources Institute . <p> To make sense of what 's actually happening , she 's launched an initiative to co-ordinate biologists across the world . The C19-Wild Research Group brings together ecological teams to share knowledge , ideas and research progress . <p> " From a conservation perspective , it 's really important to understand " how changes due to the pandemic are affecting the natural world , says Koper . <p> She has spent years researching the impacts of human development -- especially noise from human activities -- on wildlife , particularly birds . <p> When traffic volumes dropped due to COVID- 19 travel restrictions , Koper became fascinated with what the response would be from wildlife . <p> As people retreated indoors or went out alone for walks , in an effort to slow the spread of coronavirus , reports began to surface about increased sightings and encounters with deer , foxes and coyotes in urban settings . <p> Nicola Koper is leading a team that also includes Environment and Climate Change Canada , Carleton University , the University of Alberta and Arizona State University . ( University of Manitoba ) <p> Cougars have been found wandering streets in Santiago , Chile , wild boars have ambled around Barcelona , Spain , and goats were spotted eating flowers and hedges in people 's gardens in Llandudno , a seaside community in Wales . <p> In Winnipeg there were many anecdotal stories of an increased number of certain birds -- robins , bald eagles , juncos -- earlier in the spring , before COVID-19 restrictions began to be loosened . <p> Those anecdotal tales might not mean the number of animals in cities and towns has increased , said Koper . <p> It 's difficult to tell whether any increase in sightings is because people are out more and are n't as distracted by screens and other activities -- so they 're seeing more animals and birds -- or whether wildlife behaviour has truly changed , she said . <p> Determining which is the case will help researchers understand the effect of traffic on wildlife , she said . " And secondly , we might detect some really important trends that help us understand the status of a certain species . " <p> That 's where her new research group comes in . <p> " We 're still welcoming new teams to join us , so we can bring together resources and ideas and build teams that will help us understand impacts of travel restrictions on wildlife , " Koper said . <p> Birds have to fight against human noise in order to communicate . If that noise is less , they may be more successful at communicating and attracting mates , and consequently increase their numbers , says U of M 's Nicola Koper . ( Jessica Sanchez-Jasso ) <p> Her particular research group is examining the impacts on bird communities across Canada and the U.S. , specifically in cities and nearby areas . Koper hopes some initial results will be available over the next couple of months . <p> Her team will review data gathered during the past three years on eBird -- an international citizen-science program in which people all over the world submit observations about bird species -- and compare it to data gathered in 2020 . <p> " That 's going to let us compare how the communities of birds have changed in these different cities over time , " she said . <p> Birds have to fight against human noise in order to communicate . If that noise diminishes , they may be more successful at communicating and attracting mates , and consequently increase their numbers , Koper said . <p> Several bald eagles were seen flying around a thawing river south of Morris , Man. , earlier this spring . ( Trevor Brine/CBC ) <p> But the potential downside of decreased traffic is there might be more activity from mammals that eat birds . <p> " For example , we might get native predators like foxes and coyotes moving across the roadways more easily and into areas where they had n't often gone . Then they might actually end up having negative impacts on birds and other wildlife , " Koper said . <p> The U of M-led team is working with Environment and Climate Change Canada , Carleton University , the University of Alberta and Arizona State University . <p> A team from the University of Washington , meanwhile , will examine impacts of reduced pollutants on bird populations ; Florida Gulf Coast University will look at how decreased human activity impacts wildlife movements ; and researchers at Memorial University are partnering with others to investigate changes to aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems . <p> Like Koper 's group , they are all looking into whether anecdotal observations actually reflect measurable change . <p> Nicola Koper says it 's fantastic how much people are noticing the nature and wildlife around them . ( University of Manitoba ) <p> " I 've had people tell me about a Swainson 's hawk nest that they were really surprised to find in town . We looked into some of the previous records and found other records of the same thing , " Koper said . <p> " That 's why it 's so important to actually do scientific research -- to determine whether those are just unusual observations that will occasionally happen , or whether they 're actually unusual observations that are occurring because of this really exceptional human behaviour that has changed . " <p> Either way , though , the focus on nature is a win , Koper said . <p> " It 's fantastic how much people are noticing the nature and wildlife around them , " she said . <p> " It 's really a great opportunity for all of us to engage with the nature that 's actually all around us that we often do n't notice .
