<p> Last month , the head of the United Nations World Food Programme warned the UN Security Council that the COVID-19 pandemic could exacerbate severe food insecurity in countries already plagued by other crises . <p> " I want to stress that we are not only facing a global health pandemic but also a global humanitarian catastrophe , " David Beasley , executive director of the WFP , told the Security Council in an April 21 virtual briefing . <p> According to Beasley , on any given day approximately 30 million people depend on WFP to stay alive . " If we ca n't reach these people with the life-saving assistance they need , our analysis shows that 300,000 people could starve to death every single day over a three-month period , " he told the Security Council . And the death toll " does not include the increase of starvation due to COVID-19 . " <p> Even before the pandemic hit , there were reportedly 135 million people facing starvation worldwide . And Beasley told the council that " due to the coronavirus , an additional 130 million people could be pushed to the brink of starvation by the end of 2020 , " for a total of 265 million people . <p> Unless the international community takes immediate action , Beasley said the world would face " multiple famines of biblical proportions within a short few months " in many developing nations . <p> " In a worst-case scenario , we could be looking at famine in about three dozen countries , and , in fact , in 10 of these countries we already have more than one million people per country who are on the verge of starvation , " he said . <p> World Bank <p> A new report from the World Bank states that the food crisis sparked by the pandemic " is compounded by other existing crises in many countries , including the desert locust emergency , drought , climate change , fragility , conflict , violence and underdeveloped food markets . " <p> The World Bank warns that the price of food staples , such as wheat and rice , are rising at the same time that the currencies of many countries are weakening . As a result , consumer prices will spike , especially in countries that import food . <p> " Household incomes are also falling , reducing demand and contributing to food insecurity for the near-poor , poor and vulnerable , such as refugees and internally displaced persons , " states the World Bank report , titled " Assessing the Economic Impact of COVID-19 and Policy Responses in Sub-Saharan Africa . " <p> Local supply chain disruptions <p> The World Bank reports that " local agri-food supply chains are already experiencing disruptions , including reduced access to inputs and services , labour movement , transport and roadblocks , and credit or liquidity . " These disruptions , combined with global supply chain disruptions caused by export bans , are having negative impacts on African countries that import food . <p> " The COVID-19 crisis has the potential to create a severe food security crisis in Africa . Agricultural production is likely to contract between 2.6 per cent in the optimistic scenario and seven per cent in the scenario with trade blockages , " the report states . " Food imports also decline substantially ( from 13 to 25 per cent ) due to a combination of higher transaction costs and reduced domestic demand . " <p> Multilateral response <p> International Development Minister Karina Gould took part in a video conference on April 17 that brought together 270 representatives from national governments , United Nations agencies and other international actors to discuss the impact of the pandemic on food security and how to maintain food supply chains during the global crisis . <p> " We were seized by the real concerns that the current health crisis could trigger a food crisis in many regions of the world , especially in Africa , " reads a joint April 21 statement issued by Brazil , Canada , Italy and Egypt following the virtual meeting of the Group of Friends of Food Security and Nutrition on the impact of COVID-19 on food availability and supply . <p> According to the joint statement , the multilateral group is particularly concerned about the vulnerabilities of developing countries , " where agriculture is the backbone of the economy and employment . " Concerned that there are already signs that the global pandemic is disrupting food supply chains , the group echoes the World Bank , warning that the health crisis " could trigger a food crisis in many regions of the world . " <p> The joint statement asserts that " local regional , and global food security will depend more than ever on strengthening the resilience of food systems to withstand multiple shocks , and also on helping smallholder farmers , fishers , pastoralists and food processors -- many of whom are women -- obtain inputs , plant and harvest their crops , and sell their products for a fair price in a safe environment . " <p> In addition , the group maintains that " open , transparent and predictable trade is critical to keep food supply chains going and prices stable during and after the COVID-19 pandemic crisis . " <p> Nigeria <p> Last week , Reuters reported supply chain disruptions in a number of African countries , including Nigeria , Kenya , Togo , Ghana and Uganda . Fewer trucks were reportedly on the road due to uncertainty over what constitutes essential transportation . <p> " Several farmers said crops were rotting in the fields or at the depots waiting for trucks that never arrive , " the news agency reported . " And millers can not get their milled rice to buyers . " <p> Richard Byrne is a food security and livelihood specialist at Harper Adams University in the United Kingdom . Byrne is also the agricultural adviser to the Grow Edo Project , an agricultural training initiative designed to give the youth of Edo State , Nigeria , career training . The project aims to curb human trafficking in the region by giving youth hope for a productive future . The program is an initiative of the Catholic Bishops Conference of England and Wales as well as the Santa Marta Group , which is an organization of law enforcement leaders and Bishops dedicated to ending human trafficking and modern slavery around the world . <p> The impact of the pandemic on agriculture in Nigeria is of great concern to Byrne . For example , he told the Whig-Standard that farmers " have produce they can not sell . " <p> " At present , the lockdown has stopped the movement of goods and people so farm labour and inputs are restricted , " Byrne stated in an email . " There has also been an upsurge in gang-type violence in some states which has caused disruption . <p> " For Grow Edo , the impact on sales has been hard . We supply a shop in Abuja and a three-times-a-week delivery is now stopped . These sales are at the upper end of our income as we sell farm-fresh produce with provenance in the city . " <p> What would it mean for Grow Edo if borders were closed and travel restrictions imposed within countries ? <p> Byrne replied that tractors used by the project are manufactured in China . While some spare parts can be manufactured locally in Edo State , he said that it would be difficult to make other parts , such as drive belts , locally . " Where we would struggle would be to replace any pumps or generators which are imported , " he added . <p> " Our agro-chemicals are made in Nigeria but come from the other side of the country , similarly our seed is produced both in-country and imported . " <p> Price-cost squeeze <p> According to Byrne , the lockdown and problems associated with moving goods is pushing up the cost of farm inputs . " The issue then is we would have to absorb those prices as we could n't pass all onto our consumers or wholesalers , " he said . " This , coupled with currency pressure , could really retard the sector 's development . " <p> How is Grow Edo weathering the pandemic ? <p> " We are continuing with production , " Byrne said of the agricultural project . " We will harvest and store and process and wait for the lockdown to ease . We are obeying social distancing and our new borehole is providing everyone with more than adequate water . <p> " We are lucky that we are diverting labour to construction and maintenance projects , and the students are benefiting . We know our customers are still there and will want our produce -- they have confidence in it . " <p> Wake-up call <p> According to Byrne , the pandemic should serve as " a wake-up call for governments to seriously invest in resilience . " For example , there is a clear need for additional storage facilities for agricultural produce . <p> " We are lucky we have invested in on-farm storage , but we are a rarity , " he said of the Grow Edo project . " In the age of megacities , we have millions of people to feed , and the easy option has been to import . Foreign exporters have encouraged this . " <p> However , the pandemic is disrupting supply chains , making it more difficult to import and export food . And that is why there is a need to increase agricultural storage at the local , regional and national levels , he said . <p> " Good roads are also vital , " Byrne added . " In Abuja , you will see highways better than those in the U.K. , yet in Edo , the roads in sections are broken down to their foundations . " <p> Byrne noted that COVID-19 " is not the first health issue to hit Africa , but it is the first to disrupt global supply chains , so we need to build more within Africa . " <p> Need for new leadership <p> " I see so many young African men and women ready to take the lead . They just need support , and we need to revalue agriculture , " Byrne said .
