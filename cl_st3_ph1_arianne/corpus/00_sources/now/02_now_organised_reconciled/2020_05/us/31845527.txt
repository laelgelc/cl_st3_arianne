<p> Canada lynx are an elusive , lithe cat that thrives in powdery snow at high elevations . <p> Where other carnivores -- coyotes and bobcats , for instance -- flounder , lynx excel with snowshoe-like feet and long limbs allowing them to chase their preferred prey , snowshoe hare , through deep snow . <p> These admirable attributes make the animals difficult to study . In 2016 , the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife estimated that there were about 50 of the animals in the state , but WDFW had done no broad landscape-level survey . <p> Dan Thornton , an assistant professor in Washington State University 's School of the Environment , moved to the West from Pennsylvania in 2013 . One of the first meetings he attended was a lynx working group . <p> " It became pretty obvious from that there was a lot of concern about lynx status but not a lot of information , " Thornton said . <p> In 2018 , the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service announced it was drafting a rule to revoke protections for the Canada lynx . The animals have been protected under the Endangered Species Act since 2000 . Fish and Wildlife is still considering whether to delist lynx . <p> All of which got Thornton and graduate student Travis King wondering , where do these felines live ? <p> Thornton , King and others set 650 camera traps in areas of Washington believed to be good lynx habitat . Those cameras took 2 million pictures , which researchers and volunteers combed through . <p> The findings were not good . <p> Photos found lynx on only about 20% of their potential habitat in the state . The study , published recently in the Journal of Wildlife Management , covered more than 4,300 square miles in Northeast Washington with camera traps , but detected lynx in only 29 out of 175 monitored areas . <p> " The occupied area for lynx is really , really restricted , " Thornton said . " Much more so than these large lynx management zones . They are only occupying a small part of that , which I think is concerning . " <p> Lynx face a number of threats , but perhaps none is bigger than forest fire , said Jeff Lewis , a WDFW midsize carnivore conservation biologist . Lynx-preferred landscapes , in addition to cold and snowy , are high-elevation forests of lodgepole pines , Engelmann spruce and subalpine fir . <p> In a living situation typical of species that have co-evolved with rarefied survival advantages , the cats ' primary food source , snowshoe hares , live in similar habitat . <p> Unfortunately , over the past two decades much of that habitat in Washington has burned . <p> Starting in 1994 , the fires started to get bigger and more intense , said Scott Fitkin , a WDFW biologist in the Okanogan District and an author of the WSU paper . He 's worked in that area , which is the only place in Washington where lynx are considered self-sustaining , for 30 years . <p> " There have been so many fires there they all blend together in my head , " Fitkin said . " When you add it all up , we 've lost a significant percentage of what we 've considered good lynx habitat say 20 years ago . " <p> It 's not that there were n't fires before , he said , but after decades of fire suppression , the fires in the past 20 years have grown in size and intensity . That was n't always the case . <p> " In general , fire was always a part of this ecosystem and lynx lived with fire , " he said . " And the elevation where lynx occurred , the fires tended to be of moderate size and moderate severity . " <p> Once burned in a high-intensity fire , forests need at least 20 years before they become quality lynx habitat again , Fitkin said . <p> At the same time , Washington 's snowpack is changing . Although climate change models predict a reduction in overall snowpack , Fitkin said in many ways the type of snow is of greater concern . <p> Lynx have an advantage over other carnivores in powdery light snow . Hard-packed snow , or no snow , means other mesopredators , like coyotes or bobcats , are able to hunt in areas once inaccessible . <p> Unlike fire , Fitkin said wildlife managers do n't have " the quantitative data " on the impact climate change and reduced snowpack is having on lynx populations . <p> " ( It ) does seem winters are shorter and not as intense , " he said . <p> These impacts were all documented in the WSU study . The vast majority of lynx detections were concentrated in the northern Okanogan area , Thornton said . <p> " You never want to have a species that is clumped together , " he said . <p> Although WDFW had done other surveys of habitat work , the WSU study was unique in its scope . <p> " It was a good paper . A really good paper , " Lewis said . " It helped tell the story about lynx in Washington and what they 're up against and the challenges . " <p> The work may also help managers and researchers predict how a changing climate and more severe forest fires will impact other species , Thornton said . <p> " Lynx are good sentinel species for climate change , " Thornton said . " They are specialized , have larger ranges and need really cold , snowy environments . So , as they go , they are like an early warning system for what 's going to happen to other climate-sensitive species . " <p> While all of that is certainly dire , Fitkin and others said there is some hope , however tenuous , for lynx in Washington . <p> Some of the areas that burned in the 1990s have nearly recovered to suitable lynx habitat , Fitkin said . That could provide the surviving lynx in Washington access to new habitat . <p> " I 'm not writing them off , " he said . " Obviously , if we do n't control emissions and get the worst-case climate modeling , lynx wo n't be here . " <p> Because those habitats are starting to grow back , Lewis advocates for a somewhat counterintuitive approach : suppressing forest fires in the remaining lynx habitat . <p> He acknowledges that " suppression got us to this point in a lot of ways " but believes protecting the remaining habitat gives previously burned areas a chance to regrow . <p> " There is a lot of pessimism and I feel it , " Lewis said . " The approach that I 've been trying to take is there are things we can do that could be helpful for keeping them in Washington . Let 's try and do as many of the ones that make the most sense and are likely to have the most impact now .
