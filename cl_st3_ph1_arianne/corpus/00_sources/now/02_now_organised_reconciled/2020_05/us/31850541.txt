<h> 6,000 years of climate history : An ancient lake has yielded its secrets <p> For millions of years , the Murray River has flowed from the Australian Alps across the inland plains , winding through South Australia before emptying into the ocean . But the final leg of its journey once looked vastly different . <p> Our research released today conclusively shows what has long been suspected : 6,000 years ago , water levels in the Lower Murray River were so high that much of the system in South Australia comprised a huge lake . <p> We also uncovered an invaluable long-term record of floods and droughts in the Murray Darling Basin , by drilling deep into layers of silt and clay built up over 12,000 years . <p> Our findings point to how Australia 's most important river system might be altered by future sea level rise . What 's more , a better record of past floods and drought will help manage water use in Australia 's most important river system . <p> Probing the past <p> Our climate is changing and sea levels are rising . Scientists are working hard to forecast what environments such as rivers and estuaries will look like under higher sea levels and , in Australia , more intense droughts and floods . <p> One way to do this is to look back to a period 5,000-8,000 years ago , to a point in the sea level cycle known as the Holocene highstand . The Holocene refers to the past 11,700 years or so of Earth 's history . The highstand is the point at which sea levels were highest . <p> Today , the Murray River crosses into South Australia and flows within a narrow valley , then gradually widens towards Lake Alexandrina where it empties into the sea . <p> But it was n't always this way . After the peak of the last glacial period 18,000 years ago , melting ice caused sea levels to rise from about 120 meters below today 's level . About 6,000 years ago , sea level peaked at two meters above today 's level . <p> Researchers have previously hypothesized that over several thousand years , the high sea level at the mouth of the Murray acted like a dam , causing water to back up in the river , creating a saltwater lake known as Lake Mannum . <p> Our research confirms that the lake existed , and that it was enormous -- stretching from the mouth of the Murray to about 200 kilometers upstream near Swan Reach . <p> We used high resolution two- and three-dimensional modeling modeling of water levels and flows to confirm the presence of the lake , and how it formed . <p> Layers of history <p> The naturally still waters of Lake Mannum acted as a enormous trap for clay and silt discharged upstream . Under various conditions , such as floods , the sediment traveled downstream and settled to the lake 's floor . <p> Sediment core collected near Monteith in the Lower Murray River Valley showing lots of fine layers of mud . Credit : Scanned core images created by Anna Helfensdorfer . <p> Today , the climate history for the Murray-Darling Basin is written in these sediment layers . <p> We collected a 30 meter-long sediment core from the present day floodplain of the Lower Murray River . <p> The core contains an 11-meter section of sediment deposited on the floor of Lake Mannum between 8,500 and 5,000 years ago . Each meter took roughly 315 years to accumulate -- about three millimeters a year . <p> We believe each layer in the core probably represents an episode of increased or decreased river flow . <p> Most layers were probably produced when snow melt from the Australian Alps in spring and summer transported mud along the river system . Some layers will represent large floods that came down the Murray River , while others will represent floods that flowed down the Darling . <p> Longer-term variations in the thickness of the layers may correspond to extended periods of wetter and drier weather . <p> The next phase of our research will involve a close analysis of the sediment layers to obtain a reliable , detailed , high resolution record of flood and drought in the Murray Darling Basin . <p> The Lower Murray River near Mannum confined within the Lower Murray Gorge . Credit : Tom Hubble <p> What can we learn ? <p> As sea level dropped to modern levels over the last 5,000 years , the lake slowly drained and turned back into a river . <p> These days , the lower Murray River is intensively managed . Five barrages , or barriers , have been erected near the river mouth to keep the water fresh by preventing seawater from creeping in , and to maintain water levels . Significant volumes of water have been extracted for irrigation and domestic use . <p> Some people argue the barrages should be removed to restore the natural tidal estuary and allow sea water to influence lake levels . Their removal is unlikely in the near future . But our research gives insight into what could happen if the barrages were removed , and sea levels rise under climate change . <p> We expect the next step in our research , analyzing the sediment cores , to provide valuable data on long-term river flows and indicate whether intense droughts , such as the Millenium drought , are more or less frequent than the once-in-a-century figure often suggested . <p> In future , water managers deciding on water allocations may benefit from knowing how much water has historically come down the system , and how often . <p> Citation : 6,000 years of climate history : An ancient lake has yielded its secrets ( 2020 , May 6 ) retrieved 22 May 2020 from https : **39;715;TOOLONG ...
