I was wondering whether we could talk about your successes , and any potential failures you encountered along the way . When we hear about scientific discoveries , it is easy to imagine that the researchers behind these discoveries know exactly what they are looking for . Was this the case for you ? Tell me about your journey from the lab bench to the commercial adoption of your development . What role did collaboration with other researchers and with industry played in it ? <p> MH : Our case is unusual because the performance of electron microscopes reached a certain limit just above the 1 ? ngstrom barrier , and all attempts to circumvent this resolution limit did not work properly . Objects being observed could not withstand the high energy if a shorter wavelength was used . Many images had to be acquired to dig into the smaller details of materials by focus reconstruction . In the 1980s and 1990s , materials scientists waited impatiently to find a way to overcome the resolution barrier . Suddenly the aberration correction worked and opened up a new view into the atomic world of solid objects . I think that close cooperation between research and industry is beneficial for all , but what counts much more is the collaboration and understanding of the needs of researchers and respect for each other . A business strategy with the highest priority on making profit could be counterproductive for the scientific community . <p> KU : This can only be determined on a case-by-case basis . From our point of view , there are three things : Industry 's primary task is to design a good product , market it well and earn money with it . For cost reasons , Industry can not engage in basic research , or very risky research and development . This is down to the nature of completely new research ; the really innovative , that one can not from the outset say whether a research project will be successful or not . In order to achieve something really new , as in our case , one must also be able to take expensive wrong turns , be able to make mistakes and still be able to try out apparently hopeless paths . It is therefore not surprising that industry has not shown any interest in our research project . We had to proceed with the development of the aberration corrected electron microscopy alone . Funding agencies worldwide were not willing to finance this work . The reason for this was that , over many decades , people had tried in vain to find a technical solution for the aberration correction of electron lenses . It took a long time to convince the Volkswagen Foundation in Germany that our plans made sense . We then drove the development to the prototype ; a microscope that you could really work with . Even after we had achieved sensational research results with it , industry was very skeptical as to whether they would ever be able to make money with it . Any really new thing is also a commercial risk . One thing is for sure : It would not have been possible to make this development with our aberration-corrected electron microscopy in direct cooperation with industry , owing to the risks of novel research . Having proven its worth , this has now changed , and Industry has also made a lot of money with this invention . <p> The 2020 Kavli Prize for Nanoscience is just one of many prizes that your international , cross-disciplinary team of researchers has won . How much do you think your work has benefitted from a research culture that favors international collaboration , and do you think the same opportunities are available to researchers today , in the face of limited funding and issues such as Brexit . <p> MH : Our initial collaboration in Germany was actually the basis for our success . We were also driven by a race to the best resolution microscopy . This race gave us a kick to be better . However , fairness and acceptance of good results of other scientists should not be forgotten . I assume and I hope opportunities for a beneficial collaboration do always exist , as long as we are working in an open-minded environment , free from restrictions by the government or other powerful bodies . Hence , any restriction of traveling , meeting other labs or open discussions would reduce the productivity of money invested in research . In Europe , we have various networks in science that . Many British scientists benefited from these funded activities . It is very bad that British scientists wo n't have the same access to all the well-equipped laboratories in Europe as they had before Brexit . <p> KU : From my experience , I know that there are many scientists who do not trust their colleagues , and therefore prefer to do their research alone . Nevertheless , there are many funded research projects in Europe and in other industrialized countries that can only be obtained if an interdisciplinary team can be offered . This has significantly promoted the culture of interdisciplinary research . In my field , materials research , I see that the important papers in Science or Nature Group journals are often written by large teams . Each team brings its own expertise to the project . Again , I think that pressure from funding agencies is increasingly leading to the formation of larger interdisciplinary teams . <p> The potential for this is far from exhausted . I believe that the future of research , especially in large and well-funded projects , lies with larger interdisciplinary groups . I am confident that the attitude of individual researchers to collaboration will also change over time . It was clear from the outset of our project that a group of at least three would be funded . A pure device-development project of my two colleagues would not have been accepted . The requirement that a materials scientist had to be involved was clear from the outset . The focus had to be on application . <p> As you can see , we are awarded the prize not only for the development of the device , but also for its application . Since our introduction of aberration-corrected electron optics into electron microscopy , the electron optics industry has experienced a boom with great commercial success . The grouping of a theoretical physicist , an experimental electron optics specialist and a materials scientist has been successful , as can be seen . <p> With regard to current research culture , I see a tendency , on a European level above all , to want to force research into early cooperation with industry . This stems from a deep mistrust of the funding bureaucracy towards scientific research and the pressure from industry . When I was still President of the German Physical Society , I pointed out time and again that there is no investigation whatsoever into the extent to which this forced industry-oriented policy is really successful . Remember , in our case industry was not interested at all . Yet today they are making big money with it . <p> Regarding politics , Brexit is a difficult problem . Of course , I am against it . There is one point that is rarely mentioned . We , as people born in war , still remember the impactful pictures of Europe at war . For me , to live and work together in Europe was a goal of this and future generations . People have short memories when it comes to conflict . Germany , France and England would have formed a good team . The idealistic aspect of people is far more important to me than the economic aspect , which is absolutely secondary - just like the question of refugees . A tragedy . <p> Your developments in the area of nanomicroscopy and imaging have paved the way for many advances in research . My own research benefits greatly from the observational and analytical power that electron microscope has allowed me . What is your favorite area of novel research that has come about because of aberration-corrected electron microscopy and better nanoscale imaging and analysis in general ? <p> MH : For me , the most exciting developments are those that help us to carry out research against climate change . Our development of these new instruments supports this research by helping researchers have a look inside nanomaterials , and to understand the structure and function of such tiny objects . For example , electron microscopy can be used to understand the mode of action of solar cells and to optimize their efficiency , as well as find the best way to store the produced power in batteries . In both cases , it is essential to understand the processes at the atomic level . <p> KU : The study of materials and even of devices in atomic dimensions is an old dream of materials research . Measuring parameters at atomic dimensions that determine macroscopic behavior is perhaps the most comprehensive change that this new electron microscopy has made possible . We are still in the early stages , and there is still a lot of potential . One area that currently benefits directly from aberration-corrected electron microscopy is battery research . Lithium can be seen , imaged and measured with these new microscopes . Research into oxide materials has also received a major boost , because the corrected microscopes allow oxygen to be seen for the first time . The electron-optical industry is also a field that has naturally experienced a huge upswing . This should not be forgotten . Before our invention , around 1990 , this industry was in a difficult phase of stagnation and a number of companies left . Today business is booming . <p> This year , the three Kavli Prizes have been awarded to seven researchers that happen to be men . This is rare for the Kavli Prize , who have not had all-male Laureates in a decade . The Kavli Prize committee have from the outset taken many steps to ensure fairness in their selection process , which I think is evident in the diversity of their Laureates . There are now many initiatives in place that are attempting to recruit and retain a wider range of people into research . To that end , what do you think we should do to ensure that we retain a representative research workforce in terms of gender , ethnicity , socio-economic background , and what initiatives do you think are working well to level the playing field ? <p> MH : I am happy that you noticed this year 's unbalanced group of awardees . However , I assume this happened by coincidence and not by intention . What can we do to have a more representative diversity of gender , ethnicity , socioeconomic background and so on ? I think the only way is to be open to all kinds of people , and to support them independent of gender , ethnicity , or birth . We are the people who have achieved a certain level of education and we can give this knowledge we obtained to the next generation . Although our abilities and possibilities are limited , we can always act as human exemplars of how to cooperate and to interact with each other . <p> KU : I have worked in various positions in recent years to promote the integration of women into physics research . I think that we are making progress in Germany . After all , almost half of the PhD students in Germany in physics are women . However , most of them still decide to pursue a non-academic career or choose to start a family . In physics , the proportion of women in professorships is clearly far too small . Physics is simply not an attractive enough profession to retain a wider range of researchers . This needs to be worked on . After all , it is much easier to combine work and family life in Germany today - almost all universities and research centers offer childcare , which is very well received by parents . <p> Role models and mentors play a big part in retaining people within the research profession . Step by step , I achieved the closest targets and , hence , in the long run I felt that this ambiguous and challenging project of a working aberration corrector could also successfully be achieved . In this long period of working with many various people - men , women , engineers , scientists and many others - I learned a lot from all of these colleagues . I really admired some of them , and while working with them they partly became an idol . By " partly " , I mean that I was fascinated by the way they were thinking or working . Sometimes people are very good scientists but at the same time they show strange behaviors against people from other parts of the world . I admire the positive traits of many people I have worked with in my career , and do not follow or propagate any unfairness I see in this profession . <p> KU : This is an interesting question , not least because I have given it little thought so far . I would say quite unconventionally , and I wrote this in my autobiography for the Kavli Foundation , that the most important role model is my father . I think I owe a lot to him ; my understanding of other parts of culture , art , and music . In our family , a culture of knowledge and discussion was cultivated , which has guided me throughout my life . A great advantage for my profession as a physicist . As far as my later teachers are concerned , I learned a lot from them , but I often derived from these personalities what I did not want to do , or confirmation that I did not want to do things the way they wanted to do it . I know that I am not the only one who has had such experiences . Among my colleagues and friends , their qualities that are especially important to me are their skills and at the same time their modesty . They are all thoughtful types . They are all open to discussion , and always question their own views . This is a quality that I consider to be particularly important in an environment that is governed above all by opinions . Most people form these opinions at some point and then they do n't change them .
