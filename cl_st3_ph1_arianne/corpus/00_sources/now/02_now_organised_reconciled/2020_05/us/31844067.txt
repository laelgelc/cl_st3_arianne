<p> Climate change could leave freshwater fish with bigger brains but a reduced ability to effectively explore their surroundings , scientists have found . <p> In order to survive , the world 's aquatic life will need to adapt to the warmer waters which global heating will produce in the coming decades . <p> New research by University of Glasgow biologists suggests that the physiological changes fish will undergo in warmer rivers might require them to trade brains for brawn , with potentially challenging consequences . <p> In order to test how living in warmer waters might affect cold-blooded freshwater fish , researchers from the University 's Institute of Biodiversity Animal Health &amp; Comparative Medicine fished minnows from the River Kelvin , close to the University , and split them into two groups . <p> One group of minnows was raised in tanks filled with water kept at the river 's usual temperature of 14 ? C. The other spent their time in tanks heated to 20 ? C , at the higher end of the temperature range climate scientists expect the River Kelvin could reach by the end of the 21st century . <p> Over the course of eight months , the researchers paid close attention to each minnow 's physiology and behaviour , and watched how they interacted with their environment . <p> Measuring the minnows ' oxygen consumption at rest and during exercise showed that the fish in the warmer tanks used more energy in both states , suggesting their metabolisms had ratcheted up as they adapted to the heat . <p> The researchers also observed that the minnows from the 20 ? C tanks also developed bigger brains than their cooler counterparts . However , despite their bigger brains , they performed significantly more poorly in a test designed to measure their ability to navigate and find food . <p> They struggled to navigate a maze to reach a mealworm , and even though they had four attempts to learn the layout of the puzzle , their performance did not improve with repetition . <p> Dr. Libor Zavorka , the paper 's lead author , said : " While the minnows we studied were able to adapt to live in the warmer water , their reduced ability to find their way around in order to catch food does raise some questions . Although we found their brains were larger , they seemed less able to accomplish the simple tasks that minnows carry out every day . <p> " It 's likely that the expansion of brain tissue is n't accompanied by an expansion of neural density , so their brains do n't provide any additional benefits for being larger . Brain tissue requires a lot of energy to maintain , so it 's likely that the minnows ' bigger brains act solely as a drain on their resources . <p> " As their metabolisms increase to cope with the larger brains and the increased temperature of the water , they will need to eat more to fuel their bodies -- but if they are less able to find food efficiently , they will need to spend more energy looking for it . " <p> Professor Shaun Killen , head of the research group which produced the study , said : " We chose minnows for this study because they 're a useful model species , found in many places across the northern hemisphere across a broad range of temperatures . <p> " Surprisingly , this research is the first real study of how fish physiology , brain structure and behaviours might be affected by the warming of their habitats . <p> " Our findings suggest that disrupting the delicate balance between the minnows ' physiology and their environment causes some unexpected side-effects . We 're keen to extend our research further to explore the potential outcomes for freshwater fish in the increasingly challenging environments they will face in the future . " <p> The team 's paper , titled " Reduced exploration capacity despite brain volume increase in warm acclimated common minnow , " is published in the Journal of Experimental Biology . <p> This document is subject to copyright .
