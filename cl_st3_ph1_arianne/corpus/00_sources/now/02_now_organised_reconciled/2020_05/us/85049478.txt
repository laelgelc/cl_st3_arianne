<h> Fiction : The Gospel According to David <h> In his allegorical ' Jesus ' trilogy , J.M. Coetzee seems to strip the Bible for parts , losing its meanings in the process . <h> Sam Sacks <p> With " The Death of Jesus " ( Viking , 197 pages , $27 ) , J.M. Coetzee concludes a trilogy of obscurely allegorical novels -- after " The Childhood of Jesus " ( 2013 ) and " The Schooldays of Jesus " ( 2016 ) -- whose mixture of aloof understatement , philosophical discourse and cryptic symbolism has managed to bore and intrigue in equal measure . The books are about a child prodigy named David and his dependable but overmatched guardian Sim ? n -- two refugees from a land they do n't remember , whose new lives in the orderly city of Novilla and then its neighboring town , Estrella , vaguely scan with aspects of the Gospels . " The Death of Jesus " centers on David 's illness and death at age 10 from a sudden neuropathic disorder , and although Mr. Coetzee briefly explores the boy 's posthumous legacy , the fact that this book finalizes the series marks a telling divergence from the events in Scripture , where Jesus ' death is , rather famously , far from the end of the story . <p> This is to say that Mr. Coetzee 's interest lies not in divinity so much as in nonconformity -- in a promise of mental liberation that will , in the words of David 's most fervent disciple , " throw open the doors of our prison and bring us back to life . " Sim ? n , David 's well-meaning foil , is a Prufrockian rationalist who wants his charge to see the world " as it really is , not as you wish it to be . " David believes that Don Quixote -- the hero of the only book he will read -- is an actual figure whose adventures are ongoing . He espouses a version of mathematics in which numbers possess a discrete reality and can not be added or subtracted . His imaginative visions gain him admirers , particularly drifters , criminals and orphans , and by the time of his death these followers have claimed control of his memory , leaving Sim ? n on the periphery , filled with his habitual feelings of doubt and longing . <p> In the previous novels , the argument between passion and reason was often arid and lawyerly . " The Death of Jesus " is much more emotionally involving because of its wrenching portrayal of David 's death -- that is , its version of the Passion . But other scriptural allusions seem contrived . David demands that his dog and a sheep be brought together in his hospital room , a pointless literalization of Isaiah 's prophecy that the wolf shall dwell with the lamb . He rebukes Sim ? n for having once hidden him from city officials inside a cupboard , calling to mind ( but not illuminating ) the Parable of the Lamp . There is a sense that Mr. Coetzee has simply stripped the Bible for parts , losing its meanings in the process . <p> The meaning most missed , I think , concerns the nature of faith , which Mr. Coetzee approaches from a strictly secular point of view , conflating it with desire and desperation and a kind of intellectual abandon . His fundamental skepticism extends to the writing , which rummages provocative ideas but never creates the novelistic texture or density to reach a pitch of real mystery , settling instead for mere incomprehension . David 's belief in the existence of Don Quixote is not actually so strange , however extreme . We all feel something similar while under the spell of a convincing fiction . But however probing and intelligent , the trilogy 's inquiry into belief is too speculative -- too much of an exercise -- to bring about that suspension of disbelief in the reader . <p> " Things You Would Know if You Grew Up Around Here " ( Bloomsbury , 318 pages , $27 ) , by Nancy Wayson Dinan , is about a gifted teenage girl named Boyd who , like a " sympathetic lightning rod , " is burdened by the telepathic power to feel people 's suffering . The book takes place in an alternative 2015 ravaged by climate change , and in central Texas , where the story is set , severe drought has been followed by a flood of biblical scale . Boyd is the sensitive child prophet in this work of neo-scripture that posits the Hill Country as the site of humanity 's destruction and rebirth . <p> The plot follows Boyd 's dangerous solo journey through the back roads to rescue her boyfriend , who is trapped in a pecan tree . Threaded into it are short omniscient chapters both lamenting our environmental collapse and extolling the unique history and geology of the region . It is sometimes hard to tell what Ms. Dinan feels more intensely , climate catastrophism or Texas pride , but the combination gives her book a distinct evangelical flavor that frequently overwhelms Boyd 's page-turning adventure . I felt a little talked down to during these interruptions , but maybe that 's inevitable since , to paraphrase Lyle Lovett , I 'm not from Texas . <p> Offering an unqualified immersion into the uncanny is Suzanne Jill Levine 's translation of " All Fires the Fire " ( New Directions , 146 pages , $15.95 ) , a lesser-known story collection by Julio Cort ? zar from 1966 . Like his hero Edgar Allan Poe , Cort ? zar had a genius for dissolving the border between the real and the fantastic . A standstill traffic jam outside Paris becomes a months-long war of attrition in " The Southern Thruway , " while in " Instructions for John Howell , " a subpar theatrical matin ? e morphs into an eerie and enigmatic hostage crisis when a spectator is picked from the audience and menaced into performing in the next act . <p> Cort ? zar 's mordant humor is showcased in " The Health of the Sick , " about the increasingly elaborate pantomime a family stages to shield an aging matriarch from the news of her son 's death . And his political conversion to communism is suggested in " Meeting , " narrated by Che Guevara about a last-ditch moment during the Cuban Revolution . <p> But the best stories depart entirely from the conventions of understanding , establishing their own hallucinatory logic . In " The Island at Noon " a flight attendant on the Rome-Tehran line becomes infatuated by the image of a minuscule Greek island glimpsed exactly at midday : " None of it made any sense -- flying three times a week at noon over Xiros was as unreal as dreaming three times a week that he was flying over Xiros . Everything was falsified in the futile and recurrent vision ; except , perhaps , the desire to repeat it . " When he finally visits the island , the tingling of another compulsion disturbs his sense of homecoming : He ca n't resist looking up at noon to watch the airplane pass over his head .
