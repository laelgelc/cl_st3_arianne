<h> Bushfire-hit Australians still in tents as virus slows recovery <h> Wayne Keft , whose home was destroyed by the fires , says recovery has been ' slow and difficult ' for many <p> Wayne Keft , whose home was destroyed by the fires , says recovery has been ' slow and difficult ' for many ( AFP Photo/Saeed KHAN ) <p> Victims of Australia 's catastrophic bushfires are still living in tents , garages and makeshift shelters months after the blazes ended , with efforts to rebuild their lives hampered by the coronavirus pandemic . <p> Inside a small tin shed on Australia 's south-east coast , a family of six takes refuge from the cold as the southern hemisphere winter begins to bite . <p> The structure -- chock-full of toys and beds -- has been home to 51-year-old Anita Lawrence and five of her children since February . <p> She had been in Tasmania when fires ripped through the area , torching materials ready to build a new home and new life for her family . <p> " Every single little thing disappeared , " she told AFP near trees still blackened from the blazes . <p> Australia 's unprecedented bushfire crisis that scorched an area larger than most nations and displaced thousands put a rare spotlight on climate change in a rich , developed country . <p> The disaster sparked charity telethons , government promises of a quick recovery and donations from across the world . <p> But six months on and just six hours drive from hyper-affluent Sydney , scores of people like Lawrence are still living in limbo . <p> " When you come back , and there 's so much destruction , everything is difficult , " she said . <p> Before the crisis Lawrence had got by working a few days a week at the local school , teaching the kids gardening . <p> During the months-long lockdown she survived on pension savings and struggled to homeschool her own children using a single computer connected to a mobile-phone hotspot . <p> Now schools are going back and some help has arrived , in the form of local man David Crooke and his crew who have put together an extension for their dwelling . <p> It is temporary , but Lawrence now has a bathroom , heating and a bedroom until she can find a way to build a permanent home . <p> " Just to see a young boy see running water even , and a shower for the first time , that 's a big thing , " said Crooke . <p> His small team -- partially funded by the New South Wales government , Australian Red Cross and donations -- has been building shelters for those who would otherwise be destitute for months . <p> " There 's places that are completely wiped out -- our next job we go to , the ladies have a little tent in a shipping container right now , " Crooke said . <p> He himself lost his home to the fires last year and spent the summer battling blazes . <p> Armed with four homemade water pumps , his team saved several homes as fires repeatedly hit the southern coast of New South Wales . <p> Since then he has been camping out in increasingly harsh conditions -- moving from property to property , and helping build shelters . <p> But with ageing equipment and a struggle to find materials , the physical and emotional toll was " flogging " the team , he said . <p> " None of my crew have got really anything , you know , we 're week-to-week sort of guys , you rely on that pay cheque big time . " <p> - The world moved on - <p> Throughout the area , signs of life are springing from the charred-out landscape , but 66-year-old Wayne Keft said the recovery has been " slow and difficult " for many . <p> His home near Cobargo was destroyed when " a fireball went through the front of the house " . <p> He now lives in a garage , and is plagued by dust that blows in off a road nearby . <p> A surveyor 's stake for work on his new home sits in a barrel nearby , useless until the ruins of the old structure are cleared by crews who have been operating for months on similar jobs in the area . <p> Help has slowed since global attention shifted from bushfires to the pandemic . <p> Mathew Hatcher said his warehouse in nearby Batemans Bay was once full of donated goods . <p> " We were churning along , a very well-oiled machine , then COVID hit , and it kind of stopped the donations , " said Hatcher , coordinator of South Coast Donations Logistics Team . <p> The virus had also meant many larger organisations had to pull out volunteers , leaving locals struggling through trauma without appropriate mental health support , he said . <p> With tourism effectively banned in the state until at least June 1 , the area 's primary source of income and path to recovery has also been cut off . <p> Without a wage , it is almost impossible for local businesses to get a loan that would finance rebuilding . <p> " This area is going to struggle financially for the long term , " Hatcher said . <p> After the fires , Lorena Granados and her husband set up an ad-hoc market stall in front of the ashes of their shop , " Roman Leather Goods and Repairs " . <p> They have since moved to a temporary building , hoping the business can help them get back on their feet . <p> " We really were n't prepared to lose our home and business in one day , " she said . <p> The virus has slowed their trade to a trickle , but they are determined to fight on . <p> " It encourages us to keep going every day when we sell just one little item , " she said .
