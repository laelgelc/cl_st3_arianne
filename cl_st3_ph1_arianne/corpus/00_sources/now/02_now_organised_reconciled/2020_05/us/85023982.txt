<h> Life 's a ditch : State eyes new beginning for Erie Canal <p> ALBANY -- Construction of the Erie Canal was a dramatic turning point in state history , for it cemented New York City 's place as a global economic and financial powerhouse . The story of the canal is generally presented as a triumph of early 19th century engineering and of Gov . DeWitt Clinton 's vision and political willpower . <p> But as the canal 's bicentennial approaches in 2025 , New York is coming to terms with the ecological impact of digging a 360-mile ditch between Buffalo and Albany . The canal damaged wetlands in Central New York , and now , rather than helping move goods from the heartland to Manhattan , it has become a highway for invasive species that could destroy native habitats along its path . <p> Advertisement <p> The Cuomo administration , through the New York Power Authority , is working on a plan to repair the damages of the past while improving irrigation for the nearby farms . At the same time , a third of the $300 million NYPA has set for the initiative will be used to develop the canal as a tourist attraction and help revive the communities that once prospered because of their proximity to one of early America 's great man-made wonders . <p> Many have touted the proposal 's balance of economic development and environmental protection . <p> " It 's really rare to find a large-scale infrastructure initiative that results in great economic development impacts and environmental impacts , and this is one of those , " said Stu Gruskin , chief conservation and external affairs officer for the Nature Conservancy . <p> The canal 's construction separated three important ecosystems -- the Montezuma Wetlands in Seneca , Cayuga and Wayne counties , the Galen Marsh in Wayne County and the Rome Sand Plains in Oneida County -- from their water sources , reducing their natural water table levels . Locks and dams also trapped nutrients and silt . <p> In addition to providing flood and storm water resiliency and filtration of pollutants , these wetlands also are home to many at-risk species , including the cerulean warbler , which breeds in the Galen Marsh . <p> Restoring floodways and creating new hydrologic connections would replenish the three wetlands and could even expand the Rome Sand Plains . But while reconnection is the solution to the damaged wetlands , the state 's proposal also seeks to disconnect waterways in other areas to confront the problem of invasive species that have spread by means of the canal . <p> " We have to isolate and contain to prevent these species from spreading , " said George Jackman , senior habitat restoration manager for Riverkeeper . " Because once they arrive , it may be too late . " <p> There are economic benefits to preventing their spread . The Environmental Protection Fund provided $13.3 million this year to the Department of Environmental Conservation for the monitoring , surveying , education and control of invasive species . Waterfront property values can decline from invasive species , such as water chestnuts . <p> The initiative has raised concerns among recreational boaters . While the general boating population would largely remain unhampered , hydrologic separations in Rome and Rochester would require portages and hinder boaters who traverse the entire canal . <p> Plans to divert canal water for irrigation could be a boon to farmers fighting droughts in Western New York . Existing waste weirs can be retrofitted to serve irrigation purposes and because streams and tributaries flow north from the canal to Lake Ontario , there is no need for expensive pumps . <p> In 2016 , drought conditions led to a dramatic loss of field crops , according to a survey by Cornell University . Some farmers reported total crop failure . <p> John Kast , field crops manager and partner at Kast Farms in Orleans County , says the drought hit them at a time when adequate moisture determined how big their apples would grow . Without moisture , the apples were not large enough to be sold as fresh fruit and so were sold only for juice , which has a much lower price . Kast says the farm lost more than $300,000 from selling as juice rather than fresh fruit and estimates another $200,000 lost from the orchards they were unable to irrigate in 2016 . <p> David Wolfe , a professor at Cornell University 's School of Integrative Plant Science , says droughts of two to three weeks are not new to New York , but climate change models suggest this will only worsen in the years to come . <p> " There 's a recent trend in the last 20 to 30 years of more years where we get more of these short-term droughts enough to negatively affect productivity and marketability , " said Wolfe . " So , what 2016 did was really showed us how bad it can get . " <p> The proposal would also establish a new grant program to help farmers invest in their own irrigation equipment and a new governing committee would ensure water quality and availability . This could not only bolster farmers ' irrigation capabilities , but it could encourage some transition to higher-value crops like the apples grown at Kast Farms . <p> The $100 million set aside for economic development will be the first to be implemented . It includes $25 million that already has been allocated for initial tourism projects , such as a new water sports course near Seneca Falls , and development projects , such as a canal-side pocket neighborhood in Canastota . Another $65 million will invest in flood and ice jam solutions in the Schenectady area . <p> The state is hoping the plan will be the next turning point in the Erie Canal 's long history . <p> " The Reimagine the Canals initiative is designed to celebrate the history and legacy of our most iconic landmarks by increasing outdoor recreation , supporting responsible environmental stewardship , and developing new opportunities to better serve communities along the canal corridor while also ensuring these investments safeguard the canal 's role as a driver of economic growth for New York State , " said Shane Mahar , NYPA and Canal Corporation spokesperson.
