<h> Buzzworthy new books on bees , including memoirs from a professional urban beekeeper and an amateur who 's just getting started . <p> When we hear the word " bee " we 're likely to imagine the common honey bee or bumblebee . But there are as many as 25,000 recognized bee species in the world -- more than birds and mammals combined . Most of these lesser-known bees do n't live in hives , many do n't sting and relatively few make honey . But since nearly all depend on flowering plants for their food , they have evolved into the most proficient pollinators on the planet . <p> " The Good Bee " ( Michael O'Mara , 192 pages , $16.95 ) , by Alison Benjamin and Brian McCallum , examines the gamut of apian species , from mining bees that live in earthen burrows to vulture bees with a taste for carrion . Through sharp prose and precise line drawings , the authors address such subjects as bees ' evolution from wasps , about 100 million years ago , and their singular anatomy , which includes five eyes for navigating by the sun , a strawlike proboscis for sipping nectar and a furry coat for collecting pollen , the hive 's main source of protein . <p> Turning to the seven species of honey bee , the authors consider the ancient art of beekeeping , which is thought to be at least 3,000 years old ; the complicated social structure of the hive , bustling with queen , workers and drones ; and the painstaking process of converting nectar into honey . In a single day , a foraging honey bee may visit up to 2,000 flowers and collect half a gram of nectar . After partially digesting it in a specialized honey stomach , she passes the liquid to a house bee , who deposits it in one of the honeycomb 's empty cells . The house bees tend the honey , stirring it with their mouthparts and beating their wings to evaporate excess moisture . Finally , they cover the honey with an airtight wax cap until it is needed to feed the hive in winter . All told , a cache of 3.3 pounds of honey represents the work of about 12,000 bee-hours . <p> Ms. Benjamin and Mr. McCallum , a husband-wife team of London beekeepers , hope that if we learn more about bees we will care about them as much as they do . Bees are responsible for pollinating about a third of the world 's food crops , but a recent assessment of nearly 1,500 species in North America and Hawaii found that almost a quarter of bee species are at risk from habitat destruction , pesticides , parasites , climate change and other threats . The authors suggest five things that homeowners can do , from planting year-round bee-friendly trees , shrubs and flowers to building bee hotels . But they also recognize that protecting bees could require significant changes in the way we raise our food , requiring action by lawmakers , farmers and consumers alike . " And then , perhaps , " they write , " we can save these amazing creatures on whose existence our own depends , before it is too late . " <p> In " Honey and Venom " ( Ballantine , 295 pages , $27 ) , Andrew Cot ? shares his experiences as a high-profile urban beekeeper : retailing his products in New York City , installing hives for upscale restaurants and hotels , and consulting for media industries . As the book 's title implies , this can be a vicious business , and the bees are n't the only ones administering the stings . <p> Buzzing through his busy calendar , Mr. Cot ? pauses from time to time to fill us in on honeybee lifeways and history . But the focus never strays far from his own exploits , such as retrieving errant swarms from inside a hot-dog vendor 's umbrella in Times Square or from the underside of the Brooklyn Bridge . He also recounts his occasional squabbles with other beekeepers and an unexpected interrogation by New York City police detectives -- as well as his and his father 's travels around the world on behalf of their nonprofit , Bees Without Borders , which seeks to alleviate poverty through promoting beekeeping . <p> Mr. Cot ? has installed hives at the United Nations and the sculpture garden at the Museum of Modern Art , and has worked with television shows ranging from " Nightline " to " Cake Boss . " Among the famous figures who cross his bees ' paths are Martha Stewart , Paul Newman , Spike Lee , Bill Clinton and Michelle Obama . " So beyond just maintaining my small charges in a beehive and stealing their honey , " he writes , " beekeeping in an around New York City has led me to some unusual situations with bees and humans . And I love it all . " <p> Helen Jukes 's " A Honeybee Heart Has Five Openings " ( Pantheon , 238 pages , $26.95 ) is an intimate , finely observed memoir of her first season keeping bees in her back garden in Oxford , England . As her story opens , she is having trouble adapting to a new , stressful job . She feels stuck , unable " to think much beyond ' now ' and ' next ' and ' straight ahead . ' " Having helped a friend tend his hives , she is considering starting a colony of her own when she receives the promise of bees as a gift . Watching over her shoulder , we feel her anticipation as she waits for her bees to arrive , her trepidation on opening the hive for its first weekly inspection , her worry that the colony will decamp in a swarm . As she feels her way into her craft , Ms. Jukes takes in everything with the eager eye of the novice but spins it into the honeyed prose of the expert writer . <p> Between trips to the hive , she reads obsessively about bees , and we glean the fruits of her research , not only on anatomy , behavior and ecology but on the history of beekeeping and even the etymology of its terms ( " honey " derives from the Old English " hunig " ) . She admits she identifies so intensely with her bees that she finds it hard to maintain a " healthy distance , " that " at times it 's felt like a boundary has slipped , and the details I am noticing about the hive are in fact the ones I most need to notice about myself . " The book is as much about what is going on inside the author as what is going on inside the hive . <p> By the end of the season , the bees have taught their keeper about far more than hives and honey . " Down by the hive , " she writes , " away from the city 's tough exteriors , I found a place where I could take my armor plating off ; could become more exposed , more capable of touching and being touched . Perhaps I became better at caring , too . " Apparently so . As the story ends , she has discovered something not just about the honey bee 's five-part heart but about her own . She has fallen in love .
