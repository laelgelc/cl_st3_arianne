<p> The seafood lobby says assistance from the federal government has not been enough to help everyone along the supply chain . That is leaving fishermen , processors and distributors worried about their ability to stay in business as the economic slowdown from the pandemic ravages the industry . <p> Various sectors are getting a $300 million boost from a coronavirus emergency aid package from Congress that will be distributed by states to help make up for lost sales after restaurants closed their doors . In addition , the Agriculture Department has promised to buy up $70 million of catfish , haddock , pollock and redfish to distribute to food banks and nutrition programs . <p> Advertisement <p> But fishing boats across the coastal U.S. -- from Alaska to Florida -- are staying docked instead of going out to sea . Faced with dwindled demand , processors have had to scale back operations and lay off staff . <p> Almost all areas of the agriculture industry have been hurt by the pandemic . But , the seafood sectorhas been particularly wrecked because of its heavy reliance on food service , said Bill Dewey , director of public affairs for Taylor Shellfish , a large producer of farmed shellfish that is based in Washington state . <p> " The shellfish industry is already under intense pressure from the effects of climate change , tariffs and the general challenges of farming , " he said . " The additional loss of revenue caused by the pandemic is having a devastating impact on growers of all sizes . " <p> U.S. seafood sales have dipped as much as 95 percent this year , and global fishing has dipped by 10 percent since the World Health Organization in mid-March declared Covid-19 a pandemic , according to Global Fishing Watch , which monitors international fishing activity . <p> An industry coalition asked the Trump administration in late March for a combined $4 billion to be spent on buying surplus seafood , supporting supply chains and aiding fisheries , but only a fraction of that has been awarded . <p> Some in the industry say the meager aid doled out so far reinforces a long-held complaint that the industry is neglected on Capitol Hill , where power players like the beef and pork industries dominate in agriculture . The industry 's fragmented nature also makes it difficult to form a political force , compared with other agriculture sectors that lobby to support producers of a single animal , especially those from politically powerful farm states in the Midwest and South . <p> " The U.S. fishing industry is overlooked a lot when it comes to the food supply , " said Jason Jarvis , a fisherman in Rhode Island who catches fluke , black sea bass and scup . " Now it 's going to change . I think it has to . We 're looking at empty stores . " <p> Fishermen in the Northeast catching fluke or summer flounder in the winter months typically get between $3 to $5 a pound , Jarvis said . That price per pound has dropped to 75 cents -- not enough to cover mechanical , fuel and labor costs , he said . <p> Forecasts for the upcoming seasons are bleak as well . During squid season , fishermen make about $3.50 per pound , which is sometimes enough to carry them through the entire year . But the price of squid is currently $2 less than it was last year at this time , Jarvis said . <p> Jarvis , who runs a one-man fishing business on a small boat , has been delivering groceries for the delivery serviceInstacart to make ends meet while he waits for the food service business to return to normal . <p> President Donald Trump last week issued an executive order supporting expansion of domestic aquaculture , known as fish farming , but any effects from that directive will not be seen for at least months , if not years , according to industry experts . <p> Small-scale fishing and processing operations that quickly submitted applications to the Small Business Administration were able to get Paycheck Protection Program loans before funds ran out . <p> Operations in Alaska are reluctant to get back to work ahead of the summer salmon season because that would mean more contact with workers , said Linda Behnken who runs Alaskans Own , a community-supported fishery , with her husband and two sons . <p> Based in Sitka , Alaska , Behnken and her family fish for halibut , sablefish and a variety of salmon . But on a 40-foot boat like they own , the sleeping space is about the size of a station wagon . <p> " There 's no way to social distance on a boat , you 're going to be exposed , " said Behnken , who also serves as executive director of the Alaska Longline Fishermen 's Association . " And if you 're trying to limit contact , you 're not going to do that by going fishing . " <p> The high-dollar shellfish sector depends especially on diners willing to pay for fresh plates . Oyster bars -- trendy , social spots that had been booming before the pandemic -- have been temporarily shuttered under orders from state officials . <p> The vast majority of the shellfish caught by fishermen in the Cape Cod , Mass. , co-op Shareen Davis manages is trucked to restaurants in Boston and New York . With that revenue stream dried up , some fishermen are trying to selldirectly to local consumers , a pivot that 's happening within fisheries across the country . <p> " We do n't have to support big business , we can eat on a more local level and support the community again , " she said . " This is a solution that 's been overlooked , that 's a really easy solution to what we 're dealing with . " <p> Yet not enough people are buying shellfish -- which also includes clams and mussels -- at grocery stores to make up for lost food service sales . " It turns out Americans do n't know how to shuck an oyster , " joked Bob Rheault , executive director of the East Coast Shellfish Growers Association . <p> The processing and distribution segment of the seafood supply chain has been equally affected . Over the last six weeks , processors have lost on average more than $100 million and some plants have laid off dozens of workers , said Gavin Gibbons , vice president of communications for the National Fisheries Institute , the main seafood lobbying group . " It 's only getting worse . " <p> Seafood processing plants have been closely watching how the crisis is playing out in meatpacking plants that have become coronavirus hot spots . At least two seafood plants have shut down after employees tested positive . <p> Pacific Seafood in Warrenton , Ore. , temporarily suspended operations for cleaning earlier this month after an employee got sick . Thirteen employees at Bornstein Seafoods , located in Astoria , Ore. , contracted the coronavirus , leading the operation to close at the beginning of May . <p> Frank Dulcich , owner of Pacific Seafood , one of North America 's largest vertically integrated companies that has stakes in wild caught fish , aquaculture and processing segments , said his company has taken steps to implement public health guidelines in both processing plants and offices . They have installed shields and sneeze guards , sanitized surfaces and required masks and social distancing . <p> " We 're learning from our other protein providers in the meat industry , " he said . " We just got to get back to work . " <p> Those supplying canneries , like tuna and salmon , however , are seeing a bright spot . <p> Sean Wittenberg , co-founder of Safe Catch , a company that developed a test for mercury in fish , said more people grocery shopping and looking for shelf-stable foods to sustain themselves during quarantine has meant that canned tuna has been reintroduced into a lot of diets . <p> Canned tuna was a staple in Americans ' pantries for nearly 50 years , until about the early 2000s , when people stopped purchasing the product out of concern for health , like mercury poisoning , or the environment , such as unintended effects of dolphins being caught in nets . <p> " For the first time , many of these shoppers are going down the aisle , " he said .
