<p> If this pandemic is a portal , will it take us to an Asian future or drag us back to a more orientalist past ? <p> In my lifetime , the Asia Pacific region emerged out of extreme poverty , power struggles , epidemics and imperialism to become the most dynamic in the globe . This geopolitical shift has unnerved America , but it has also provided places like California with tremendous human capital and transnational networks that enhanced its ingenuity and prosperity . <p> This coverage of the coronavirus pandemic is part of your subscription to The San Diego Union-Tribune . We also provide free coverage as a service to our community . <p> My parents survived the turmoil of the Cultural Revolution in China and found refuge in colonial Hong Kong where I was born . We moved to New York City in the early 1990s , and , like many immigrants , I endured taunts about my food and my accent , and commands to " go back to where you came from ! " <p> Advertisement <p> This decade , an Asian American renaissance is underway , from the " Linsanity " phenomenon of basketball player Jeremy Lin to " Crazy Rich Asians " to the PBS documentary series " Asian Americans " -- just to name a few touchstones that show we are here to stay . <p> Still , practicing American citizenship while preserving Chinese heritage in an era of U.S.-China rivalry often seems impossible . I can never be loyal or critical enough . Over time , I came to embrace the Asian American identity , which I believe holds the promise of bridging and transcending the cultural gulf between Asia and America . <p> Months into the novel coronavirus pandemic , it is clear that it has taken a dire social and economic toll that will shape our society for years to come . <p> The coronavirus outbreak has elevated U.S.-China tensions to a fever pitch when high-level officials on both sides propagated conspiracy theories and inflamed nativist anxieties . This narrative war in the midst of a public health catastrophe put Asian diaspora communities in a double jeopardy of trying to avoid a virus that does n't discriminate , and to avoid discrimination , just as viral . <p> The most recent spike in anti-Asian harassment , ostracism and violence is yet another painful reminder to Asians and Pacific Islanders that our belonging is precarious . This racism is reminiscent of other shameful episodes in U.S. history such as " Yellow Peril " fears that were codified in the Chinese Exclusion Act for over half a century , wartime incarceration of Japanese Americans , the murder of Vincent Chin in 1982 , and the targeting of Muslim and Sikh Americans after 9/11 . <p> Meanwhile , in China , xenophobic evictions and hostilities against foreigners , particularly Africans , are spreading , along with fears of second wave outbreaks . <p> Advertisement <p> Scapegoating is the oldest trick in the nationalist playbook . What 's more tragic now is that nations are squandering the chance to unite respective resources and talents across borders that could slow the contagion and save lives . <p> The White House is using the pandemic to further isolate America from Asia , Latin America and Africa by suspending certain green card applications . This comes after years of endangering DREAMers and refugees , surging wall construction and for-profit detention , and separating families . These backward and inhumane policies are also detrimental to our ability to recover from the pandemic and economic recession . <p> A moment arises in these troubling times for Asian Americans to rally our people and to appeal to the shared humanity across this land and along the Pacific Rim . <p> As our ancestors have taught us , we can seize opportunity out of a crisis . We can turn the spotlight on our suffering toward illuminating the systemic injustices that plagued minority communities long before the pandemic . Asian philanthropists and grassroots and faith-based networks have donated personal protective equipment to thousands of front-line workers , underscoring the transnational connections and goodwill that hasten international collaboration . <p> Advertisement <p> We can support migrants who are among the hardest hit , and we can also learn best practices from Asian nations that have curbed community spread and democratized testing . <p> Before this " Great Pause , " the trend lines of America 's waning supremacy and Asia 's ascendancy fascinated scholars and pundits . In the wake of COVID-19 's staggering toll , we can set aside that debate because superpowers failed to rise to the occasion . <p> The enormity of global challenges ahead -- climate change , deepening inequality and infectious diseases -- require nations to cooperate and build resiliency . The imperative to heal will call for a rethinking of national security and its related spending and strategies -- swords will have to be beaten into ventilators . <p> A post-pandemic world worth fighting for will demand an economic orientation that prioritizes collective wellness and inclusion . It must require each of us to become caretakers for each other and our planet . The sheer magnitude of rebuilding this world may be bigger than America and Asia , but Asia-America synergy is more essential than ever . <p> Advertisement <p> Asian Americans are uniquely positioned to be the bridge toward that future . Let 's not waste our pain and step up to the moment . <p> Tsoi is the assistant director for the 21st Century China Center at UC San Diego School of Global Policy &amp; Strategy .
