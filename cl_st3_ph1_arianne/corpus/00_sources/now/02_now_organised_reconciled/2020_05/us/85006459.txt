<h> Dallas is a concrete jungle , but Janette Monear knows how to fix it <h> Dallas ' tree-hugger extraordinaire is leading the Texas Trees Foundation 's efforts to transform the city , including adding a 10-acre health and fitness park at the intersection of Harry Hines and Inwood . <p> Janette Monear , president and CEO of the Texas Trees Foundation , poses for a portrait at the foundation 's office on Swiss Avenue in Dallas . Monear is spearheading efforts to change the city of Dallas ' urban landscape by strategically planting 300,000 trees each year . ( Lynda M. Gonzalez / Staff Photographer ) <p> In these days of housebound isolation , we 're turning to walks and bike rides to raise our spirits . <p> Just witness the restless hordes packing the Katy Trail . <p> This is no surprise to Janette Monear . <p> The 72-year-old CEO of the Texas Trees Foundation has long been spreading the gospel about the holistic benefits of the great outdoors . <p> " Nature , like family , is often taken for granted , " Monear says . " Now that people are sheltered in place , one of the only ways to connect is with nature . " <p> Unfortunately , too many people here do n't have that opportunity . By any measure , Dallas is woefully lacking in " green infrastructure . " <p> Nearly 40% of the city 's surface area is a baking concrete jungle of buildings , pavement , parking lots and such . <p> As a result , Dallas is heating up faster than any U.S. city other than Phoenix . <p> Monear is on a crusade to rectify that . <p> As head of Texas Trees , Monear and her small staff of 13 work with municipalities , corporations and nonprofit partners to find the best places to plant trees in parks , schoolyards and along streets and other public rights of way in Dallas . <p> Texas Trees has identified nearly 2 million spots that need a tree , working with Oncor to make certain none are under power lines . <p> Robert W. Decherd , who helped establish the foundation in 1982 , says Monear is doing an excellent job in bringing the issue of Dallas ' urban heat islands to the forefront . <p> " Janette has been a primary force in all aspects of Dallas ' urban landscape for more than a decade , " says Decherd , chairman , president and CEO of A. H. Belo Corporation , parent of The Dallas Morning News . " Her influence is felt not only in projects Texas Trees Foundation initiates but in all matters having to do with the greening of North Texas , smart planning for parks and transportation corridors , and farsighted policy in both the public and private sectors . " <h> Worst of a bad lot <p> Monear and Texas Trees ' most ambitious project will cool down , spruce up and improve safety at the Southwestern Medical District , home to Parkland Health &amp; Hospital System , UT Southwestern Medical Center , Children 's Health System of Texas and other health institutions . <p> Ironically , the medical district is the city 's hottest and most unhealthy urban heat island -- 1,000 acres in a former industrial area where concrete abounds and trees do n't . <p> It 's even worse than downtown . <p> " We said , ' How can we have a medical district that is so terrible on the outside when they 're doing such wonderful work on the inside ? ' " Monear says . " That 's particularly unfortunate in these times when the heroes inside the buildings ca n't use their brief moments of downtime to collect themselves in a place of solace . <p> " We 're bringing a healing landscape to the district . It 's remarkable what the transformation will be for human health , safety , connectivity and well-being . " <p> Texas Trees wants to bring a " healing " landscape to the area around UT Southwestern and Children 's Medical Center Dallas , including a 10-acre park at Harry Hines and Inwood . ( Smiley N. Pool / Staff Photographer ) <p> Walking in the district is challenging at best . More than half of the 21 miles of roads in and around it do n't have sidewalks . <p> " We want to get people and doctors from hospital to hospital , clinic to clinic , from DART and TRE into the district , " Monear says . " In addition to the design for this , we want mobility options that would make it much easier for people to utilize the district . " <p> The first phase of the nearly $200 million project kicks off in July when conceptual drawings are turned over to the design team to create actual plans . <p> The initial piece will transform the part of Harry Hines Boulevard that runs through the district into what 's being called the " Green Spine " with trees and plants , walking and biking trails , and intermodal transportation along the highway . The public/private partnership has $18.5 million in hand and $10 million requested from Dallas County , leaving it to raise $4 million more to reach the projected $32 million needed to build it . <p> The second and more ambitious phase , the " Green Heart , " will turn the busy and dangerous intersection of Harry Hines and Inwood Road into a 10-acre health and fitness park that will be twice as large as the original footprint of downtown 's Klyde Warren Park . Its projected cost is $150 million . <p> When completed , hopefully in late 2025 , it will give health care workers , patients , visitors and area residents respite from the largest mass of concrete in town . <p> " We knew we were going to redesign the intersection of Harry Hines and Inwood , " Monear says . " So why not create a great park in the middle of a heat island ? " <p> To get to this point , Monear and her professional staff had to pull together the district 's diverse stakeholders , including the city of Dallas , big-name foundations , government entities , top executives of all three hospital systems , the medical district board , federal officials , politicians and people who work and live in the area . <p> Billionaire philanthropist Lyda Hill is a major contributor to various Texas Trees initiatives , including this one , because she believes in the mission and Monear 's ability to pull it off . <p> " I do the stuff behind the stuff . That 's why Janette and I make sense together , " Hill says . " I 'm a math major and all about data . Janette creates the vision . She 's able to bring people together to figure out , ' What is the big picture ? ' Unless you 've got the big picture working , your little picture 's not going to be anything . " <p> Decherd , who is also chairman of Parks for Downtown Dallas , agrees that Monear is the right person to lead the charge . <p> " The Southwestern Medical District Streetscape Plan will dramatically improve the feel and function of one of Dallas ' most important corridors , " he says . " Janette 's leadership and stick-to-itiveness have been essential to the development of the plan , which will impact a geographic area reaching far beyond the UTSW campus . " <p> Workers rest while cutting up a downed tree near Walnut Hill Lane and Marsh Lane on the day after tornadoes hit the area in October . ( Smiley N. Pool / Staff Photographer ) <h> One step up , two back <p> But in the past year , Monear has felt like the Greek figure Sisyphus , rolling a boulder uphill only to have it roll back down . <p> Straight-line winds and torrential rain in June followed by tornadoes in October devastated large areas of the city with an estimated total of nearly 5,000 trees downed . Thousands more are still standing but severely damaged . <p> The tornadoes cut a 15-mile swath , and the equivalent of 201 acres of trees were toppled . The bulk of the damage was between Walnut Hill and Royal lanes . <p> " A year from now , we probably wo n't have replaced what we lost from the storms , " Monear says . " It 's like one step forward , two steps back . " <p> The day after the Oct. 20 tornadoes , Monear drove through the areas of destruction before going to Texas Trees ' offices in a Victorian house in Bryan Place . <p> Preston Hollow looked like a war zone . <p> " What was amazing was how some of the trees had actually twisted , " she says . " They did n't shear off ; they just twisted . " <h> Communing with nature <p> Monear has been communing with nature since she was a child growing up in Cedar Rapids , Iowa , the smack-dab middle child with a sister six years older and a brother six years younger . <p> Her mother established a local nature center . Her father took her hunting and fishing in Minnesota . <p> " That 's where my call to nature came , " she says . <p> After high school , Janette studied elementary education . But she married her high school sweetheart and quit college before getting her degree . <p> She built on her job as a bank teller , working her way up to vice president of advertising and promotion at a large bank in Cedar Rapids . <p> In 1982 , Janette , her husband and their two young daughters moved to Minneapolis , where they lived in a cul-de-sac home that backed up to a 3,000-acre nature preserve . " Our backyard was a sanctuary for animals . We used to call out to the owls from our windows . " <p> She went from banking to higher education and then into the nonprofit world . <p> In 2007 , Dallas businessman and philanthropist Bobby Lyle , longtime chairman of the tree foundation , needed to replace its executive director , who was taking a private sector job running a ranch . <p> Both men thought Monear was the best candidate to move the foundation from piecemeal to strategic . <p> They were impressed with her urban forestry work at the University of Minnesota Extension Service , where she was instrumental in developing the St. John 's University Arboretum , and at the Tree Trust in the Twin Cities . At the Tree Trust , she co-produced , wrote the narration and edited the Telly Award- winning documentary Spirit of the Trees . The six-part educational series that aired on PBS in 2003 explored the connection between Native Americans and trees . <p> " We needed someone with strong leadership skills and experience in dealing with city and county government , as well as knowledge of how to develop effective private/public sector alliances , " says Lyle , whose name is on the engineering school at Southern Methodist University . " We thought Janette was ideally suited for the challenge . " <h> Courtesy visit <p> Monear came down from Minneapolis as a courtesy . After experiencing Big D in its summer meltdown glory , she decided that maybe Minnesota winters were n't so bad . <p> But soon after she returned home , Monear learned that she had early-stage breast cancer . And that changed her thinking . <p> " Sometimes the timing is right , " she says . " I was divorced , and my kids were out of college . I was impressed with Dallas ' entrepreneurial spirit -- unlike anything I 'd ever seen before . So on Oct. 26th , I had surgery for breast cancer , and on Nov. 7th , I moved to Dallas , thinking , ' You know what ? I 'm not going to think about breast cancer , I 'm going to think about what needs to get done . ' " <p> Lyle says Monear and the team she 's put together have marshaled financial , public and philanthropic resources and high-level collaboration that were n't available to the foundation before Monear took over . <p> " She is all about ' we , ' never seeking the spotlight but always willing to let the light shine on her team , " he says . " We have never regretted the day that we brought the ' Tree Lady ' from Minnesota to Texas . " <h> How bad is bad ? <p> In conservationist lingo , Dallas proper needs to increase its tree canopy from a ghastly 28% to a bare minimum of 35% . Take out the Great Trinity Forest , one of the largest urban bottomland hardwood forests in America , and the city loses about a third of its shade and oxygen . <p> Some 300,000 trees must be planted in the near future to make even the slightest of headway . <p> Monear knows such things because the foundation has mapped and tracked Dallas ' nearly 15 million trees since 2009 . The effort was paid for by the Lyda Hill Foundation , Communities Foundation of Texas , McDermott Foundation and Alliance Data . <p> The initial legwork was done by summer interns , who braved sweltering heat , mosquitoes , snakes , river sludge and poison ivy with GPS in hand and logged trees by species in 621 plots within the city limits . <p> Access to the great outdoors -- even just through a window -- has been scientifically proven to help boost our mind , body and soul . <p> It 's called the biophilia effect , Monear says , and it 's a powerful one . <p> " We know through research that trees and nature bring down symptoms of ADHD and that young women make better decisions when they have a view of the outside , " she says . " We know that after surgery , people who can see trees have 20% less postoperative complications . " <p> " Where you have trees , it 's cooler , it 's greener and there are higher levels of oxygen . Trees release their own kind of essential oils , called terpenes , which activate anticancer cells in our bodies . " <p> Summer sun can send surface temperatures 40 to 50 degrees higher than the air above them . During the 2018 heat blast , Texas Trees recorded surface temperatures of 142.5 degrees on the plaza at Dallas City Hall and 151 degrees at Fair Park . <p> " You used to have to quantify the value of planting trees , " Monear says . " Now everybody 's getting on the bandwagon . " <p> Monear has been described as a ladylike but formidable gust of wind that would never be mistaken for a gentle breeze . <p> All true , says Matt Grubisich , Texas Trees ' director of operations and urban forestry , who has worked with Monear since 2009 . " But a gentle breeze does not bring about change on issues like climate change and resiliency in Dallas , Texas , " he says . " It takes persistence , science , creativity and a lot of work . <p> " Janette truly wants any acclaim to be about the work and not her . There 's a great quote I like that says : ' The true meaning of life is to plant a tree , under whose shade you do n't expect to sit . ' I think that sums her up pretty good . " <h> AT A GLANCE : Texas Trees Foundation <p> Founded : 1982 as the Dallas Parks Foundation by Robert W. Decherd , chairman , president and CEO of A. H.
