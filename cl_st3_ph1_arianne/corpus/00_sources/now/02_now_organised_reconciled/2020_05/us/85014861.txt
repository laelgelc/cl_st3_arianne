<p> Archaeologists in Mexico have discovered the remains of dozens of mammoths in a finding that could shed further light on the hunting methods of prehistoric communities . <p> The discoveries were made near the construction site of a new civilian airport , General Felipe ? ngeles International Airport , north of Mexico City . They give archaeologists " an unprecedented opportunity to delve into more than 30,000 years of history , " Mexico 's National Institute of Anthropology and History said in a statement Thursday . <p> Pedro Francisco S ? nchez Nava , the institute 's national coordinator of archaeology , said the remains of about 60 mammoths had so far been uncovered in three areas since exploration started late last year on the airport construction site , which was formerly occupied by the Santa Luc ? a air base . One of those areas was the shoreline of the former ancient Lake Xaltocan . <p> The skeletons -- mature males and females and their young -- were found in the shallow areas of the former lake and were more complete than those found in deeper parts of the former body of water . <p> Researchers believe the animals became fair game for hunters when they were in the shallow muck of the lake , Mr. S ? nchez said in his statement . The researchers were still analyzing the bones to see how many could form complete skeletons . <p> " It is not ruled out that humans have taken advantage of these heavy animals , once they got stuck in the mud , " he said . <p> About 15 human burials of the pre-Hispanic period were also discovered , and the archaeologists believe they were of farmers . Some were buried with pots , bowls and clay figurines , like that of a dog , the institute said . <p> The Xaltocan excavations lie about six miles away from a planned landfill site in the town of Tultepec , where last year archaeologists discovered the bones of about 14 mammoths in two large pits believed to have been dug about 15,000 years ago . <p> The institute announced those findings in November , saying the bones could shed new light on the hunting habits of prehistoric communities who may have forced the Pleistocene animals into man-made traps . <p> Adam N. Rountrey , a collection manager at the University of Michigan Museum of Paleontology , said at the time that the find in Tultepec was " interesting , " but he noted there had been debate about whether sites of mammoth remains represented hunted animals or scavenged natural deaths . <p> Competing theories explain the demise of the mammoths , but it was most likely a combination of climate change , which created untenable conditions for the animals and also killed off a plant-based diet , as well as contact with humans who sought their skin and meat .
