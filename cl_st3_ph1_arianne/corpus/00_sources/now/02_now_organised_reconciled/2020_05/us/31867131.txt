The real story is climate speed . <h> special report <h> 1 . Faster , sooner <p> A two-hour cloudburst drenched Charleston on Wednesday , turning downtown streets into swirling rivers . Nearly 5 inches fell over the city 's hospitals , turning the medical district into an island . Five inches fell on Johns Island , turning parking lots into lakes . It was a mess . And it 's not normal . <p> Set aside the notion of climate change . The climate has always changed . The real story is about speed . The pace of change . From rain bombs to higher sea levels , the impacts are coming faster . This is as real as Wednesday 's storm . And the one four weeks ago . And so many others in the past five years . <p> In the coming months , The Post and Courier will explore these accelerating forces and their many ripple effects . We 'll explore the underlying science and responses by our elected leaders . We 'll look at the winners and losers . We 'll examine potential course corrections . <p> And we 'll do this in real time , as the king tides rise , the hurricanes gain strength , amid the thunder and lightning . Why ? Because a breaking news story only skims the surface of what 's really happening . Deeper currents can remain hidden amid the immediate need to stay dry or move your belongings to higher ground . <p> The most pressing steering current is climate speeding , the pace of change . <p> It 's an issue that carries particularly high stakes in places where land meets the sea . <p> Walker Condon walks home through the flood from work carrying her shoes on Calhoun Street Wednesday , May 20 , 2020 , in Charleston . Suddenly , the skies erupted with lightning and thunder and wind . <p> The National Weather Service broadcast an alert about a possible tornado on Johns Island . Why ? <p> The laws of thermodynamics : A growing human population has unleashed massive amounts of carbon dioxide and other gases , mostly by burning fossil fuels . These gases trap heat , and warmer air holds more moisture . When this moisture falls as rain , it releases energy in the form of heat . It 's like adding an accelerant to fire : more heat , more moisture , more heat -- and you end up with more downpours such as Wednesday 's . <p> And the one just four weeks ago , which dumped 6 inches on James Island . <p> And the crazy one in October 2015 . <p> That storm dumped 17 to 23 inches on peninsular Charleston , roughly equivalent to 3.2 billion gallons of water . It was akin to moving Niagara Falls over Charleston for an hour . Much of the peninsula south of Calhoun Street merged with the Atlantic . Public officials called it a 1,000-year event , including then-Gov . Nikki Haley . <p> But record-breaking downpours followed in 2016 and 2017 . In 2018 , Hurricane Florence dumped another 23 inches in parts of South Carolina . Rivers inland from Myrtle Beach rose as high as two-story homes . <p> All told , the Southeast has seen a 27 percent increase in the number of downpours since 1958 . <p> All this water from above is meeting another accelerating force below , a sea level rising faster than many scientists originally predicted . <p> America Street and other neighborhoods were covered by water after several inches of rain fell and flooded parts of the peninsula Wednesday , May 20 , 2020 , in Charleston . Upward curve <p> Scientists have good data on this . They 've been measuring the sea level in Charleston Harbor continuously since 1921 . Since then , the sea level here rose about 1 foot . <p> Part of this has nothing to do with saltwater . When the last ice age ended 20,000 years ago , sheets of ice melted in what today is New England . Freed from the weight , land there moved upward while land to the south , including South Carolina , sank like the lower end of a seesaw . <p> Known as subsidence , this sinking has happened at a relatively slow rate -- about 5 inches during the past century . This gives marsh-building sea grasses time to trap sediment and rise with the water , as long as the pace is n't so quick . <p> But in the mid-2000s , scientists began to rethink the pace of sea rise . It was n't rising in a linear way as many had thought . It was accelerating . An upward curve . Researchers at the Virginia Institute of Marine Science were among the first to pinpoint this accelerating trend in specific areas , including Charleston . <p> This was no easy task given the sloshing nature of the sea , said Molly Mitchell , one of the institute 's lead researchers . Natural tidal fluctuations and weather patterns can mask long-term changes . But over time , the patterns in Charleston and other coastal cities were unmistakable . <p> From 1990 to 2000 , the sea level rose 1.4 inches . <p> From 2000 to 2010 , it added an additional 2 inches . <p> From 2010 to now : 2.7 inches more . <p> Apply this curve to real life , and you had an explanation for the record-breaking number of sunny day and nuisance floods in Charleston -- floods that occur at high tide . Those happened about four times a year 50 years ago . Now that average is closer to 40 . <p> Follow this curve into the future , and you see a growing threat -- a sea level that rises an additional 3.2 inches by 2030 . <p> Morgan Perry and Kira Elizabeth Schlepp peer out of the front door of Kira Elizabeth Design Studio to see the water rising on Calhoun Street on Wednesday , May 20 , 2020 , in Charleston .
