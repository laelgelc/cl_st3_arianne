It has reminded us , in dramatic and tragic fashion , that our technologically advanced and interconnected civilization can be brought to an abrupt halt by a microscopic pathogen . And the coronavirus is but a harbinger . Climate change will precipitate future calamities . Another pandemic will come . Terrorism remains an ever-present threat . <p> It 's a dangerous world . And while we ca n't prepare for every possible future event , we can be significantly more " at the ready " for what will come . Several paradigm-shifting technologies are emerging that can monitor and detect these kinds of events in real time , enabling both automated systems and human responders to react swiftly , intelligently and with far greater effectiveness . <p> Given these increasing dangers and such rapidly advancing tracking technologies , an ongoing debate has been reignited around balancing data collection , privacy and civil liberties . The good news is that these new technologies give us the ability to develop and deploy lifesaving applications at unprecedented speed -- while also safeguarding privacy . That 's why , in my opinion , we ca n't allow well-meaning , but misguided , privacy concerns to impede progress . We should move forward with every bit of speed we can muster . <p> The reality is that tracking and surveillance systems have already been deployed with greater frequency worldwide . COVID-19 has only accelerated this trend because of the pressing need to identify and track those who are infected . In the past few months alone , dozens of applications have been built that track people , their movements and their body temperatures in real time . They are being deployed in hospitals , airports , factories , sports venues -- even elevators -- to identify infected individuals so that human responders can swiftly implement containment procedures . <p> This is happening in western democracies and in authoritarian regimes alike , all over the world . What 's different is that these applications are based on an entirely new event-driven architecture ( EDA ) that actually can make it more difficult to violate privacy protections . Here 's how . <p> For decades , software development architectures , including those used to create tracking applications , have been database-centric . Even as computing moved to the cloud , data has been captured and stored in ever larger volumes in databases for subsequent processing and analysis . In addition , database-centric apps routinely capture everything , so data can later be filtered for what 's relevant and what 's not . <p> EDA-based apps are fundamentally different , starting with the complete lack of a database . Software , compute resources , AI and other services are distributed out to the edge ( edge computers , intelligent sensors , handheld devices and the like ) . When an event is detected -- an infected person entering a factory , a sensor detecting an impending flood or the signature sound of a gunshot -- the processing and system actions are executed at the edge , immediately . <p> From my company 's experience , these applications are truly real time and can respond to tens of thousands or even millions of events per second when required . What 's more , they are smart enough to filter out extraneous data . The point is , nothing -- including any personally identifiable information -- needs to be stored in a database . And when the situation has been remediated , all the data associated with that event can be deleted from all of the edge devices . <p> This difference can be hard to grasp . After all , if an application is using facial recognition in conjunction with a thermal sensor to identify an individual with a fever , how is it that personally identifiable information is not being captured ? Much depends upon the design of the application . In this example , it 's not necessary to identify an individual by name . Instead , the system needs only to identify an event . In this case , " an individual with a fever has entered the building , and this person has moved from the lobby to the cafeteria . " That allows human responders to locate the individual and take action . And again , once the event has been remediated , the images that could be used to identify the individual can be destroyed . <p> I recognize that privacy protections vary from country to country and that governments or bad actors can violate local privacy laws and protections if they are intent on doing so . But this EDA-architected approach is built to be less invasive than database-centric tracking systems . I 've observed that they 're less invasive than systems that record video data on any storage device . <p> There 's simply too much at stake to let privacy concerns impede the building and deployment of these new systems . They can be lifesaving . And the proper use of these new surveillance and tracking technologies can be viewed not as a threat , but as an extremely beneficial development for a world in crisis . We need to get people back to work and enable them to dine out , attend sporting events and go about their lives -- safely . We have the ability to make this happen . <p> After 9/11 , we necessarily adjusted to a proliferation of new security protocols and technologies in our airports and gathering places . Today , we can embrace a new era and provide real-time , event-driven applications that can mitigate the worst possible outcomes of future crises . And , we can do it while protecting our civil liberties . The time is now to hit the accelerator .
