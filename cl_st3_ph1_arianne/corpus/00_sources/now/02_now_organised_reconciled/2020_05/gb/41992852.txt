<p> " Beauty is truth , truth beauty -- that is all , " wrote John Keats . The famous words come from his 1820 poem Ode on a Grecian Urn , but they are a fitting description for the practice of botanical art and illustration . It 's a place where art meets science - a feast for the mind as well as the eyes . <p> Although the nation 's lockdown continues , it has a distinctly green-fingered feel about it this week . Chelsea Flower Show goes online for the first time in history , garden centres have reopened and we 're able to walk around London 's parks for as long as we want . But we 're dearly missing the tranquillity of London 's museums and attractions , which -- from the Garden Museum in Lambeth to Kew Gardens -- boast some of the finest botanical art collections in the world . <p> Thankfully , the ubiquity of botanical art means that we 're never far from being able to enjoy it . So soothing is it to look at that it 's now become a big part of our homes , on art prints , duvet covers and greetings cards . And while galleries are closed , the consolation of art has been replaced by nature -- our daily walk is now the place where we have time to stop , think and reflect -- so there 's never been a better time to appreciate the artform or even give it a go yourself . <p> The aesthetic of a traditional botanical illustration is immediately recognisable : a flower or plant drawn in painstaking detail from its petals sometimes down to its roots , set against a white background , often accompanied by its Latin name . Its original scientific purpose demanded that each drawing be highly specific and accurate , says Fiona Davison , the Royal Horticultural Society 's Head of Libraries and Exhibitions . " When plants were our source of drugs it was vital that people used the right plant -- mistakes could be deadly . It is very difficult to describe the subtle difference between different varieties in words so doctors and apothecaries relied on drawings , " she says . " As the science of botany advanced , highly accurate drawings were needed to assist in plant identification and classification . Botanical illustrators would use microscopes to study and reproduce the most exquisite detail and even dissect plants to show how they were constructed . " <p> Botanical illustrations offer " a beautiful but succinct way to illustrate the whole life cycle of a plant , " says Maria Devaney , Galleries and Exhibition Leader at Royal Botanic Gardens , Kew . Traditionally painted with watercolour on paper or vellum , she explains that " every detail about the plant must be accurate and exact . " <p> " At the root of all botanical art is the investigation of nature and the scientific recording of the detail of a particular plant . What began as a purely decorative effect in the 18th century developed into an important aspect of science in the age of exploration , " she says . <p> The rise of digital technology has meant that the tradition of botanical art in its original form -- the scientific recording of the details of particular plants and flowers -- has receded somewhat . ( The vintage-like quality of the works may have something to do with their attraction . ) But it has a long history , with humans recording the natural world as early as some of the first discovered cave paintings . Early examples of drawings of plant life from the time of Pliny the Elder have now been lost , and medieval illuminated manuscripts provide some of the earliest known examples of botanical art in its purest form . <p> Despite these advances , the artform remains a vital part of Kew 's work ; it has worked with artists since its inception as a Royal Botanic Garden and continues to do so today . Joseph Banks , who was Kew 's first unofficial director and botanist to King George III , asked artists to paint plants from around the world -- and those drawings are still used for research purposes . " Another popular practice is recording and painting all the plants growing in a single garden , known as a Florilegium . The Highgrove Florilegium is perhaps one of the best known recent examples , " says Devaney . " In wider popular culture contemporary botanical imagery is everywhere from fashion , home decor and furnishings , mainstream contemporary art and even the humble emoji , where everyone can include a botanical art image in texts and messages . " <p> Roses by Regine Hagedorn , from the Shirley Sherwood collection at Kew ( RBG Kew ) <p> But for all the benefits , photography and technological advances can not always compare to the work of an artist . " Even in the days of modern digital photography , nothing can replace the intense and intelligent scrutiny of the trained human eye , " says Davison . The RHS continues to run an annual competition for the very best botanical artists , which sees artists from all over the world competing . <p> The modern relevance of botanical art can also be seen in the ways it inspires artists who are interested in the natural world . " There is a unique marriage between art and scientific study in the art form , " says Emma House , curator at the Garden Museum . She adds that its collaborative nature is another part of its enduring appeal . The Garden Museum 's recent Tradescant 's Orchard show brought 50 artists together to record heritage varieties of fruit still growing today . " Many heritage varieties have become rare in recent years and some are at risk of disappearing all together . Therefore recording them in illustrated form can help to disseminate knowledge about them , promote them and ensure their survival . " <p> Devaney agrees that botanical art can now serve a more urgent purpose . " The art world is constantly evolving to reflect society as it changes and at the moment with concerns about climate change and threats to eco-systems around the world , botanical and plant-based art has never been more important as a tool to raise awareness . " <p> But part of the appeal will always be the inclusive , mysterious and magical qualities of nature . " People love nature , and getting close to it and finding ways to appreciate and understand it , as well as bringing the outdoors indoors , is one of the key reasons why botanical art remains a vital and emotive genre of modern art , " Devaney says . <p> Perhaps for now , though , it 's the therapeutic value of botanical illustration that matters the most . Says Davison : " Although the primary intention is always scientific , it also helps that the images are really beautiful . People who try botanical art themselves really seem to enjoy the opportunity to study a small piece of nature in a really focussed way . It can be a kind of meditation , a really great way to de-stress . " <h> Botanical artists to know <p> The Marianne North Gallery at Kew Gardens ( RBG Kew ) <p> John Sebastian Miller ( 1715-1792 ) . His illustrations of The Sexual System of Linnaeus ( where plants were divided into groups , according to their stamens and pistils ) were his greatest work . <p> James Sowerby ( 1757-1822 ) . He was one of the first illustrators for Curtis 's Botanical Journal , which began in 1787 and is still printed today . His 36-volume work , English Botany , was one of the defining publications in the form . <p> Pierre-Joseph Redout ? ( 1759-1840 ) . A French artist , also known as the ' Raphael of flower painting ' . <p> Marianne North ( 1830-1890 ) . A prolific artist who painted over 1000 species from 9 different continents as she travelled the world solo . Kew 's Marianne North Gallery is named after her and displays many of her works . <h> How to start making your own botanical art <p> Lucy T Smith , on of Kew 's botanical artists ( RBG Kew ) <p> Lucy T Smith , who is one of Kew 's botanical artists , says the best way to start making your own botanical art is to draw the plants and flowers around you . " Keep a sketchbook of your work , so that you can see your progress . If you date your sketchbook like a visual diary , you can come back every season to a particular plant that you enjoyed drawing and painting . You can also document how the plant grows over time , for example as flowers mature into fruit and release seed . It 's really important to observe plants from life , as that is the best way to truly understand them . "
