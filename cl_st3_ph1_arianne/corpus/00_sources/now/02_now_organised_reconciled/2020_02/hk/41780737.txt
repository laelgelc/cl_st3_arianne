<h> The case for women running shift to renewables <h> Share this : <p> Coal fired power station silhouette at sunset . Photo : iStock <p> There is a harsh truth that Southeast Asia must face . While the region remains one of the most vulnerable in the world to climate change , member states of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations are a long way behind on meeting their climate commitments . <p> Of course , ASEAN countries are not alone in this . According to UN Environment Program figures , the world is on track for a 3.2-degree global temperature increase by 2100 , a far cry from the 2-degree target and 1.5-degree ambition set by the international community in Paris in 2015 . <p> This is a planetary issue and not only Southeast Asia 's burden to bear . But the region has a unique set of circumstances that must be addressed . <p> Foremost is the fact that Southeast Asia is one of the only regions where coal use is steadily increasing , closely following big users like China and India . Every new coal plant locks ASEAN countries into carbon emissions that our children and grandchildren will suffer from . Rural women and girls , in particular , stand to lose the most from these choices . But at the recently concluded COP25 , only a single ASEAN country -- Laos -- joined the NDC ( Nationally Determined Contributions ) Alliance to commit to further emissions reductions in 2020 . <p> Coal and other fossil fuels are not thriving because of some natural free-market advantage . Instead , they are artificially allowed to propagate thanks to continued government subsidies -- an energy source in decline but for the crutch of taxpayers ' money . <p> Globally , subsidies to fossil fuels stand at around US$400 billion each year . In Southeast Asia , they amounted to almost $35 billion in 2018 . While coal power is likely to remain profitable into 2040 , renewables are likely to be even more profitable . But in investing in distributed renewable energy there is an opportunity to bring reliable energy to the last-mile user in a way that large-scale sources can not . Not only would this pull the plug on coal 's life support , it could usher in benefits for communities that would otherwise be left in the dark . <p> Those adept to drive this shift to renewables are those with the knowledge , skills , capability and ambition to effect change in their communities : women . <p> Traditional norms dictate that men are often the first choice when it comes to making decisions and accessing renewable-energy technologies , training , capital and finance . But while men maintain this primacy , it is actually women who can best capitalize on the benefits of renewables . <p> Frequently , women are the " household @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ energy sources in the home . Women are also crucial links in Southeast Asia 's agriculture value chain , where renewable energy can provide significant benefits . It is often women who collect water for irrigation , perform daily farm labor , process agricultural products and rear livestock . <p> From irrigation to agro-processing machines , food storage , lighting , refrigeration and more , distributed renewables revolutionize how women use their time , labor , income and productivity . Distributed renewables can lessen the time and energy women spend on laborious tasks and give them some control over savings and new incomes . This could have a positive snowballing effect on health , safety , education and a community 's ability to bounce back from unexpected climate shocks . <p> Southeast Asian countries are not starting from scratch . Nearly $110 billion was invested in renewables in the Asia-Pacific region in 2018 .
