<h> Floods a glimpse of the future as climate warming steps up <p> Inside the damaged Howden Hut on the Routeburn Track , which about 30 people were inside when a landslide crashed into it . <p> OPINION : Torrential rain and flooding in the south of the South Island this week provided a glimpse of events likely to become more frequent as the climate continues warming . <p> This first week of February has delivered about 1000mm of rain in 60 hours to Milford Sound . That huge total in such a short time has badly damaged and closed State Highway 94 between Te Anau and Milford Sound and caused a number of landslides , including one that has smashed a hut on the Routeburn Track , just north of the highway . <p> On Wednesday morning , Southland communities , including residents of Gore , Mataura and Wyndham , were advised to evacuate homes because the Mataura River had breached its banks . <p> NZTA/supplied <p> Part of the Milford Rd was washed away in torrential rain , trapping hundreds of tourists and workers on the road and at Milford Sound . <p> A metre of rain @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , even though Fiordland is a very wet place , as are the western slopes of the Southern Alps . <p> In March 2019 , the Cropp River site farther north in the Southern Alps and inland from Hokitika , recorded a record two-day rainfall of 1086mm . <p> High Country Helicopters <p> Flooding around Gore on Wednesday . <p> Moisture-laden air is constantly crossing the Tasman Sea , bringing frequent rain to the western South Island as the air rises up the slopes of the Southern Alps , cools and loses its freight of moisture . <p> As the climate warms , those heavy rains are getting heavier . That 's because the amount of moisture ( water vapour ) the air can hold goes up rapidly with rising temperature . <p> The 1-degree Celsius or so of warming the globe has experienced in the past century has gone along with an average increase of a few per cent in the amount of moisture the atmosphere contains . <p> When a storm develops , air flows towards the storm centre ( known as " convergence " ) , concentrating @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ storm or flowing over mountains , cools and causes condensation , " wringing out " water from the air through clouds and rain . <p> The science tells us that a 1degC rise in temperature increases atmospheric moisture by 7 per cent , at most . However , when a storm draws air in to converge , the increase in moisture can be much more than that . <p> For high-intensity rains lasting only a few hours , the increase per degree of warming can be up to 20 per cent or more , according to the latest results from the National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research ( Niwa ) . <p> So , as the climate warms , it becomes easier and easier for record rains to occur , as there 's just more moisture in the air . Experiencing 1000mm of rain in two or three days will become more common over time ( on the West Coast at least ) , and new records of 1100mm , then 1200 and 1300mm will be set in future West Coast storms . <p> That poses obvious risks to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ increases the occurrence of land slips and river floods . <p> To mark the turn of the millennium back in late 1999 , I was asked to prepare a weather forecast for January 1 , 3000 , another 1000 years into the future . <p> I envisaged a northwest storm , with up to 1000mm of rain in a day in Fiordland , thinking that was fanciful enough for such a distant future . <p> But given this week 's events , I am thinking we will not have to wait that long at all , less than another 100 years perhaps . That is , unless we knock climate change on the head by cutting emissions of greenhouse gases and getting to the zero-carbon economy as fast as possible . <p> When humanity stops emitting carbon dioxide into the atmosphere , the warming will stop soon after . The future is in our hands . <p> Professor James Renwick is head of the School of Geography , Environment and Earth Sciences at Victoria University of Wellington and is also a member of the Climate Change Commission .
