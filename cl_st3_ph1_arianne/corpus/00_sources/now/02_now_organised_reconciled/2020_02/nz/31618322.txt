<h> Lessons from the Australian bushfires <p> New Zealand is at the forefront disaster risk reduction education within schools , but recent events have highlighted that it 's not enough . Now is not the time to be complacent , writes the University of Auckland 's Carol Mutch . <p> Over the summer , our screens were filled with apocalyptic images from Australia of raging bushfires , charred buildings , traumatised people and injured animals . The smoke that drifted across the Tasman and turned our skies orange reminded us that our lives can change in an instant . <p> Closer to home we might have been following the recovery of those injured in the Whakaari/White Island volcanic explosion , the re-entry into the Pike River Mine or latest opening of post-earthquake developments in Christchurch . We can no longer say , " It wo n't happen here " because it can , it has and it will . <p> Over the last week , I have been helping teachers and principals in Australia prepare for their return to school . I was part of a wider group of academics and arts educators who hosted a seminar in Sydney @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ We provided advice from our research and practice to answer the question that most teachers had in their minds , " What do I do on the first day back ? " <p> My advice was drawn from my research across five disaster settings -- the 2009 Samoan tsunami , the 2010/2011 Canterbury earthquakes , the 2011 Japanese triple disaster , the 2015 Vanuatu cyclone and the 2015 Nepal earthquake . <p> I highlighted that schools take on a range of roles they might not be prepared for -- as relief hubs or community shelters ; as places of safety and security for traumatised students , their families and community members . Principals become crisis managers , dealing with repairs , relocations or rebuilding alongside their roles as educational leaders . They often put the needs of their staff and students before their own -- risking their physical and mental health . Teachers , who often are disaster victims themselves , turn up to school day after day , doing their best to balance teaching the curriculum with helping students process the events they have experienced . While they are @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , they soldier on and do their best . <p> Even though I am back in New Zealand , I am still receiving questions from teachers in bushfire-affected regions : " What do I do when some children want to talk about the bushfires and the others do n't ? " At least , I could provide some suggestions . <p> My main work is in teacher education , yet apart from running some workshops after the March mosque attacks for our pre-service teachers , they receive no other information about their role in disaster preparedness , response and recovery . What the Canterbury earthquakes and the Australian bushfires have highlighted is that these events are far beyond what systems are prepared for . In Canterbury , it was eight years before the mental health needs of children and young people were fully recognised and the Mana Ake programme put in place . In Australia , the scale is beyond what they have dealt with before -- the trauma will be widespread and ongoing -- and schools will be left picking up the pieces as best they can . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ education , yet a once-over-lightly focus on ' Shakeout ' or ' What 's the plan , Stan ? The local community would be informed and engaged in relevant risk identification and planning for possible events . Principals would receive training in crisis management . Teachers would have a basic understanding of their roles from their pre-service teacher education and would receive regular updating in professional development courses . Schools would have more counsellors and social workers to support building resilience and providing after-event trauma screening and support .
