With the care and precision of a high-speed drill in a dentist 's hand , the St. Catharines-based author etches imaginary scenes that are both vivid and memorable . <p> Take these two sentences from The Saturday Night Ghost Club , this year 's One Book One London selection : <p> " The oaks of the Niagara public links rose like broken fingers against the street-lit sky . The expensive hotels rose in columns of cold light to the south , and beyond them the falls were lit by green spotlights , the water rushing endlessly , siphoning away those precious tourist dollars . As one library official told me last fall , the book selection committee wanted to return to the notion of " reading for pleasure . " <p> And pleasurable it is . Does it push the boundaries of our complacencies and challenge our assumptions ? <p> American librarian Nancy Pearl is widely credited with originating the community-wide reading club movement at the Seattle Public Library in 1998 . She named it If All Seattle Read the Same Book , and chose Russell Banks 's novel The Sweet Hereafter as its inaugural title . <p> Since then , Pearl 's project has been replicated hundreds of times across North America . In Arizona , it 's OneBookAZ . In San Francisco , One City One Book . In Iowa , it 's called All Iowa Reads . New York City has One Book One New York . And in Brighton , England , it 's just called City Reads . The goals of these campaigns vary widely : to provoke discussion about important contemporary issues , to bolster community spirit and identity , to improve @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ its critics . The late American literary critic Harold Bloom complained " these mass reading bees " are akin to a city 's populace dashing out to fast-food joints to eat something " horrid , " all at once . Others are concerned about the promotion of groupthink , social engineering , or the cleavages a book can cause in a community . <p> Pearl herself was adamant that One Book projects ought not to be political or a means of social engineering . <p> " Keep in mind that this is a library program , " she said . " It 's not an exercise in civics . It 's not intended to have literature cure the racial divide . This is about a work of literature . " <p> Fair enough . And by that standard , The Saturday Night Ghost Club fits the bill . It is entertaining escapism bound to kindle a love of story and the embrace of literacy . <p> Those challenges , however , are already the explicit mandate of dozens of schools , English teachers , bookstores and small library branches across @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ every bedtime-story-reading parent in town . <p> One Book One London , however , represents an opportunity to do something more : to animate a city , too often subdivided by ward , neighbourhood , economic class , or social malady , to talk about a common experience . To once a year gather around a literary hearth to discuss issues common to us all . To wrestle with who we are as a city and reflect on our common biases and perceptions . Maybe even to dream a little . We congregate often enough -- think the public gallery at city hall -- to argue opposing sides of issues that divide us . <p> Other One Book projects in other cities have found no shortage of books that address themes that should concern us too : human rights , poverty , climate change , injustice , aging , social isolation and so on . <p> With due respect to both Davidson and Pearl , One Book One London should represent a unique , once-a-year opportunity in which our superb , taxpayer-funded library system moderates a series of important community-wide @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ become .
