<p> In the run-up to the 1993 election , it was clear that deficits and debt would be an issue . The Mulroney government had been banging on about them for nearly a decade , even as they grew . The Liberals under Jean Chr ? tien had their " Red Book " of promises that laid out a pathway to reducing ( though not eliminating ) the deficit . <p> It would be wrong to say there was a consensus among academic economists that deficits had to be radically reduced , but that was certainly the dominant view , the orthodoxy . Nonetheless , there was a cadre of respected left-leaning economists who dissented , arguing for economic stimulation rather than retrenchment . <p> For the NDP , this presented an exquisite problem . Their supporters in the union movement and the wider public were bound to be particularly hard-hit by the spending cuts that an attack on the deficit would surely entail . Their leader , Audrey McLaughlin , tried to use the thinking of those dissenting economists to argue that a policy of economic expansion aimed at creating jobs and ending the recession of the day @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <p> But the media judged McLaughlin 's views severely , based on the economic orthodoxy , which was supported enthusiastically by the business community . Her economic program was treated as unserious , neglecting the officially designated Most Important Issue of the Day . Not just the columnists , but many news reports were withering . And the NDP has been living with this ever since . Witness the fact that , 22 years later , in the election of 2015 , the NDP 's Thomas Mulcair felt he needed to be tougher on the deficit than Justin Trudeau , the ultimate winner . <p> Why am I bringing up this hoary old tale ? Because the political system -- indeed , humanity -- faces a far more significant challenge that even the most hysterical worriers about the deficit ever argued . Indeed , it is an existential challenge . And this is not playing out in the context of an academic orthodoxy challenged by serious dissenters . In fact , in the scientific world , it is no longer true to say there is " consensus " on @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ it . <p> No one with expertise regarded seriously by his or her scientific peers doubts that we are in a period of profound climate change , that its effects are overtaking us at this very moment , and that huge alterations need to be made to our economy and lifestyles to avoid a climate catastrophe that will damage our lives and possibly even wreck those of our children . <p> Last October , the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change ( IPCC ) released a special report , produced by 91 of the world 's leading climate scientists , relying on roughly 6,000 scientific studies . It projected that by 2040 -- just 21 years away -- the world would be ravaged by intensifying food shortages , swamped coastlines and raging wildfires . That is the year , on current trends , that the world 's temperature will reach 1.5 C above pre-industrial levels . Like all science , these conclusions are , to a degree , tentative . The polar ice caps are melting faster than was expected just a few years ago . The IPCC report 's projections @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ low side . But the direction in which humanity is headed is simply not a matter of scientific dispute , even if there remains a political debate about what to do about it . <p> The IPCC report said it would take massive reforms to our economy in very short order to avert this rolling human tragedy , which will only mount with each year that we and our leaders fail to act on the scale of the danger . The result , of course , will be an economic as well as an environmental disaster . <p> We are now entering an election year in Canada , in which issues related to climate change -- the carbon tax and pipelines -- are likely to be at the centre of the debate among the parties . We are lucky in comparison with the United States and the United Kingdom , for example , where Trump and Brexit have squeezed the media space devoted to climate change . <p> But reporters here also need to be careful that in reporting on climate-related issues , they do not unthinkingly fall into some @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ those is the tendency for reporters to rely on the parties and their leaders to critique each other : step back , let the politicians argue , and then let the people decide . That may make sense when the political debate is about whether to raise or lower the corporate tax rate , for example , or whether to establish pharmacare . These are at least partly questions of social values . <p> But just as the media once held Audrey McLaughlin to account on deficits -- on considerably weaker grounds -- in this election year , journalists should be holding our politicians to account , not just to each other , but to the scientifically established facts of climate change . News stories on the carbon tax , pipelines , and similar matters should routinely take time and space to root the debate in what we know from authorities , such as the IPCC , about the scale and pace of climate change . It is part of the media 's job , when a politician advances a policy , to point out that the motivation may be to win support among seniors , or Westerners , or unions or farmers or small businesses . But this kind of horserace coverage also systematically underweights the likely substantive effects of policy on our collective future . <p> The horserace is naturally fascinating to every political reporter . I know , because I spent much of my career chronicling it . But elections nowadays -- elections in which climate change is an important issue -- should not be covered the way we watch a horserace or a hockey game , cheering on our favourite but going home afterward having been entertained , whoever wins or loses .
