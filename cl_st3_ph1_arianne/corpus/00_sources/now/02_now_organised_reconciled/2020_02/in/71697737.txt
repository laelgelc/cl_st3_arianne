<h> Last Updated : Tuesday 18 February 2020 <p> Countries submitted their first round of national climate targets -- Nationally Determined Contributions ( NDCs ) -- in 2015 . They are legally obliged to increase their individual and cumulative level of climate ambition in 2020 . This is not just a matter of advocacy . It is a legal requirement in the Paris Agreement . <p> Article 4.9 of the agreement states : <p> " Each Party shall communicate a nationally determined contribution every five years ... " . <p> That is not the full text of the article , but it is the crux of the obligation . The point is to get countries into the habit of thinking systematically about the climate in their domestic planning processes . <p> Article 4.3 gives direction to this planning process . It states that : <p> " Each Party 's successive nationally determined contribution will represent a progression beyond the Party 's then current nationally determined contribution and reflect its highest possible ambition , reflecting its common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities , in the light of different national circumstances . " <p> This means that parties must continuously @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ submitting the same targets every five years and over , or submit less ambitious targets than their previous efforts . <p> There is a legalistic argument made in some quarters that the agreement does not require new targets in 2020 . " <p> We will come to the distinction between the Paris Agreement and the Decision 1 / CP21 ( aka the ' Paris Decision ' ) in a bit . Regardless of whether your 2015 NDC has set a target for the year 2025 or the year 2030 , these paragraphs ( read with Article 4 of the agreement ) highlight 2020 as a year to increase ambition . <p> One could split hair over the difference between communicating a new NDC or updating the 2015 NDC , but there is no legal argument for any country to just sit on its hands in 2020 . <p> An anti-ambition advocate could also point to the first words in these paragraphs -- " requests " -- to argue that this is not a binding requirement , merely a @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ -- Article 4.9 of the Paris Agreement , which these paragraphs also refer back to . There is no legally binding target , but the obligation to regularly set national targets is binding . There is a less common misconception that treaties are binding while decisions are not binding -- that is also not quite true . <p> Decisions made within the authority of pre-existing treaties can have legal force , depending on their language . The Paris Decision was taken by the Conference of Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change , so it is ( at first glance ) within the authority of a pre-existing treaty . <p> Besides , the language of the Decision refers back to the legally binding Paris Agreement , so it can not be used to dilute the legally binding nature of that agreement . The ' GST ' is an accountability mechanism outlined in Article 14 of the agreement . It periodically takes stock of the globe 's mitigation efforts , to inform the next round of targets . The first full GST is scheduled for 2023 . <p> NDCs are supposed to take into account the outcome from the GST process ( when it is available ) , but there is nothing that legally delays the next round of NDCs to after 2023 . A specific definite explanation is impossible , but here are some informed guesses . <p> First , climate politics is highly influenced by United States ( US ) domestic politics . Technically , the US Constitution requires new treaties to be ratified by two-thirds of the US Senate .
