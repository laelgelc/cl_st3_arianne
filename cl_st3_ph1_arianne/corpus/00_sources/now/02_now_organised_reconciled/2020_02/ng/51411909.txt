<p> It is fashionable to leave Davos and reflect , ' was it worth the trek ? ' This year I think it most certainly was . I noticed some big differences . <p> Firstly , there are no urgent economic crises , waiting to be managed . Economies are growing ( slowly ) . Unemployment is low . Inflation is way below targets , and central banks have been systemically lowering interest rates ( 49 central banks have made 71 cuts in rates ) . <p> The economic issues are technical and nuanced , such as the effect of negative rates increasing the risks in the search for yield . You get the idea : nothing for the reasonable man or woman on the Davos omnibus to worry about . <p> That left room for the issue of climate change . We arrived with thumping huge announcements , first from Blackrock that it would begin divesting its carbon stocks , and then from Microsoft -- pledging to go carbon negative ( and soon ) . Big pledges for the biggest of big issues . <p> Perhaps , though , what struck me most was the difference in the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ <p> On one side there were venture capitalists , private equity types and investors who mingled with the technology CEOs who were looking for funding . On the other side were the social leaders , concerned with diversity , digital privacy , the gender gap , developing world issues and , of course , the environment . <p> At first blush , you 'd think the two would be like oil and water . Well , that 's what I always thought . <p> I was wrong . Together they found much common ground . Techies and investors are very interested in digital privacy and empowering the poorest in the world ( and gaining market share on the way ) . The social leaders love technology and recognise -- properly regulated -- what a force for good it can be . <p> Their meeting of minds was helped by the fact most of them are under 40 , and so have a very different view of inclusion than the older generation . <p> The two key protagonists in Davos could hardly be further apart in age or philosophy .
