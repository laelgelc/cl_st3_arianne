<p> Born of a sense of abundance rather than sacrifice , chef and writer Tom Hunt ' s first foray into reducing food waste was a meal for many . In making hundreds of dishes with organic produce that would have otherwise been lost to the dumpster , his so-called Forgotten Feast extended beyond the fundraiser itself . Bolstered by the fact that as an individual , he had made a measurable difference , it ended up being a catalyst . <p> " The reason I set myself on this journey of climate change campaigning and focusing on food sustainability is because I fed those 200 people , and there was a massive ripple effect from that one event , " says Hunt . " The Forgotten Feast was about celebrating the solutions rather than talking about what 's wrong , and celebrating good food in its entirety from root-to-fruit . " <p> Over the past decade , Hunt has created an integrated approach to eating -- one that emphasizes both enjoyment and the environment . Based on a body of research and the UN 's Sustainable Development Goals , his root-to-fruit philosophy -- which is at the heart @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and Planet -- is a means of tapping into " holistic " thinking . As with his salvaged spread , the book is " a celebration of plenty , " first and foremost . <p> " I 've written it to be actionable and as simple as possible for people to follow , " says Hunt , " which is why the three key principles -- eat for pleasure , eat whole foods , eat the best food you can -- are quite loose and philosophical ways to approach food . Rather than it being a really strict and basic list of things you can do to improve your impact on the environment . " <p> The food system -- from soil to table -- generates roughly one-quarter of the globe 's greenhouse gas emissions . According to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization , if food waste were a country , it would be among the top three carbon emitters .
