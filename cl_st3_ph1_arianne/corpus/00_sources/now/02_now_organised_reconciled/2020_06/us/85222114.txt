<h> The game theory of using geoengineering to fight climate change <p> The possibility of employing geoengineeringcould help break the political deadlock on a global climate change deal , according to a new paper . <p> Why it matters : Deliberately trying to engineer the climate to offset warming is risky and as yet untested . But with the effects of climate change compounding and further international agreements stalled , there may be no choice but to try -- or at least threaten to do so . <p> Solar geoengineering would be much cheaper than drastically cutting carbon emissions , but it carries with it as yet unknown risks of side effects . That 's why many environmentalists remain opposed to even experimenting with geoengineering . <p> Yes , but : A new paper in the journal Humanities &amp; Social Sciences Communications suggests that the possibility of geoengineering could reframe the climate debate . <p> Using game theory ( more or less as described in this scene from " A Beautiful Mind " ) , Gernot Wagner and Adrien Fabre make the case that countries most vulnerable to climate change and most willing to engage in deep emissions cuts @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , rather than accept insufficient climate action . <p> At the same time , those countries less worried about climate change might be more willing to compromise on deeper emission cuts if they fear that the other side would choose geoengineering over a weaker climate deal . <p> The upshot is that just the existence of the option of geoengineering can nudge the two opposing sides into agreeing on tougher climate action . <p> Yes , but : Game theory aside , the worse climate change gets , the more likely one or more countries might try geoengineering to save themselves .
