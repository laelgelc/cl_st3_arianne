<p> ( The opinions expressed here are those of the author , a columnist for Reuters . ) <p> By Clyde Russell <p> LAUNCESTON , Australia , June 23 ( Reuters ) - Australia 's mining lobby group , the Minerals Council of Australia , long derided by environmentalists as coal-loving dinosaurs , has launched a climate action plan that is probably significant for what it does n't say than what it does . <p> On the surface the council 's report looks reasonable , committing to an ambition of net zero emissions and outlining a series of actions aimed at achieving the goal . <p> But the document , released on Monday , is short on specifics , with many of the action points and activities couched in vague language that is open to interpretation . <p> An example is the activity point on " supporting adaptation " . <p> " Understand the types of adaptation investments needed in the minerals sector , especially in regard to operations , employee health , supply chains , water use , energy resources and local communities , to help minimise the adverse impacts of a changing climate , " the report @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and words , but leaves the reader wondering what exactly the group is proposing and what would " adaptation investments " actually look like . <p> Leaving aside the verbosity , it 's the lack of specifics that is probably the report 's most unsatisfactory aspect . <p> There is no time frame for committing to net zero emissions , and there are no time frames specified for any of the action points , even those that seem fairly specific . <p> It calls for the " widespread deployment of low and zero emissions technologies " , but does n't spell out what that means . <p> Does this imply the council supports moving all mining equipment from diesel power to electric ? Does it support using renewable energies such as wind and solar , and battery storage , to power remote mine sites ? <p> The council merely says it supports these moves , but then it also supports a whole range of other measures , so gaining a sense of the priorities is challenging . <p> Elsewhere in the report the council says there are opportunities for pursuing @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and deploying low emissions technologies including renewables , hydrogen , proven , safe and reliable Carbon Capture Utilisation and Storage ( CCUS ) and advanced nuclear solutions , as well as digitisation , automation , and electrification " . <p> But the council does n't specify which one of the plethora of above ideas is high on the agenda , and the inclusion of widely discounted technologies such as CCUS would appear to be little more than a sop to the coal mining industry . <p> Outside of the coal sector and its political promoters , it 's hard to find any credible researcher , analyst or industry leader who believes CCUS is viable from a cost or scale perspective . <p> The council 's mention of nuclear power also appears designed to distract from other issues , given Australia 's policy of no nuclear power and widespread public support for that position . <h> RENEWABLE ENERGY MINERALS <p> Where the report is on more solid ground is the recognition that mining will be vital to produce the metals needed to drive a switch to cleaner energy and technologies . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ being the world 's top exporter of iron ore and coking coal , the country also has significant other advantages . <p> It ranks fifth in world copper and cobalt output and has the second-largest reserves of copper , cobalt and bauxite . It is ranked third in reserves of lithium and rare earths . <p> Australia is the world 's top producer of bauxite , the base for aluminium , in which it ranks number five in global output , according to the government agency Geosciences Australia . <p> The council is correct to focus on developing these minerals in a push to drive the uptake of renewables , but at no point in the report does it talk about coal , other than a brief mention about a demonstration carbon capture and storage project . <p> It 's hard to see how Australia could achieve a net zero emissions mining industry while remaining the world 's largest exporter of coking coal used to manufacture steel , and the second-largest thermal coal exporter . <p> Of course , the council may not be considering the emissions created once the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ is no mention of this in the four-page report . <p> If the council wishes to be taken seriously on climate change , it will have to do considerably more to flesh out its plans . At best , the report is a reasonable first statement of intent , but it falls short of being a comprehensive roadmap . ( Editing by Himani Sarkar )
