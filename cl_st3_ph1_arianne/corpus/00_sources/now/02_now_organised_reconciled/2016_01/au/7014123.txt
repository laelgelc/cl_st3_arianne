<h> The alternative to Uber 's surge pricing is n't fair either <p> Uber 's surge pricing seems to offend our sense of egalitarianism , but it 's because of this feature that the service is constantly available for those who need it , writes Chris Berg . <p> On the morning of New Year 's Eve , the ridesharing company Uber sent its customers an email warning of increased prices particularly between 12:30am and 4am the next day , the time that revellers were likely to want to go home at the same time . <p> Nevertheless , on January 1 , there was an inevitable spate of outraged press stories where customers complained about the extremely high prices charged by Uber during those peak hours . In some cities , Uber prices were nearly 10 times the normal price . One person paid $720 for a ride from Sydney to Blacktown . <p> This ca n't have been a surprise . The price is set by an algorithm . As well as the email that morning , Uber notified riders of the surge prices and required @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ ride request . The Uber smartphone application also allows riders to estimate fares in advance . <p> One suspects that more than a few of these unhappy riders were " tired and emotional " , in that charming media euphemism . <p> The case for what Uber calls " surge pricing " is simple . Uber drivers can not be simply forced to work at the busiest or most inconvenient times . They have to be enticed to drive on New Year 's Eve - an evening where many drivers would probably rather be partying than ferrying passengers . Higher prices are enticing . This is basic supply and demand stuff . Surge prices also encourage drivers already on the road to go to where demand is highest . In the absence of surge pricing , there would almost certainly be shortages and queues . <p> Uber did not invent market incentives . The company just exploits them . Allowing for demand-driven pricing is one of Uber 's best features : it ensures the service is constantly available for those who need it . When a smaller surge occurred during the Sydney siege , the outrage was worse , as it seemed like Uber was profiting from the city 's fragile state . <p> Supporters of markets have a habit of sometimes dismissing these concerns out of hand , but they should n't . Thomas Aquinas argued that the just price was what a just person would agree to . Others believed the just price was whatever the prevailing local market price was . <p> This final explanation might seem a cop-out but it packed an intellectual punch in the medieval period . According to these proto-free marketeers , the just price was that which could be freely and voluntarily agreed to by two independent agents .
