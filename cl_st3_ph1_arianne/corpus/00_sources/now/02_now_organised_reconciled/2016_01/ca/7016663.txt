<p> On last year 's Family Day , after weeks of unseasonable warmth , officials at Castle Mountain Resort in Alberta gave up . In the middle of what should have been the busiest time of the ski season , the resort , having received less than a third of its usual annual snowfall , closed for the year . " The weather has been against us , " said general manager Brad Brush in a news release . <p> The mild winter took other ski-industry casualties too . Resorts throughout Canada and the U.S. , including Mount Washington and Schweitzer , closed early . Hemlock Resort near Vancouver did n't open at all . Things looked dire for the ski industry . <p> Then , a new season began , and brought a massive dumping of early season snow . That conversation inevitably leads to discussions of climate change , but perhaps not in the way most people might expect . Dire predictions say climate change could decimate the ski industry in the long run -- some scenarios predict a devastating loss of mountain snowpack by 2100 . In the shorter-term , officials are realizing that changing climate patterns may not spell doom for skiing -- they may actually benefit the high-elevation resorts of Alberta -- but it may spell doom for predictable , reliable weather . For the ski industry , unpredictability is becoming the new normal , and the industry is starting to adjust . <p> Take Lake Louise , for example .
