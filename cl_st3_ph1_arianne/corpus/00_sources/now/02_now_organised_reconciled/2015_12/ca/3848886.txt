<h> SCHNEIDEREIT : Here 's how to make sense of Paris climate talks <p> U.S. President Barack Obama , left , and other world leaders applaud as they pose for a group photo at the United Nations Climate Change Conference outside Paris on Monday . ( JACKY NAEGELEN/AP ) <p> Despite the tankerloads of ink -- and their cyberspace equivalent -- now being spilled on the possibility of a groundbreaking deal to control global warming coming out of the current super summit in Paris , let 's cut to the chase , shall we ? <p> Unless the world falls down the rabbit hole and conducts these talks in Wonderland , the Paris talks -- which wind up Dec. 11 -- wo n't lead to legally binding targets on nations ' greenhouse gas emissions . <p> There 'll likely be targets , all right , called Intended Nationally Determined Contributions ( INDCs ) , but countries will set them themselves and will face no penalties -- beyond global tut-tutting -- if they fail to meet them . <p> By the way , of the 90-some INDCs apparently set @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ couple have been judged sufficient to meet the announced goal of keeping the world 's temperature rise to a maximum of 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels . <p> But I digress . <p> Though hardly alone in this position among the players , the United States ' negotiators -- key to any global deal -- are extremely unlikely to agree to any targets that would be legally binding . <p> The reason is simple . Any such agreement would significantly impact U.S. laws , giving it the status of an international treaty . Under the American constitution , all international treaties signed by the president must be ratified by two-thirds of the U.S. Senate before they can take effect . <p> The likelihood of the current U.S. Senate approving such a treaty are roughly on par with expecting former prime minister Stephen Harper , now an opposition MP representing Calgary Heritage , to jump from the Tories to the Green party . <p> In other words , unthinkable . <p> Current Republican majority control of the U.S. Senate has been blamed for that state of affairs . But let 's @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ was contemplating signing a legally binding international climate change treaty setting emissions limits . <p> In 1997 , as President Bill Clinton 's administration was involved in talks that would ultimately lead to the first Kyoto protocol , the Senate voted 95 to 0 -- a cross-partisan show of unity by Republicans and Democrats -- to lay out the conditions any climate treaty had to meet to be acceptable . <p> That shot across Clinton 's bow included two key provisions . First , that the treaty could impose no greenhouse gas emission limits on the U.S. unless developing nations also accepted such limits . Second , that the treaty could not result in serious harm to the U.S. economy . <p> The following year , Clinton signed Kyoto , which imposed no emission limits on developing nations . But that was simply for dramatic effect . The president -- knowing the treaty had zero chance of being ratified -- never submitted it to the Senate . The U.S. was never part of Kyoto . <p> President Barack Obama is in a similar position .
