<h> Public attitudes are turning in favour of cutting emissions <p> People take part in an internationally organized march called the Global Climate March , in Vancouver , B.C. on Sunday , Nov. 29 , 2015 . The protest is targeted at sending a message demanding climate action to world leaders meeting at the Paris Climate Conference . <p> When it comes to concern about important public issues , citizens are most likely to worry about topics that impact their day-to-day lives . We worry about whether the economy will be strong enough to sustain our jobs and maintain the value of our homes . We care about the quality of the health care we will receive if we get sick , and we express frustration about the state of the roads and bridges we drive on every day . <p> So , when polling firms ask the classic question about " what the most important issue is facing your community , " the environment rarely scores high on the list . National surveys suggest the same thing , which tends to lead to the false conclusion that the environment -- and specifically , the threat of global warming -- is not a priority for Canadians . <p> As the United Nations Climate Conference continues in Paris , there is a great deal of evidence showing Canadians and citizens of other industrialized countries want their governments to make a binding commitment to reducing greenhouse gas emissions ( GHGs ) . Citizens are also willing to dig into their own pockets as part of an effort to cut emissions . This is a major shift , as in the past , high levels of concern about the environment could not be squared with specific proposals to introduce carbon taxes and other methods of encouraging people to emit fewer GHGs . <p> A survey conducted earlier this year by the Environics Institute in partnership with the David Suzuki Foundation found six in 10 Canadians are in favour of Canada joining with other countries to sign an international agreement to reduce emissions @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of pact . <p> What is particularly interesting is support for Canada signing onto a new international agreement on climate change has increased in the past four years . Between September and November 2009 -- when the worldwide economic crisis was deepening and environmental issues were pushed to the back burner -- the proportion of Canadians who wanted the country to join an international climate agreement dropped from 68 per cent to 59 per cent . The figure fell further to a low of 56 per cent in 2011 before increasing to the current mark of 61 per cent . <p> This shows that even though Canadians are still concerned about the economy , they also want to see their federal and provincial governments take action on climate change . Reducing greenhouse gas emissions is not an " either/or " scenario , which it has often been framed as by the former federal Conservative government during the past seven years . <p> Even in the United States -- which never joined the Kyoto Protocol and where Republican politicians in Congress are extremely allergic to the idea of joining an international climate @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ sign a binding agreement that would force the country to reduce greenhouse gas emissions , according to a New York Times/CBS News poll released this week . <p> This shift in opinion on both sides of the Canada-U.S. border may be in part because citizens are starting to see the social and economic consequences of climate change . For example , the state of California forced communities to cut their water usage last year after years of crippling drought . A Probe Research survey conducted on behalf of the San Diego County Water Authority in March found 62 per cent of residents in that Southern California community were in favour of mandatory water restrictions being imposed on citizens . There is a direct economic and social cost to a warming planet that is only going to increase as climate patterns become more erratic and the weather becomes more intense . <p> Citizens seem to understand this and are not instinctively opposed to policies that would give them a nudge toward reducing GHG emissions . In 2008 , former Liberal leader St ? phane Dion 's proposal to introduce a carbon tax was swiftly rebranded by the Conservatives as a " tax on everything " and economic voodoo ( despite the fact in the same year , British Columbia also introduced a provincial carbon tax ) . <p> Today , a majority of Canadians ( 58 per cent ) are in favour of a B.C.-style carbon tax being levied in their own province , according to the Environics Institute survey .
