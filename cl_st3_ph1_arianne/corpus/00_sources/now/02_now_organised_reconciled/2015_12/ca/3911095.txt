<h> Plenty of hot air at Paris climate summit <p> James Hansen , one of the first scientists to warn about global warming , calls the just-completed Paris climate summit a " fraud . " <p> And , of course , he is right . <p> Strictly speaking , the summit was fraudulent . It committed none of the 195 nations attending to do anything substantive to combat global warming . <p> It recycled goals of past United Nations climate-change summits without providing a mechanism for attaining them . <p> It was welcomed by countries that emit large quantities of greenhouse gases because , ultimately , it required nothing of them . <p> Still , the Paris agreement could end up being a useful fraud . Certainly , it is easy to see why the summit 's results have come under attack from both right and left . <p> The agreement itself is largely empty . It requires signatories to " communicate and maintain " plans to combat climate change . It does n't say these plans must be sufficiently robust to meet the summit 's minimum goal @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ century to less than 2 C. <p> This is important since , according to the UN , the 188 national plans submitted to date ( including Canada 's ) are far too weak to meet either this target or the summit 's more ambitious goal of 1.5 C. <p> Similarly , the agreement calls on member nations to reduce carbon emissions to a level where the amount produced is offset by the amount absorbed -- all by the end of the century . <p> But it does not require signatories to achieve this goal . <p> Indeed , the only legally binding element of the agreement is a requirement that nations update their unenforceable carbon reduction plans every five years . If they do n't , there are no penalties . <p> A companion document repeats a pledge , first made in 2009 , to have rich nations transfer at least $100 billion annually to developing countries to help them combat climate change . <p> Here , too , reality does not live up to the rhetoric .
