ERIC FEFERBERG / AFP/Getty Images <p> As negotiations are underway at the United Nations Climate Conference in Paris , the debate is increasingly shifting toward the moral imperative for adopting a new climate deal . But the reality remains that the economy matters . Many industrial heavyweights located in jurisdictions with a carbon price , like the aluminum industry in Quebec , face pressures when their competitors are n't subject to similar restrictions . Whether international trade issues are included or left out @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ will succeed , or will fail like the last deal , the Kyoto Protocol . <p> Many factors contributed to the death the Kyoto Protocol , but none was more critical than the failure of governments to align the goals of climate policies with those of free-trade agreements . The difference between the climate and trade regimes is key . In the first case , if a government does n't meet its greenhouse-gas targets , it can pull out of the UN agreement with few consequences beyond a momentary blow to its international credibility . But if a government breaks trade laws , it can be challenged before a tribunal and required to take measures that have economic consequences . <p> The sad truth is that the only years that have seen global greenhouse gases ( GHGs ) trends decline are those coinciding with periods of global economic downturns . This comes as no surprise as , historically , increases in GHGs follow growth in global GDP enabled by our access to cheap fossil fuels : oil , gas and coal . <p> Economic growth generally occurs because of increases in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and trade . While the prices of fossil fuels are determined by international markets , the cost of GHGs are not accounted for , and the consequence is climate change , with the costs falling on everyone . <p> The UN and some governments have worked to bring GHGs into the global economy by developing carbon pricing schemes like carbon taxes or markets . This is the case of Quebec , British Columbia and Alberta , for example . Canada 's Ecofiscal Commission recently argued that governments and businesses in Canada should not perceive carbon pricing as a significant economic threat . But , as they noted , many details missing from their analysis are needed for a more thorough account . So far , the implementation of such schemes has been inconsistent across regions and has had little impact in curbing global GHGs . The result : GHGs associated with global economic growth currently trump any progress made by the combined efforts of existing national and international climate-change policies . <p> To boost the efficiency of carbon pricing schemes , nothing prevents governments from agreeing to implement trade mechanisms , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ costs that carbon pricing imposes on traded goods . However , in practice , few have yet seriously negotiated or implemented such mechanisms . Addressing carbon pricing through trade rules will level the playing field for businesses , help overcome free riders in international climate policies and reduce the risk that heavily emitting industries will relocate to other jurisdictions to avoid regulations . <p> Solving climate change is undoubtedly the greatest challenge of our time . In Paris , negotiators are faced with deciding whether or not climate measures adopted by countries can constitute barriers to international trade . Concerns over the loss of competitiveness of exports sold on international markets may push some countries with economies heavily dependent on fossil fuels , like China and India , to prevent other governments from resorting to any form of unilateral measures against goods and services on grounds related to climate change .
