<h> The IPCC 's Himalayan Error Sheds Light on Peer Review <p> The Fourth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change ( IPCC ) , released in 2007 , contains an error regarding the expected rate of glacier loss in the Himalayas . A new paper takes this error as a case study , and uses it to examine more broadly the practice of peer review -- a key element in the production of IPCC Assessment Reports -- and the role of peer review in science and at the science-public boundary . <p> The paper , " ' Peer Review is Melting Our Glaciers ' : What Led the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change ( IPCC ) to Go Astray ? , " by Laszlo Kosolosky , takes a comprehensive look at the error , which suggested that Himalayan glaciers might disappear by the year 2035 , when in fact most projections indicate that at least some ice will remain in coming centuries . Comments that would have stopped the error from being made were overlooked during the review process , their analysis found . Kosolosky adds some context that helps account for these errors : the enormity of the task of sorting through and replying to all comments ( almost 40,000 for the section of the report , Working Group II , in which the reference to glaciers appeared ) and the unavoidable reliance on " grey literature . " This non-peer reviewed literature might appear less worthy of inclusion in the IPCC 's reports , but includes highly valuable information , without which the IPCC 's job would become nearly impossible , Kosolosky writes . <p> Kosolosky uses the case study of the IPCC 's error as an avenue to discuss the role of peer review and also science in general . This character of peer review conflicts with the demands posed on institutions such as the IPCC : " Society demands that science present true claims , whereas science , through peer review , aims only to rule out unacceptable claims .
