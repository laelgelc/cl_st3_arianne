<h> Turn the knobs on a new interactive map and see what future emissions , adaptation strategies mean for world hunger <p> Well , not quite , but officials from the UN 's World Food Programme and the Met Office Hadley Centre today unveiled a website to attendees of the 2015 Paris Climate Conference that lets users control future emissions and climate adaptation scenarios , and see what impact they will have on global food security . <p> You can select from present day , the 2050s and the 2080s , and examine what each country 's food security vulnerability will be depending on emissions and adaptation . <p> " This map paints a stark picture of how climate disasters drive hunger . In Paris , we must decide between a future world where ending hunger is achievable -- or one where we and every future generation continue this losing struggle responding to the scourge of global hunger , " said Ertharin Cousin , executive director of the World Food Programme in a statement . <p> The most vulnerable countries are in sub-Saharan Africa -- a region where @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ both South and Central America are vulnerable as well . <p> The interactive map paints a drastically different picture depending on your level of optimism . Take a country like Mozambique -- highly agricultural and full of subsistence farmers , but prone to climate change-related impacts such as increased floods , drought and tornadoes . It is , in fact , one of the most disaster prone countries in the world . <p> It is estimated that about one-third of their population ( 26 million people ) is already " chronically " food insecure . Under a scenario of low emissions and high climate adaptation , Mozambique 's vulnerability to food shortages rises an additional 8 percent by the 2050s . <p> But , with high emissions and no adaptation , food insecurity rises 36 percent higher than today . And , projecting this scenario to the 2080s , the country 's food insecurity increases 60 percent over present day numbers . <p> The World Food Programme 's most recent estimates find that globally about 795 million people already do not have enough food to be healthy . But the map -- which works by using climate projections , economic models , and current food availability stats -- shows we can curb how many people will suffer . <p> And that large swing in hunger prevalence in places like Mozambique will be , in part , determined by what comes out of Paris this month , where delegates from 195 countries descended this week to the United Nations climate change meeting dubbed COP21 , or the twenty-first meeting of the Conference of Parties . <p> The goal ? A legally binding agreement to keep the warming of the planet 's average temperature below 2 degrees Celsius over the temperature since the Industrial Revolution . <p> The linchpin of such an accord will be an agreement on major global carbon emission reductions .
