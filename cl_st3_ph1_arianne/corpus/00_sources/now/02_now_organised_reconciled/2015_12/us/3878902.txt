BIODYNAMICS ARE A BIG DEAL <p> Winemaking changed radically about 50 years ago , thanks to a mechanical and chemical overhaul in farming . This shift -- coupled with the rise of omnipresent , " international " grapes and the use of additives -- produced a safer , more consistent product . Too consistent , some might say . And certainly too chemical . <p> In " Natural Resistance , " the recent film by Jonathan Nossiter , a winemaker holds two handfuls of vineyard dirt for the camera . One , farmed with chemicals , is pale and hard and " smells like laundry detergent . " The other , organically farmed , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ death , " says Nossiter . <p> A revolution has been building quietly in the world of wine . Organically farmed vineyard acreage more than tripled from 2004 to 2013 . And a growing number of winemakers are taking things one step further . Biodynamic agriculture , based on the 1924 teachings of Austrian philosopher Rudolf Steiner , treats the farm holistically . Chemicals are forbidden ; companion crops ward off pests ; moon cycles govern times to plant and pick . The system self-regulates without recourse to poison . Strange ? Sure . But the results routinely silence critics . Ch ? teau Pontet-Canet converted to biodynamic methods in 2005 ; the winery 's 2008 Bordeaux was hailed as one of the best of the vintage . Demeter Association , an organization that certifies biodynamic farms , reports a steady 10% to 15% annual growth in applications since 2006 . <p> With luck , that trend will produce a future full of funky , distinctive , and wildly diverse wines -- a welcome return to the not-so-distant past . <p> 5 .
