<h> Pattrick Smellie : A lesson in terrible political timing <p> Three years ago , with great fanfare at Parliament , the then Minister of Energy , Hekia Parata , awarded the first oil and gas exploration permits under the Government 's new Block Offer programme . <p> At that time , a barrel of West Texas Intermediate crude oil was fetching US$105 ( NZ$155 ) a barrel and the room buzzed with enthusiasm for this new way of encouraging exploration of this country 's large , but lightly explored hydrocarbon basins . <p> On Wednesday , a rather smaller group assembled in the Beehive Banquet Hall for Energy Minister Simon Bridges to announce results of the fourth round of Block Offers . <p> While nine exploration blocks were awarded -- three onshore and six offshore -- the total work programme for all nine is a paltry $4.4 million , compared with 15 permits last year and work programmes worth $110m . <p> None of this year 's blocks are in frontier basins and no new industry players emerged . One of the biggest local players -- Shell @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Shell has announced a " strategic review " of its assets here and may pull out after more than a century of operations in New Zealand . <p> New Zealand Oil &amp; Gas and Texas-based Anadarko were present , but did n't participate in this year 's bidding . Anadarko is looking to delay some of its exploration work and has a decision on offshore Canterbury drilling due by next April . <p> Coming just four days after a new global commitment to combat climate change , a limp ceremony for the oil industry could n't have been worse political timing . <p> The local head of Greenpeace , Russel Norman , gleefully pronounced this " another nail in the coffin of New Zealand 's oil programme . " <p> However , what must really hurt is the fact that oil is a dog of an industry to be in right now . That West Texas crude price , above US$100 in 2011 , is barely touching US$36 a barrel today . <p> Some see oil sinking as low as US$20 a barrel and this week 's Treasury forecasts assume oil @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ consumption in developed economies and ongoing over-production mean limited opportunities for New Zealand oil exploration . <p> All over the world , oil companies are scaling back , abandoning high cost , long shot exploration . <p> Shell NZ 's announcement is part of that trend . <p> It owns 83.75 percent of the Maui oil and gas field , 48 percent of Pohokura , and 50 percent of Kapuni , collectively worth billions of dollars even at current oil and gas prices , but it 's not clear who might buy those assets in the current climate . <p> Among possible contenders are locally owned Todd Energy and the Austrian oil producer , OMV , both of whom are partners with Shell in the Maui and Pohokura fields . A mid-sized Australian player like Woodside Petroleum might have a look , although Kupe operator Origin Energy has too many problems of its own in Australia to be a serious contender . <p> A bottom-feeding opportunist might also emerge if they think there 's a bargain to be had . <p> Shell 's interests in three deep-sea exploration licences , two @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ New Caledonia Basin , may be harder to shift . <p> The cost in jobs would not be great if Shell were to leave . Most of its workforce is part of the joint venture that would continue to operate the existing producing fields , and to produce revenue for new owners and royalties for the Crown . <p> But it is not drawing too long a bow to suggest actions like Shell 's are harbingers of an industry in decline , not just here , but everywhere in the next few decades .
