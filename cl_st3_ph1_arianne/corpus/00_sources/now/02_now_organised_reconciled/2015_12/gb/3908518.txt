<p> As regular as Christmas -- " It ca n't be TheBedside Guardian already ! " -- here it is again , this unique , touching , provocative compendium that is at once an account of the year itself and of the Guardian 's year . 2015 ( or more precisely autumn 2014 to autumn 2015 ) was a particularly vivid , unsettling year both for Britain and for the paper . In June , Alan Rusbridger 's 20-year tenure as editor came to an end and Katharine Viner took over . Rusbridger 's " Farewell to my readers " is , in a sense , the centrepiece of the book , unexpectedly affecting in its quietly spoken rejection of editorial imperialism and its parallel embrace of the changed post-internet relationship with readers . He identifies the current Guardian as having directly evolved out of the great Manchester original and the Scott Trust , set up by CP Scott , the first editor , of which Rusbridger will become chair in September 2016 , as its foundation and the source of its independence . " If you take a day off , the building itself would produce the Guardian . " <p> The year covered by the book has been a challenging one for the world , and in particular for those of us who share the Guardian 's sensibilities . Climate change continued apace , unchecked by wavering politicians , while deniers remained maddeningly vocal and undeterred ; the Labour vote collapsed under Ed Miliband ; the Syrian situation continued on its bloody path ; immigrants in their hundreds of thousands clawed a desperate route to the perceived promised lands of Europe , Islamist gunmen assailed Paris , slaughtering 12 cartoonists ( the subsequent attacks are not covered here ) ; and Jeremy Corbyn swept to entirely unpredicted power , taking left , right and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ everything that makes the Guardian what it is . There is a feisty foreword by Naomi Klein , which focuses on the paper 's campaigning aspect , praising its courage -- " in the face of the most urgent crises we face as a human family " -- in declaring the unacceptability of what she calls " normalised abuses ... of people , privacy and planet " . Malik Meer , the Guardian 's features editor , has made the selection , and there is n't a single one of the more than 80 pieces that does n't engage and reward the attention . If I had a cavil , it would be the scant attention paid to the arts , an area in which the Guardian has outdone most of its competitors in maintaining broad and deep coverage , treating the theatre , books , art and music as if they were a crucial part of the discourse of our times . There is a sharp comment on the long-closeted prequel to Harper Lee 's To Kill a Mockingbird , an elegant homage to the late Omar Sharif , a @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ obituary of Deirdre from Coronation Street -- and that 's it . This is doubly disappointing : first , because of the quality of so much arts writing in the paper , and , second , because the book 's account of the year is incomplete without it . <p> Meer ends the collection gloriously with a small masterpiece , " All Hail the Bearded One " , a speculative account by Chris Mullin of Corbyn 's first 100 days in office . It is both fantastical and profound , credibly outlining a series of unlikely events that result both in Corbyn 's election and the subsequent triumph of his administration . But the beauty of the piece is that it is predicated on an assumption of generosity , humanity and good humour on the part of the British electorate . It is in fact a masterly little example of that rare genre -- utopian writing -- and reminds us that the Guardian , for all it its progressiveness , technologically , politically and socially , is a newspaper deeply rooted in the utopian socialism of the end of the 19th @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ a most uncommon combination . The Bedside Guardian 2015 is also , of course , a book , celebrating within hard covers the most notoriously ephemeral branch of the profession of letters . It is a book that will , I suspect , be read for some time ahead , as testimony both of who we were in 2015 and what we believed in.
