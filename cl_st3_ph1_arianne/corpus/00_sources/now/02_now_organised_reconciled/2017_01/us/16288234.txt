<h> Cape May Hawkwatch Marks 40 Years of Tracking Wildlife Image <p> CAPE MAY -- It was n't so long ago -- less than 80 years -- when hunters commonly flocked to the southern tip of New Jersey to shoot hawks for sport . <p> That 's because autumn in Cape May is the ideal time and place to capture a sky filled with raptors ; the peninsula is an extension of the New Jersey coastal plain bordered on the west by the Delaware Bay and to the east by the Atlantic Ocean . This makes Cape May a natural funnel for migrating raptors as they head south in fall . <p> Forty years ago , when American Birds magazine dubbed Cape May the " Raptor Capital of the World , " New Jersey Audubon launched the Cape May Bird Observatory and its pioneering project , the Cape May Hawkwatch . <p> Its founder was a world-renowned raptor researcher , Bill Clark , who petitioned New Jersey Audubon about the need for a Cape May observatory . He , and the dozens of hawkwatchers to follow since , attempted to track roughly 50,000 migrating raptors between September 1 and November 30 each year . <p> The Hawkwatch steadily evolved . Dunne became the bird observatory 's director for more than 30 years , new , much-safer platforms were built , and the Hawkwatch became a focal point for visiting and resident bird watchers . <p> One thing has remained constant : Every year , birds and birders flock to Cape May for the annual count . <p> Today , after 40 years of service , the Cape May Bird Observatory is overseen by its current director , Dr. David La Puma . There are also limited-time opportunities to spot a Swainson 's Hawk , Golden Eagle , or Rough-legged Hawk soaring over . <p> " One of the benefits of a 40-year-old bird observatory is that we have several long-term data sets keeping our finger on the pulse of migration , " La Puma said .
