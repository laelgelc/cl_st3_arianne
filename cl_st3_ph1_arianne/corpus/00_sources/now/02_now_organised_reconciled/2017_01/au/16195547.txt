---- we must plan ahead for threat of coastal erosion <p> With summer heat settling in over the next few months , plenty of us will be returning to beaches in favourite holiday spots up and down the NSW coast . <p> Will we notice any changes there ? Are the paths that used to lead down through the bush and grass of the foredune a bit shorter than they used to be ? Are those half-buried sticks in fact uprights that once held steps now washed away by the ocean ? And the beach itself -- does it look a bit narrower and steeper than it used to ? Does a high tide now scoop out -- as it never used to -- a low sand cliff ? Those expensive waterfront houses surely used to be much further from the beach -- were n't they ? Or are we imagining all this ? It is only when the consequences of poor planning are suddenly revealed by a freak storm -- as happened in Collaroy and Wamberal on the central coast last year -- that the issue gains wide attention . <p> But whether it excites the public imagination or not , our coast is changing , and the pace of change is quickening . Oceans , fed by meltwater from vast glaciers in our ever-heating climate , are rising inexorably -- creating new coastal conditions . The storm-driven sea levels that did such damage last year were an exception ; as the decades pass they will become the norm . Even if the world 's governments act in concert to halt greenhouse gas emissions and eventually halt the rise in temperatures , the lost glaciers will not quickly be refrozen . Although , as the Herald has reported , the full package has not yet been published , the outlines are clear . <p> The Planning Minister , Rob Stokes , has attempted to ensure the decisions that affect communities are made as far as possible by those communities through their local council . A Coastal Council will audit the management plans of councils to ensure they are based on sound research and mapping , and likely to be effective . Even this sensible , pragmatic approach however has made some stakeholders apprehensive . <p> Some criticism is inevitable . The proposed reforms are attempting to bring order to a situation resembling the wreckage floating in the sea after last year 's storm : a mess of conflicting hopes , needs , interests and concerns . Soon , we can expect , some coastal holdings will be entirely uninsurable . We can expect , too , that the anger of those facing substantial possible losses will quickly be directed at governments . Is the council responsible for their losses ? Is the state government ? Should tax- and ratepayers have to foot their private bills ? <p> Governments have known of the likely consequences of climate change since the 1990s ( the Hawke cabinet discussed the possibility of a carbon tax in 1991 ) . Most people were probably unaware then of the looming problem rising sea levels pose , but more recently the issue has been widely publicised . Should there be a cut-off date for compensation -- so that if you bought water's-edge property before 2000 you gain indemnity , but afterwards not ? <p> It is a difficult issue precisely because of the emotion involved . Anything that alters the ground rules for housing arouses strong feelings and demands difficult political decisions .
