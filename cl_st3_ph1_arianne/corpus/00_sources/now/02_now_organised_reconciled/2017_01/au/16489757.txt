<p> After 200 years of changing the landscape of the world 's most distinctive continent we are only just beginning to understand how European settlers and their successors have vitally affected its ecology , its diversity and its climate . <p> And while those effects pervade much of our island continent , they are singularly visible in Western Australia 's South West . <p> Recording social history for RN 's Hindsight program in the 1980s , an interview with an observant old forester , Jack Thompson , offered me an early lesson . <p> Jack worked for most of his life in the jarrah and karri forests of the South West , initially as a tree feller and ultimately as a conservationist . <p> One vivid recollection was his daily journey to work from the forest camp . <p> " I remember once , we were camped on the Balingup Brook and we could n't find a place to cross this brook , " he told me . Not so much that the redoubtable Jack took it all in his stride , but because the Balingup Brook no longer floods the way it did in his day . <p> Rainfall has declined significantly in this part of Australia . Anecdotes are sometimes evocative reminders of change . <p> More recently , writing a book for the WA Department of Water , Till the Stream Runs Dry , I talked to those now responsible for measuring the considerable decline in rainfall : men like hydrographer Bernie Hawkins , whose career began in the 1960s . <p> " The winters then were massively wetter , " he said . Forests fell to make way for wheat , which had a direct effect on rainfall . The old settler saying " rain follows the axe " could easily be re-stated as " rain heads for the woods " . <p> It has recently been shown that where rain meets woodland , trees attract rainfall . As rainclouds cross a landscape and encounter higher ground and/or wooded areas they are much more likely to shed their contents . By contrast , where rain sweeps across a largely un-treed landscape , not much reaches the ground . <p> The most graphic illustration of this is aerial photography of the Rabbit Proof Fence , which runs parallel to farmland in the eastern WA Wheatbelt . But uncertainty and unpredictability also complicate our understanding of climate change . We are still learning surprising lessons . <p> For instance , frost is now more frequent on winter nights , especially in the far south of WA , and affects both farm and forest . But there is a logical explanation . Cloudless , non-rain-bearing skies are colder . <p> CO2 has the potential to encourage plant growth and perhaps increase forest cover , minimising the risk of further drying . But it does n't . CO2 is best absorbed by the lighter hued deciduous trees that dominate much of the northern hemisphere . Much of our antipodean vegetation is too dark to permit effective CO2 absorption . <p> Water issues apply equally to city dwellers and rural communities . Perth residents are the highest water-users in Australia , and declining supply now demands a big rethink to meet the demands of a still-growing city . <p> Changing silviculture practices may well assist .
