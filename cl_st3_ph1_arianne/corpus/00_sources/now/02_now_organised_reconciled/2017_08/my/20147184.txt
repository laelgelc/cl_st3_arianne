<p> IT was a lovely August morning in Salem , Oregon , with a warm sun blazing from a blue sky , when the world began to end . <p> Or , that 's what it felt like . Imperceptibly , the sky darkened , and instead of growing hotter , the air grew cool . It was as if dusk began at 9.30am . <p> Then , abruptly , in just a few minutes , a bit after 10am , night spread across Salem , where I was watching the eclipse with my family ( I 'm originally from Oregon ) . Cars were obliged to use their headlights , and I had to pull out my headlamp . <p> The throngs of eclipse-watchers on the state Capitol grounds cheered and roared with approval . <p> Eclipse-mania has shadowed Oregon for many days . Flights have been jammed full , and some cars are said to be renting for many hundreds of dollars a day . <p> Shops ran out of eclipse sunglasses , and customers began lining up before 4.30am in front of a coffee shop that gave away eclipse glasses with coffee ( later it recalled the glasses as ineffective ! ) . <p> With many hotels full , farmers rented their fields to campers . As we drove to Salem on back roads , we saw people setting up lawn chairs hours early to get prime eclipse-watching sites on farmers ' fields . <p> The " totality " of the eclipse lasted almost two minutes . Venus and Jupiter appeared in the " night " sky , and confused birds reportedly began to sing their evening songs . <p> I understood why the ancient Chinese thought that an eclipse reflected dragons eating the sun . Or , why the Arapaho Indians thought that darkness came because the sun and the moon were having sex in the sky . <p> " It was incredible ! " said Zoey Castillo , a 9-year-old who was part of a group of Girl Scouts invited to watch the eclipse from the balcony of Governor Kate Brown 's office . <p> " I 'm so glad I got to watch it one time in my life ! " <p> Miranda Trentzsch , 9 , said the Girl Scouts had been told that the next total solar eclipse in Salem would come in 2108 , and added : " If I live to be 100 , then my kids can watch the next solar eclipse with me . " <p> The greatest drama only lasted about five minutes -- the sudden darkening , the disappearance of the sun behind the moon , and then its reappearance and what seemed the breaking of a new day -- but the crowds of watchers " oohed " and " aahed " and roared their approval . After viewing my first total solar eclipse , a couple of reflections : <p> FIRST , the appeal of the solar eclipse is not just its rarity , but the way it puts us in our place . It disrupts the routines we rely on and reminds us of the vastness , beauty and rigour of the solar system . <p> One moment we are the masters of the universe . The next , the moon occludes the sun and we have to wait for light to reappear . <p> Yet , there 's also a majesty in the way scientists predict eclipses with such precision . We may not be masters of the universe , but our astronomers are masterful at taking apart the celestial clocks . <p> Scientists know to the minute when eclipses will happen many years from now . This scientific precision diminishes the sense of superstitious fear and awe that accompanied such past events . <p> In Shakespeare 's Macbeth , the murder of King Duncan seems to lead to a solar eclipse that turns the day dark and reflects the horror and evil of human misconduct ; today , the punctual arrival of an eclipse seems a tribute less to superstition than to mathematical exactitude . <p> SECOND , there was no controversy about the arrival of this eclipse . We all accepted the scientific consensus about its timing and swarmed to the best viewpoints . <p> So , why is there such resistance to the similar scientific consensus about other foretold events , such as climate change ? <p> My Times colleague , Justin Gillis , made this point in a notable article : we as a society trust scientists in their predictions about eclipses but ignore the scientific warnings about the far more dire consequences of our cooking the planet . <p> As Gillis notes , it 's not as if such cautions are new , for scientists have been discussing global warming since 1897 . <p> Nor is the problem that the climate warnings have not been verified , for global average temperatures have indeed risen almost 2 ? C since then . <p> " They told us to expect heavier rainstorms . They told us heat waves would soar . They told us that the oceans would rise . All of those things have come to pass . " <p> I chatted with Brown during the eclipse , and she dryly made the point : " In Oregon , we actually make public policy based on science and data . " <p> It would be nice if Congress did the same . Obviously , there remains a range of climate possibilities ahead , partly because feedback loops are difficult to predict and uncertainty is inevitable . <p> There 's also a legitimate debate about the best policy responses to climate change , but our national response has been little more than a shrug , and that 's difficult to reconcile with the scientific consensus about the risks ahead . <p> It 's a new day in Salem again . We now understand that a solar eclipse is n't an apocalypse , and our confidence that the world is n't ending is a reminder of our increasing understanding of the vast universe around us . <p> As the light returns and the sky warms , I 'll be celebrating , not just the majesty of the heavens , but also the wisdom of the scientists . <p> I wish I had similar confidence in the rest of us to recognise other atmospheric risks that will be far more consequential for our planet . <p> NYT <p> Facebook.com/Kristof <p> Twitter.com/NickKristof <p> The writer , a winner of two Pulitzer Prizes , is a columnist for the ' New York Times '
