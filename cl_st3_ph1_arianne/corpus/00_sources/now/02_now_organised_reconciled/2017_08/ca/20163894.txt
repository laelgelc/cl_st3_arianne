<p> Keely Weget-Whitney flashes a peace sign as she and Fin Donnelly celebrate the end of her swim down the Fraser . <p> Sunday , Aug. 6 is a day Keely Weget-Whitney will never forget . <p> That morning , Weget-Whitney entered the Fraser River at the Cayoosh Creek Campground boat launch as part of a planned 64-kilometre swim to the mouth of the Stein River north of Lytton . <p> But the 25-year-old from T'it'q'et wound up going farther than that , finally exiting the river at Lytton , after swimming the rapids between the Stein and Lytton . <p> " I was handling the swim very well , so I just kept going , " she told the News . <p> Her swim was part of the Salmon in the Canyon River Fest event . She was accompanied by two guided River Fest rafts and by Lillooet 's Kevin Aitken , who paddled beside her in a kayak . <p> She is familiar with the Fraser ; in 2011 , she journeyed down the river from its source in the Rocky Mountains to the Pacific Ocean -- a 1,400-kilometre expedition by boat , raft and foot as a member of the Sustainable Living Leadership Program founded by Fin Donnelly . <p> Donnelly was one of those who accompanied her on the rafts . <p> After her adventure in 2011 , Weget-Whitney hoped to be part of a relay swim down the river in 2014 . But plans for that were scuttled after funding fell through . <p> But the idea of swimming part of the river had been planted in her mind . <p> To train for her swim , she practised in the cold , glacier-fed waters of Seton Lake , swimming from the beach to the point . <p> The week before her Fraser River swim , she swam for 10 kilometres in the river " to get to know the river " and make sure she understood the hand signals that would be used to communicate with the guides in the rafts . <p> In contrast to Seton Lake , she said the water in the Fraser River was " really warm -- I did n't get cold once when I was swimming . It was more of a struggle getting through my swims back and forth in the lake compared to the Fraser . " <p> She said she was able to control her breathing and hold her breath so she did n't gulp down mouthfuls of polluted river water . <p> " It 's disgusting , " she said of the river water . " Part of the reason why I wanted to do the swim was to bring awareness of how the salmon are suffering right now . Do you know they 're not even allowing anybody to fish any sockeye ? That 's a worry of mine . " <p> As part of her mission to raise awareness she talks about the effects of climate change , the danger posed by fish farms , declining salmon numbers and whether the damage to wild salmon can be reversed . <p> " The salmon are the lifeline to 50 other eco-systems . When they 're gone , everything else will suffer -- the bears , eagles , our own people . It 's really sad . " <p> She said it 's an ancient First Nations custom to bless the first salmon caught in the fishing season , say a prayer and then throw the salmon back into the water . <p> " Nowadays , we 're actually throwing them back in just in hopes that our salmon is going to come back . It 's hard to say what will happen in the future . " <p> There were some touching and inspirational moments during her swim . <p> Eagles are sacred to the St'at'imc and Weget-Whitney said she felt " very blessed " along the river as eagle after eagle appeared to watch over her as she swam through the rapids during her six-hour swim . <p> " There were about 13 eagles from here to Lytton that literally blessed each rapid before I went through , " explained Weget-Whitney . " I felt they were watching over me .
