<h> In Churchill , the flies are winning <p> With grain no longer an option , the Port of Churchill 's viability will depend on attracting other cargo for shipping . <p> ( SUPPLIED PHOTO - Richardson International Limited ) <p> " Ye Gods the flies " was the first entry in the diary of Samuel Hearne when he arrived at the mouth of the Churchill River ( Fort Prince of Wales ) in 1766 . Since that time , despite the best efforts of governments and residents , the flies have been the only consistent variable . <p> Although heroic efforts on the part of numerous federal and provincial governments have been made , softening permafrost is persistently damaging and destroying the rail bed to Churchill from The Pas . The rail bed has supported year-round traffic since September 1929 , but it is now obvious it can not be expected to function without ongoing significant subsidies for repairs . <p> Over the years , Churchill served as a port for shipping grain , a site ( in 1942 ) for a major U.S. army and air force base , a rocket firing site with a range to the southeast , a small naval signals base and a location for various research initiatives . In many respects , although the total number of residents seemed high , the reality was the population was a series of insulated clusters that had little in common with each other and consequently , the social structure was segregated according to activities . <p> In addition , there were two Aboriginal communities , Inuit and Dene . They were moved there in the 1950s by Indian Affairs , largely to make the work of the department easier , while assuming the communities thus located would be better off . This turned out to be a disaster and ultimately resulted in these groups going back to locations that better serve them . <p> Climate change has not positively favoured Churchill , nor will it in the future . The rail line is already unserviceable . Although it can be fixed for the present , the reality is " fixing " will become a regular and hugely expensive routine . Much of the rail line was constructed on permafrost , which worked well until the change of climate began to soften the base . This has caused problems with the track near Churchill , but this is only the beginning of consistent failures of the track bed due to routine softening of the permafrost . <p> Churchill 's population has at times exceeded 5,000 . Today , however , it hovers around 900 . With the demise of the railroad , the economic generators are limited to tourism via air transport . The approximate tourism season of five months begins in July -- when the belugas are near the port -- and ends late November when the polar bears leave the cape to go out onto the ice to catch seals . Periodic research largely consists of small numbers of outside scientists , who may rely occasionally on local guides or assistants . <p> Before major investments of time , money and social infrastructure are made by any level of government , maybe it 's time to put rail transport on hold while a clear assessment of its long-term viability is undertaken . Simply put , what are the costs compared to the benefits ? Although the viability of a road could be considered , cost -- along with safety factors in winter -- might limit this as an option . <p> There are a few basic questions that urgently need to be considered before decisions are made . Churchill 's competition as a northern Canadian terminal consists primarily of St. John 's , N.L. , Halifax and Montreal . Are they serving Canada 's northern Arctic as effectively as Churchill might ? Is there a potential route , perhaps farther to the west , away from the muskeg , that might provide better support for a rail line ? And at what cost ? Is there sufficient need now to justify the cost ? As the Northwest Passage becomes a practical shipping lane , will this have a bearing on Churchill 's viability ? <p> Grain companies have their facilities located such that existing routes to the west and east are meeting their needs . They would have little interest in the Churchill route . This implies that goods other than grain would be the cargo through Churchill : might it be ore or oil ? If not , what are the other options ? <p> Tourists to Churchill can be served by air , as well as train , but during the busiest parts of the season , extra flights may become necessary . <p> Successive Manitoba governments have invested much time , money and effort to make and keep Churchill a viable entity , with little success . <p> Former premier Duff Roblin , shortly before he left office , said : " When I became premier , Churchill was on the agenda and 10 years later ( 1967 ) when I am about to leave , Churchill is on the agenda ... have we accomplished nothing over the whole 10 years ? " <p> Subsequently , former premier Ed Schreyer , from 1969 to 1977 , continued the effort to improve Churchill , with about the same results . <p> The flies are winning . As a community , Churchill has taken unprecedented time and money on the part of both federal and provincial governments , with little to show for it except the need for more money .
