<h> Letters and comments , Aug. 4 <h> On treaty land <p> Re : Honouring the treaties a guide for present , future ( Aug. 3 ) <p> Nice words . Now back them up . <p> Insert a Treaty page into each Saturday paper . The first inserts are devoted to the numbered treaties , starting with Treaty 1 . Then you venture into the non-numbered treaties , starting with 1701 . Then you move into the unceded territories that have no agreements . Then , you step into the quicksand that is the Indian Act and explain why Indigenous leaders are among the most vocal about keeping what must be the most debilitating legislation in Canada for Indigenous peoples . <p> Provide commentary , interpretations and debate . Explain how , in this day and age where the justice system twists every word , the intent of poorly-written treaties can somehow survive , never mind flourish . Give us the voices of Indigenous and non-Indigenous residents of each territory . <p> Show us the way forward . Show us what reconciliation looks like . Because we ca n't continue as we are . <p> -- Prayer E. Dogge <p> ? <p> This is a good start . It will be helpful if you can provide details of the treaty ( and other treaties ) so people will not be making uninformed comments . We should also be sharing information about how the treaties were not only not honoured , but that the intent from the beginning was not to honour them . We need to work on repealing the Indian Act and replacing it with something that provides protection instead of penalty . Details of this are also needed . Perhaps even more important , we need to talk about the Doctrine of Discovery that " allowed " us to claim these lands illegally in the first place . Other laws such as the royal proclamation of 1763 that look well-intentioned need to be documented for what they were really intended to do and how they were used . <p> -- Don Schau <h> Environmental health <p> Re : Save the planet through innovation , not taxation ( Aug. 2 ) <p> I agree with federal Environment and Climate Change Minister Catherine McKenna , who thinks it 's time for Premier Brian Pallister to let everyone know what his plans are to put a price on carbon . I also think Pallister has a unique opportunity to present a plan that will benefit both businesses and residents of Manitoba . <p> With the Canadian Taxpayers Federation looking to derail any carbon-tax plan , it 's time to shift the conversation . To combat climate change , we do n't have to choose between collective action defined only by government regulation and new taxes . Alternatively , we can introduce a carbon-fee-and-dividend system . <p> Carbon-fee-and-dividend is an elegant solution . A steadily rising carbon pollution fee is assessed at the wellhead , mine or port of entry . All the revenues collected would then be returned to citizens of Manitoba in the form of a monthly dividend cheque . An increasing carbon pollution fee translates into more competitive costs for low-carbon sources of energy . Dividends paid directly to citizens means that the revenues are put back into the economy , giving them the choice to purchase low-carbon alternatives . Businesses will respond to the increased prices of high-carbon energy , investing and innovating in clean technologies . <p> Yes , Pallister has some difficult choices to make , but with a carbon-fee-and-dividend system , Manitobans can truly have a " made in Manitoba " climate-change solution . <p> Greg Dyson <p> Markham , Ont . <p> ? <p> Re : Water is life ( Letters , Aug. 2 ) <p> I enjoyed reading Sybil Shaw Hamm 's letter that commends the women who paid tribute to water at the opening of the Canada Summer Games . I echo her words about our nation being blessed with an abundance of fresh water . In fact , the boreal region , which is our country 's largest ecosystem , contains one-quarter of the world 's wetlands and more surface water than any other continental-scale landscape . <p> Locally , the boreal covers over 80 per cent of Manitoba 's surface . <p> With almost 100,000 square kilometres of freshwater area , Manitoba has a huge responsibility to protect and manage our waters for now and for future generations of people and wildlife . We are fortunate that so many of our lakes and rivers remain healthy , but this is a luxury we must work to keep . <p> As many countries and jurisdictions have witnessed piecemeal developments carve out the majority of their landscapes due to short-term thinking , Manitoba 's possibilities are the envy of many nations . As our province 's boreal region is largely healthy and free of commitments for development , we still have the opportunity that most places in the world have lost -- the chance to plan for a balance of conservation and sustainable economic developments . This is going to take a lot of work and much planning , but if we get it right we will enjoy thriving economies in tandem with clean waters , fresh air and healthy wildlife populations . <p> As the Manitoba government develops its green plan and economic development strategies , I encourage them to focus largely on enduring prosperity for both the economy and the environment . As well , the province has long-overdue commitments to work with communities and enable them to design land-use plans for large resource-management areas , many of which are thousands of square kilometres in size . <p> The planning processes could be focused on long-term sustainability with a goal of balancing economic prosperity and environmental health , including the well-being of our vast network of fresh waters . <p> Ron Thiessen <p> Executive Director <p> Canadian Parks and Wilderness Society , Manitoba chapter <p> ? <p> Re : A chance to capitalize on disruptive change ( Aug. 2 ) <p> Among the numerous examples of wishful thinking contained in Nazim Cicek 's column is the claim that all the signatories of the Paris climate agreement prove that there is no more pressing global crisis than climate change . Yet the fact that one-third of these countries are either building or planning no fewer than 1,800 new coal-burning electric generating plants strongly indicates that their Paris commitments are anything but sincere . <p> Cicek also has too much faith in both the global and domestic demand for electric vehicles , particularly when recent estimates show that among the two million projected vehicle sales in Canada this year , only 4,000-5,000 will be electrics . These are almost guaranteed to be in provinces offering generous subsidies . Remove them , as was the case in Hong Kong , Denmark and Georgia , and sales take a major nose dive . <p> Finally , Cicek has too much faith in Manitobans , and probably in all Canadians , to take more positive action in making this province a leader in the development of renewable energy sources . Here , again , the likelihood is remote since polls show the majority of residents oppose carbon taxes , while few show any great inclination to make the major lifestyle changes that supposedly would reduce their carbon footprints . These would entail purchasing more electric vehicles , installing solar roof panels , demanding more public transportation , greatly reducing air travel , boycotting products from major polluters such as China , the United States and India , upgrading home insulation , etc . <p> So the reality is that combating climate change continues to be in the low priority category not only in this country but also worldwide , and until that changes , no one should expect enough widespread adoption of wind , solar and biomass energy to supplant fossil fuels . <p> You can comment on most stories on winnipegfreepress.com .
