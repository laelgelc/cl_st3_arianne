<h> The Breathing Hole review : What War Horse did for horses , this does for bears <h> The bear is , in all his charm and majesty and significance , a triumph for the author 's ambition and imagination <p> Jani Lauzon will make her Stratford debut in the 2017 season as Aga in The Breathing Hole , a new play commissioned by the Stratford Festival for Canada 150 from playwright Colleen Murphy . ( Photography by ) Lynda Churilla <p> The central character of Colleen Murphy 's new play is Angu'juaq , a polar bear whose preternatural , not to say symbolic , existence stretches from 500 years in the past to 20 years in the future . He is brought to life in Reneltta Arluk 's production in three separate incarnations . Before the play proper gets started , in the theatrical equivalent of a pre-credits sequence , he 's a shadow-puppet , created by a couple of children . Then , as a cub , he 's a hand-puppet , fondly manipulated by the Inuit woman who has saved his life and adopted him . Finally , and magically , he 's a skeletal life-size puppet , very much in War Horse style but maybe even finer , inhabited by an actor ( Bruce Hunter ) and moving with an enchanting blend of delicacy and dignity . <p> A hush comes over the house whenever he appears . Particularly impressive are his fishing expeditions . He reaches down under the stage floor into the breathing hole , the food-source , of the play 's title and comes up every time with a full-sized seal : another triumph for the design department . <p> The bear is also , in all his charm and majesty and significance , a triumph for the author 's ambition and imagination . He holds together an uneven but , in its last moments , overwhelming play . The end is prophesied in the beginning : Hummittuq , the woman who , having lost her own children , will adopt Angu'juaq , looks down through the breathing hole into the waters of the Arctic and sees them turning black . The year is 1534 , or so the program tells us ; the play has no way of doing so . The other members of Hummittuq 's community laugh at her and at her cub , but eventually come to value , even love him , as a mascot and as a hunting companion . <p> The play , a Stratford commission , is also the first at the festival to feature a plurality of indigenous characters , played by indigenous actors . The most experienced , Jani Lauzon , plays Hummittuq , and is excellent . She appears later on as , or inside , a second bear , the protagonist 's mate . The others in the play 's opening scenes are hobbled by fake-primitive dialogue that seems inappropriate and rather demeaning . Why have people talk in what used to be called pidgin-English when they would not have been speaking English at all ? <p> Things get much livelier when we flash forward to 1845 ( though again we have only the program 's word for it ) . The British arrive , in the persons of Sir John Franklin 's ill-fated expedition . They too encounter our bear . Some , frightened , want to kill it , but Franklin , an officer and a scientist , holds them back . He 's a humane fanatic , given a lovely performance , both funny and dignified , by Randy Hughson . Thomas Mitchell Barnet and Jamie Mac stand out as junior crew members , jaunty and terrified , and much given to slipping on the ice . Juan Chioran is authentically wild-eyed as the first of them to anticipate starvation ( and , it seems , the only one to dress appropriately for the climate ) . <p> The richest moments come when the explorers meet the natives : descendants , presumably , of the Intuit ( or " Esquimaux " as Franklin punctiliously calls them ) we met two centuries earlier , and played by the same actors . The non-violent culture-clash makes for wittier , more sophisticated writing than we have heard previously , and the performers rise to it . <p> And so to 2028 and then to 2033 , dates at which fashions and technologies seem surprisingly identical to those we have now . In the penultimate scene , scientists and environmentalists politely oppose oil-drillers and tourism promoters . In the last scene , the bad guys have won and everybody is on board a Northwest Passage cruise ship , casually going where Franklin &amp; Co tried and fatally failed to go . <p> The writing here descends to Ugly American clich ? , far worse than the simplicities of the opening scenes which were at least an honest effort . There is the occasional bright spot : an indigenous steward ( Johnny Issaluk ) , asked what his mother would think of his present employment , replies that actually she got him the job . Most importantly there is the last stand of Angu'juaq , still swimming in the blackened waters his foster-mother foresaw so long ago . We first met him in a culture whose inhabitants revered all creatures , even while eating them . Now he has to contend with human fear , human brutality and human pollution . <p> The production throughout is an audio-visual treat , what with Daniela Masselis ' set designs and Itai Erdai 's bright Northern lights and Carmen Braden 's soundscore . The final tableau , the previously immortal bear in an oil-drenched sea , is unforgettable . And though some of this may be put down to anthropomorphic sentimentality , it strikes deeper than that . There are plenty of plays that tell of climate change . This may be the first to find a way of showing it . <h> Almost Done ! <p> Postmedia wants to improve your reading experience as well as share the best deals and promotions from our advertisers with you .
