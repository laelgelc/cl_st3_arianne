<p> And those lobbying to clean up the endangered Lake Winnipeg say action to stop those spills must happen now . <p> " It 's very , very concerning because every time there is a combined sewer overflow that means more phosphorous and nitrogen getting into the river and up into Lake Winnipeg . The phosphorous can get deposited in the sediments of the lake and be available in future years , " said Vicki Burns , director of the Save Lake Winnipeg Project . " So , the longer we wait to correct this problem , the harder it 's going to be to fix it . " <p> In combined sewers , a single set of pipes sends sewage and rainfall to treatment plants , which can overflow during heavy rainfall and snow melt events . The sewage contains nitrogen and phosphorous . Those nutrients promote algae growth , which pollutes the lake and sometimes contains toxins that harm animals and humans . <p> The city submitted a master plan to the province to reduce combined sewer overflows in December 2015 , with options then ranging in price from $830 million to $4.1 billion . The province , which regulates Winnipeg water , has n't yet mandated which final option the city must pursue . <p> Burns believes the estimated $4.1-billion tab to completely separate Winnipeg 's combined sewers and stop all overflows would be worth the investment . <p> " In many situations in life , you know that if you do n't deal with the problem when it starts , it 's just going to get bigger and bigger , " said Burns . <p> But not everyone is convinced that completely separating all combined sewers is worth its $4-billion price tag . <p> " I do n't think it 's good bang for the buck ... Nobody likes the idea of sewage going into the Red or the Assiniboine or the Seine ( rivers ) . But at the end of the day , we are taking some steps to reduce that with the billion-dollar option , " said Coun. Brian Mayes , chair of Winnipeg city council 's water and waste committee . <p> The city has requested provincial approval of an $830 million to $1.2 billion option instead , which would capture 85% of sewage overflows in wet weather events during an average weather year . That option would allow some " floatable " waste to get through and an average of 15 overflows per year . <p> Mayes said he 'd like to see the city pursue that option and add more green alleys , which use permeable paving material to divert storm water from sewers , to help prevent overflows . <p> Winnipeg 's water and waste department notes the city has invested $300 million to reduce overflows and provide basement flood relief so far . <p> " ( We 're ) proceeding with work that we think needs to be done . We still are going ahead with ( combined sewer ) separation work , " said Duane Griffin , branch leader of wastewater planning . <p> But Griffin notes that sewer upgrades ca n't yet be credited for annual fluctuations in the volume of sewage CSOs send to Winnipeg rivers . The drop from 12.5 billion litres in 2015 to 9.2 billion litres in 2016 , for example , is more accurately credited to Mother Nature , he said . <p> " The results will fluctuate based on where the rain fell , how hard it rained and what the river elevations are , " he said . <p> The mix of human waste and rainwater in each overflow can vary . In one 2015 storm , it consisted of 96% rain water and 4% wastewater , the city says . <p> Manitoba 's Sustainable Development Minister Cathy Cox could n't be reached for comment Wednesday or Thursday . In an email , spokeswoman Olivia Billson wrote that the city 's CSO reduction master plan is still under evaluation , though the province is concerned the problem will worsen . <p> " With climate change a reality , we are concerned that combined sewer overflows will continue to increase over time .
