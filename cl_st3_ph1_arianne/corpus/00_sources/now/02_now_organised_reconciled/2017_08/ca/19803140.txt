<p> British Columbians should not be lamenting Petronas ' decision to pull its Pacific Northwest Liquified Natural Gas proposal . Instead , they should be celebrating the demise of a project built on bad economics , climate change denial and wishful thinking . <p> A few pundits have told the Petronas story as a tragedy . Some are blaming the new NDP government , others their B.C. Liberal predecessors for not moving faster to land a deal . <p> The real culprit is the abysmal economics of LNG -- the need for expensive new pipelines and terminals and the high costs to liquefy gas and transport it across the Pacific . These investments would only make sense if prices were way higher than they currently are . <p> Optimistic predictions for LNG were made based on abnormally high prices from 2011 to 2014 . It is very unlikely that gas prices will return to those highs as global LNG export capacity is poised to grow by a third by 2020 . <p> So what if B.C. had managed to stake out some LNG turf ? Let 's turn to Australia , where success turned into a political crisis earlier this year . <p> Just as the taps were turned on for three massive new export projects on the country 's east coast near Gladstone , gas prices shot up for Aussie households and businesses in major urban areas like Sydney . Local prices for gas at one point cost more than Australia 's exported gas in Japan . <p> In April , Australian Prime Minister Malcolm Turnbull announced the government would begin imposing restrictions on LNG exporters . <p> Moreover , major new conflicts have emerged in Australia between the gas industry , now aiming to supply huge export volumes , and farmers whose land would have to be fracked to access the gas . <p> As the Australian government tilted its tax and royalty regime toward encouraging new LNG investment , the return to the Treasury -- for the development of the publicly owned gas resource -- has been meager . A recent report from Australia 's McKell Institute found " the industry will need to record at least $238 billion in profits before a cent in royalties is paid to the Australian people . " <p> The Australian government ordered a comprehensive review of the tax and royalty regime in late 2016 , however , there are doubts for meaningful changes . <p> This is what " competitiveness " looks like . Ultimately , such deals shortchange the public and represent a transfer of risk from the private to the public sector . <p> What about jobs ? The Australian experience shows the vast majority of jobs occur in the construction stage and completed facilities employ few permanent workers . <p> Gladstone , Queensland has seen boom and bust due to a temporary surge of workers to build three LNG plants in the area . In this city of 60,000 people , there were 14,500 construction jobs for a few years and now only 500 permanent jobs in the completed LNG terminals . During the boom , housing became unaffordable for locals , and seniors and professionals were forced to move away . <p> It is understandable that economically challenged regions of BC would want high-paying LNG jobs , but the reality is there are few to go around . <p> Finally , it 's worth recalling that the world is trying to get its climate change act together by transitioning away from fossil fuels . Thanks to new LNG capacity , Australian greenhouse gas emissions are on the rise and the country will miss its 2020 emissions target by a large margin . <p> The B.C. government spent millions in public funds and built up the hopes of workers and rural communities on an LNG dream . It wasted political capital , time and money on LNG instead of developing renewables and investing in energy efficiency and climate-friendly infrastructure . <p> Natural gas is a finite resource . British Columbians should be thankful that government efforts to quickly liquidate it for export have come to naught . Forget the laments : Petronas ' decision to pull out of B.C. is a blessing . <p> Marc Lee is a senior economist at the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives , B.C. office . <h> Comments <p> We encourage all readers to share their views on our articles and blog posts .
