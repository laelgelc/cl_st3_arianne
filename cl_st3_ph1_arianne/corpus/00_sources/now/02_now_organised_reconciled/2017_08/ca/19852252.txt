<h> News Local <h> Mourning Nature an elegy for a lost world <p> It 's been nearly 30 years since Bill McKibben announced that nature was dead . Global climate change , he told us in his book The End of Nature , was altering everything we had come to identify as natural phenomena . The rain that used to nourish our gardens contained acid ; sunsets were red because of pollution suspended in the air . It was n't only nature itself that was dead , he wrote , but also our perceptions of it , our trust in it , and our sense of place within it . <p> " More and more frequently , " he wrote , " these changes will clash with our perceptions , until our sense of nature as eternal and separate is finally washed away , and we see all too clearly what we have done . " <p> That time has come . Over the past three decades , humanity has passed from a state of alarm about nature 's imminent demise to a state of mourning over its death . McKibben asked us to think about what it feels like to live on a planet where nature is no longer nature . What is the sadness about ? " <p> That , essentially , is the question that Karen Landman and Ashlee Cunsolo set out to explore in their book Mourning Nature : Hope at the Heart of Ecological Loss and Grief , published recently by McGill-Queen 's University Press . A collection of 11 essays by workers on the front lines , where environmental degradation meets and impacts the people who are faced with it daily , Mourning Nature is both an elegy for a lost world and a call to action , to get past our shock and grief at the death of nature and get on with the work of mourning and survival . <p> " Essentially , " says co-editor Karen Landman , whose PhD in geography from Queen 's was about how we perceive nature and how that varies depending on who we are , " the book is about mourning ecological loss in the Anthropocene , " by which she means this latest period in Earth 's history that has been influenced by humans . And most of that influence has been felt in the past 200 years , since the Industrial Revolution . <p> " This loss , " she says , " can range from large-scale landscape change , such as in the Arctic , where the ice melt is unprecedented in our lifetime , to the loss of what seemed like an everyday experience of sparrows in London , England . The book seeks to bring our sadness , anger and grief to the fore in academic discussion . " <p> Grief , the editors argue , can have a devastating effect when it is unacknowledged . Ashlee Cunsolo , director of the Labrador Institute at Memorial University in St. John 's , recalls interviewing an Inuit woman in Nunatsiavut , N.L. , about changes she had seen in her environment in her lifetime . The woman spoke calmly for hours about rapidly warming temperatures , decreasing sea ice , changing weather patterns , and the alteration to plant and animal life . <p> " As we were speaking , " writes Cunsolo , " I asked her how she felt about all the changes . She paused " ? she looked at me " ? and she began to cry . " <p> Just as Kingston writer Diane Schoemperlen , in her 2004 book Names of the Dead , felt it imperative to name the people who died on Sept. 11 , 2001 , in the World Trade Center disaster , or the way we erect memorials bearing the names of those who have fallen in battle , the writers collected in this volume speak the names of the many species of plants , animals and birds that have disappeared and are disappearing before our eyes . <p> John Charles Ryan , for example , writes about the scented or brown Boronia , " a slender shrub endemic to the southwest corner of Western Australia , " that citizens of Perth celebrated as " the very breath of spring . " Because of habitat loss and overharvesting , as with so many other wildflowers in Western Australia , Beronia , writes Ryan , " no longer signifies the rising ' breath of spring . ' " <p> Similarly , Helen Whale and Frankin Ginn mourn the disappearance of House sparrows in London , England . " Extinction , " they write , " haunts our time as never before . " <p> Artist Jessica Marion Barr , who received her PhD at Queen 's in 2015 , traces the elegiac voice in art , in her essay " Auguries of Elegy : The Art and Ethics of Ecological Grieving . " She points to the work of such apocalyptic artists as Edward Burtyinsky , Christian Boltanski and Gwen Currie ( reproductions of their work are included in the text ) and to Chris Jordan 's film Midway , which depicts the plight of the Laysan albatross as it is pushed to extinction from ingesting plastic trash from the Pacific Ocean . Such artists , Barr writes , " explore an ethics of ecologic , melancholic grieving as a pedagogical and activist strategy . " <p> In other words , the writers and artists collected here do n't just sit and lament the many losses around them ; they use the energy generated by their grief to find ways to work through it , often by ecological activism , with the goal of waking us up to reality , no matter how strong our urge to resist . " I believe , " writes Ashlee Cunsolo , " that it is in our stories , in our writing , in our philosophies , in our sharings , and in our songs , protests , art and other expressions that we take up this challenge , and confront these slow-moving environmental traumas . " <p> Mourning Nature , says Karen Landman , " is about acknowledging and articulating loss and the grief associated with loss . What have we lost ? How is it affecting us ? Our culture , " she adds , " is uneasy discussing death , grief and loss . This book is meant to bring out a conversation on this subject . " <p> " Successful mourning , " write the editors in their Introduction , " is not about internalizing or replacing the other ; rather , mourning is about recognizing that all we have to give to mourning , to the dead , to what we have lost , is in our own living and our own acts of mourning and remembering . " <p> Mourning is not passive , it is active . As Freud noted , mourning is work . <p> " No longer debilitated , " writes Cunsolo after her interview with the woman in Labrador , " but inspired by the mourning process , I could keep moving forward . " <p> Kingston writer Wayne Grady has also written about death and extinction in such works as Bringing Back the Dodo and The Bone Museum . <p> Mourning Nature : Hope at the Heart of Ecological Loss and Grief .
