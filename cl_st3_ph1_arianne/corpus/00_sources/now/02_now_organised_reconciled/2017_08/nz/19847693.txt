<p> Anthony Maturin harnessed his outrage at the plight of the poor and maligned to bring their stories to the world through the lens of his camera . <p> His work for social justice and the alleviation of suffering is documented in several photographic books revealing the hardship of people , from civil war-ravaged Guatemala to the depths of the South African slums . <p> Tony Maturin <p> Maturin 's image of a woman cooking in an Eastern South African slum . <p> To get truthful documentary photographs , Maturin once said it was crucial not just to visit but to live with the subjects and really get to know them . <p> Over his long life , and right into his 70s , he spent months at a time in different countries documenting the struggles of disaffected citizens . <p> supplied <p> Maturin 's photo of spirit healers training in South Africa . <p> The hardship and difficult living conditions never bothered him . <p> He said in one interview that he felt physically uncomfortable living in luxury . " I 'm very comfortable living in what other people would call very dirty conditions . I do n't like comfort zones ; they 're not good news . " <p> Maturin 's eventful and international approach to life started early . He was born in Manila , Philippines , as his parents were " passing through " on their way to Hong Kong , where his father had been offered a job in the sugar trade . <p> A disrupted childhood , including the divorce of his parents , meant that Anthony and younger sister Gill spent their early years with relatives at Ormesby Hall , in the north of England -- a household that included a governess , nanny and many servants . <p> Later the children moved to London to live with their mother . It was while walking in London one day that Maturin saw a beggar on the street and asked his mother why they had n't stopped to give him some money . <p> The occasion alerted him to the fact that life was not all servants and comfortable living . <p> In 1939 the family moved to New Zealand . Living with relatives in Karori , Wellington , where he attended Karori Normal School , he found it difficult to fit into wartime colonial life . A spate of shoplifting and general poor behaviour led to him being sent as a boarder to Christ 's College . <p> A poor scholar , Maturin enjoyed only the choir and woodwork class . With no idea what to do with his life , except an overwhelming desire to leave school , he was offered a job on a Southland hill country station . <p> Things improved immediately and he took to the shepherd 's life in the mountains , with his dogs , horse and a rifle . His mother , however , became increasingly concerned that he was consorting with " a rough lot " and asked the station owners to keep him away from " bad language and drink " . <p> Her words must have had an effect , as he was always softly spoken and made a commitment later in life not to drink alcohol again " until every child has clean drinking water " . <p> It was when he was helping out at Inverary Station , in the Mid Canterbury foothills , that he met his future wife , Anne Wright . They married in 1956 , and had two daughters . <p> Around this time he developed a love of classical music and attended Bible and philosophy classes to deepen his spiritual life . <p> This spiritual awakening led him into charity work which started with a three-month stint in Vanuatu , building a medical centre on Ambrym Island on behalf of the Anglican Church 's Lepers Trust Board . <p> It also led to the breakdown of his marriage and Maturin soon found himself living on a boat in Akaroa harbour , where he made an erratic living selling fish , and violins he had made himself . <p> Life on a boat suited him so much he decided to sail to the Solomon Islands to do further development work . But acute seasickness put paid to this venture and he quickly decided his future lay elsewhere . He enrolled at St John 's Theological College in Auckland , planning to become a parish priest but the theology proved too much and Maturin instead leaned towards the Quaker way . <p> After a return to Christchurch and a second marriage , he joined Amnesty International . <p> But the carefully worded Amnesty letters to various governments did not suit his activist approach and in the late 1980s he set off independently to Guatemala to document human rights abuses in some of the highland villages during their civil war . <p> Armed only with a small recorder , microphone , spare shirt and underwear , he videoed and recorded interviews with leaders of the resistance movement , including political activist Rigoberta Menchu . <p> He had several narrow escapes while interviewing , but was able to smuggle enough footage through Mexico to produce two documentaries for Amnesty and his book , Pilgrimage to Chajul . <p> Through the 1990s Maturin felt increasingly concerned about the situation in the Middle East . United Nations sanctions had been imposed on Iraq after the invasion of Kuwait , and in 2002 he joined a group travelling there to document the longer-term effects of these sanctions on the ordinary people . <p> What he saw when he arrived shocked him , in particular the inability to import vital medicines for hospital care . He interviewed nurses and market stall holders , taxi drivers and street kids of the once prosperous and thriving country . <p> He returned to New Zealand with cholera , much to the alarm of the public health doctors , most of whom had never seen the disease before . On his recovery he organised a lobbying group against the sanctions , and held an art exhibition to support local NGOs in Iraq . <p> In 2004 , he and his partner Sandra , whom he had met at a Quaker gathering in 1996 , took up a VSA assignment in Cambodia . <p> The photographs for the resulting book , A Certain Grace , formed the basis of a later exhibition at the Dowse Museum in Lower Hutt . The book could be called Tragedy and Triumph , Maturin said in a 2006 interview , " but I did not set out to report on tragedies . Rather , I hope the pages reveal what I found wherever I went , the triumph of the human spirit in those circumstances . " <p> He also produced Two Barang to Cambodia , an account of his and Sandra'sr time in South East Asia . <p> At the age of 78 , he and Sandra took on another VSA assignment , this time to the Eastern Cape Province of South Africa . <p> During their time there he enlisted the help of poets and singers , giving them an opportunity to have their work published ( and paid for ) , even writing some poetry himself under the pseudonym " Tyebe ' ' -- the bearded one . <p> The ensuing book , Grace 's Song , pays homage to all those on the ground , working for social justice and the alleviation of suffering in the Eastern Cape . <p> His final campaign , to raise awareness and " do something " about climate change , was , due to ill health , pursued primarily sitting at his computer and through social media . <p> He continued to produce a regular climate change newsletter for Quakers and other faith groups , contributed financially to organisations and individuals working on this issue , and lobbied politicians . <p> While physically able , he attended meetings to support the efforts of younger generations of activists , encouraging them with the mantra -- " You 've just got to keep on going ... "
