<p> Wairarapa 's dry summer climate will become even drier . A plan to build large water reservoirs in the northwestern hills , capture water when it is plentiful and transport it down to farms and towns when its needed most makes sense . But economic and environmental hurdles may mean this $200 million project never gets off the ground . Piers Fuller reports . <p> More than 50 years ago work on one of New Zealand 's most ambitious water catchment schemes began - and took three decades to complete . <p> The Lower Wairarapa Valley Development Scheme transformed large swathes of the region from Greytown south , and was seen as a successful project in increasing agricultural productivity , and providing extensive flood protection . <p> Backers of a proposed large scale water storage , distribution and irrigation project in the upper valley say it will have an even greater impact on the output and resilience of the region . <p> Water Wairarapa , the body charged with progressing the new scheme , says it has the power to lift the economy of the whole region and create environmental and recreational benefits . <p> Funded equally by Greater Wellington Regional Council ratepayers and taxpayers , via Crown Irrigation Investments , $7.4 million has so far been spent investigating the project . <p> Water Wairarapa project director Michael Bassett-Foss said a more reliable source of water results in long-term economic , social gains and improved resilience . <p> " Water storage and a catchment-wide approach to water use and management also provides opportunities to augment summer stream flows , enhance water quality , supplement urban water supplies and create new recreational uses . " <p> A study three years ago concluded that irrigating an additional 30,000 hectares in the Wairarapa valley would add $157 million of GDP to the greater Wellington region per year and create 1200 new jobs . <p> ENVIRONMENTAL AND ECONOMIC WORRIES <p> The proposal is not without its knockers . They say it does n't make economic or environmental sense . <p> Wellington Fish &amp; Game manager Phil Teal said the main objection stemmed from the negative environmental effect it would have on waterways . <p> " We 're strongly convinced that the nutrient loading of nitrogen , for example , are fully allocated in the Ruamahanga Catchment . That 's if you want to maintain a healthy ecosystem . We see a dam with large-scale irrigation would really see a risk of land intensification , such as dairy and cropping . " <p> Fish &amp; Game commissioned economist Peter Fraser to investigate the scheme 's financial viability . He argued the whole plan does not stack up and should be canned before more money is spent on it . <p> He said despite what Water Wairarapa has said about most of the growth being in arable farming , the bulk of Stage 1 is predicated on dairy farming being an " anchor tenant " and at current milk solid prices farmers could n't afford the water at its projected cost of between 24-28 cents per cubic metre . <p> " How much more ratepayer money is Water Wairarapa going to actually spend on something that quite clearly does n't work ? They 're trying to get something that is investment ready , that never will be investment ready because it 's a dud . " <p> Greater Wellington chairman Chris Laidlaw said there had been too much focus on the water price - something that has not yet been accurately established - and added the project is just about to have another assessment on farmer demand . <p> He said the scheme will not go ahead unless it stacks up economically and environmentally and pointed out that increasing dairy production was no longer a focus . <p> Dam Free Wairarapa was established to fight the possibility of a reservoir being installed in the upper Mangatarere valley in Carterton . Though that site is now off the table , members still oppose the scheme on the principle it will damage the environment and displace farms . <p> Chairman David Field said they are concerned about the whole Ruamahanga catchment and the effects intensification could have on water quality . <p> PREPARING FOR DRIER FUTURE <p> A recent Niwa report , commissioned by the regional council , shows Wairarapa is expected to get drier as a result of climate change , and certain types of farming will be hit hard , including pastoral farming . <p> Water Wairarapa said reports like these point to a changing environment , which will need more water . <p> They commissioned a report by agricultural consulting firm BakerAg on Future Landuse Scenarios . <p> Though the report predicted further intensification of land use including dairy in the initial period until 2025 , based on current trends the long -erm predictions in the period from 2025 to 2040 indicated a shift away from water on pasture to increased water on forage , arable , vegetable and fruit crops . <p> " Economically , water will , over time follow the path of highest sustainable returns , which is expected to be in seed , fruit and vegetable production where soil class and climatic conditions permit . " <p> In the long term , 25-65 years out , the report expected that a changing world environment , both in weather and food logistics , will see the Ruamahanga valley increasingly exporting edible crop , vegetable and fruit to a Pacific-based market . <p> " With vision , irrigation will bring a vibrant business and social growth to the Wairarapa community . It will be about a value-add product leaving the Ruamahanga valley and wider districts . " <p> FARMERS WANT MORE WATER <p> South Wairarapa crop and specialist seed farmer Richard Kershaw grows over a dozen variety of plants on his Martinborough farm . Most of it is for seed and vegetables grown under contract for overseas markets . <p> He said it was all dependent on the availability of reliable water . <p> " Without irrigation it is very difficult to grow any of this stuff , " Kershaw said . <p> At a very basic level , irrigation allows crop growers to significantly extend the growing season , increasing the number of crop rotations . <p> Kershaw has been a vocal backer of the water storage and distribution scheme since it was mooted and believes it will be the stimulus for significant economic growth . <p> He also said that without irrigation he would not get a contract to grow because he would be considered a risk .
