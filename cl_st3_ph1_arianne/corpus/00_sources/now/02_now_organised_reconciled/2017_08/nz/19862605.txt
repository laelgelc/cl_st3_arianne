<h> Suicide and silence : Deciding who can say what <p> Annette Beautrais has a fractious relationship with the media , whose suicide reporting she seeks to influence . <p> There are tides in the public conversation about suicide in New Zealand , and right now the water 's right up to the seawall . <p> Nigel Latta devoted a full episode to the subject in his primetime documentary series The Hard Stuff last year . In March , the glossy TV series 13 Reasons Why slipped into the country via Netflix , then earned a tailor-made , if toothless , " RP18 " rating due to concerns that it glamourised suicide and explicitly depicted suicide methods . <p> In recent weeks the government 's chief science adviser has released a discussion paper on youth suicide ; a Taranaki headmaster 's speech to his students about suicide has been widely shared on social media ; and Auckland 's Herald newspaper has published a series on youth suicide that includes photos of victims and detailed accounts of their family 's grieving . Unicef reported New Zealand had the highest youth suicide rate -- that 's teenagers between 15 and 19 -- in the developed world . <p> Nigel Latta is a staunch defender of suicide expert Annette Beautrais and her message that media must tread very carefully . <p> But one of the more curious stories on the issue came in mid-July , when comedian-turned mental health campaigner Mike King said schools had cancelled his speaking engagements after being advised by the South Canterbury DHB , and its suicide prevention coordinator Professor Annette Beautrais , that it could be unwise for him to be talking to students . <p> Mike King believes experts have lost touch with the public on the issue of suicide . <p> King claimed that Beautrais 's advice to the school showed she was promoting a " silent " approach to tackling suicide that was outdated and -- judging by New Zealand 's stubbornly high suicide rate of over 500 per year -- apparently ineffective . Beautrais declined media requests to respond . <p> This story was familiar in several ways : the presence of Beautrais as suicide expert , the criticism of her methods by someone who 's trying to tackle suicide from a non-academic perspective , and her reluctance to respond publicly . In this field her voice carries real weight , but sometimes she 'd rather not talk at all . <p> Saying " no " to a reporter is hardly unusual , but in the case of Beautrais , it 's complicated . For decades , she has been regarded as New Zealand 's leading suicidologist , an academic who 's been studying suicide and suicide prevention since 1991 . She was director of the Canterbury Suicide Project until 2009 , at which point she left the country , reportedly in despair at the difficulties of securing suicide research funding . <p> She 's since advised the World Health Organisation and the International Association for Suicide Prevention . On returning to New Zealand she has taken up the role at SCDHB , run training programmes around the country and authored a report on rural suicide . She is also an adjunct professor at the University of Canterbury . <p> The significance of her relationship with media is that certain types of reporting of suicide have been shown by researchers to trigger further copycat suicides when seen by vulnerable people -- a so-called " contagion " effect . Based on that research , Beautrais has long been a strong voice , both in policy advice and in public statements , against irresponsible media coverage of suicide . <p> But somehow , a concern about contagion appears to have morphed into a more general distaste for and distrust of media . She declined to talk to Stuff for this story , and to reporter Matt Philp in 2007 for a similar feature . In 2006 , she expressed her frustrations to a Herald reporter : " I know the way journalists write ... I object to the fact that you can find anyone in the community who has an opinion on suicide , and then you ring me up and give my opinion equal weight . " <p> Last month , in an Otago Daily Times article about a number of suicides in south Otago , she explained to the reporter that drawing conclusions about rare events is statistically problematic , but also threw in some generalised scolding : " I 've never seen newspaper articles take the time or caution to say to people ' these are statistically rare events data , and you ca n't read anything into them ' " -- a claim that paradoxically ceased to be true the moment the newspaperprinted it . <p> Friction with media might seem trivial , but this matters . Because right now those who say media must shut up about suicide seem to be losing the argument . High-profile public figures -- not just Mike King and other community campaigners , but former coroners -- have lost faith with a " don't-talk-about-it " approach . New Zealand has some of the strictest rules in the world about media reporting of suicide , but they they were slightly relaxed last year ( press can now identify a suspected suicide without waiting for a formal coronial finding ) and are often broken without penalty . <p> If one of Beautrais 's goals has been to keep media on the straight and narrow , it seems she 's losing the fight . Which makes you wonder , has she been going about it the wrong way ? <p> NO INCREASE IN SUICIDE <p> Neil MacLean first encountered Beautrais when he was Christchurch coroner , and she was running the Canterbury Suicide Project . He has great respect for her research and reputation in the field , but came to disagree about just how silent media should be . When media guidelines were softened by the Ministry of Health , " I remember that she and others were very critical that the ministry and others had been conned by the media into doing something very unwise . " <p> Maclean was chief coroner from 2007 to 2015 , and in 2010 began releasing annual suicide statistics . He was criticised for doing so , but " I watched out with special interest to see what happened . And the answer was nothing . The suicide statistics carried on exactly as they had before . " <p> Maclean is n't challenging the accuracy of the academic research but he does n't believe it 's always relevant . He says academics have relied on " historical stuff " such as a huge spike in a particular method of suicide in Hong Kong after a highly publicised death in 1998 . <p> " But translating it to New Zealand , I do n't think I 've ever seen anything that would suggest that anything that 's been published has actually led to an increase of suicides . <p> " There 's not doubt there is a contagion effect , " says Maclean , " particularly amongst young people if someone commits suicide and somebody else close to them knows about it . There seems to be something going on that leads to imitation . But that 's nothing to do with what 's coming out publicly . " <p> The way he sees it , Beautrais 's perfectly legitimate concern about contagion and copycat suicides " became more broadly a view that there should be no discussion , that even releasing up-to-date suicide statistics is dangerous " . <p> And these days , says Maclean , the internet and social media make attempts to control information more futile than ever : " I think the bigger world has passed her by . " <p> Professor Sunny Collings , from Otago University 's suicide and mental health research group , said that there was a time when Beautrais was New Zealand 's only suicide researcher of any substance , but she has been joined by other experts as the field has evolved over the years . <p> She said most scientists in the field are in broad agreement about the evidence around the risks of irresponsible media coverage , but " where you 're more likely to find variation is in the application of the evidence in individual cases " . <p> Collings said it 's regrettable when views become polarised on an issue such as suicide reporting , as " that becomes the story rather than the actual issue , which is that we 'd all all feel better about New Zealand if we could work towards fewer people dying through suicide . " <p> Last week the government 's chief science adviser Peter Gluckman released a discussion paper on youth suicide that talked briefly about the risks of media reporting . Gluckman told Stuff that although Beautrais had n't been directly consulted for the paper , its conclusions align closely with hers , simply because both it and Beautrais rely on the same scientific evidence . <p> He says the most definitive summary is in a 2015 report from Public Health England , which says the risks around media coverage of a suicide increase when it is frequent , when it is on the front page , when the word " suicide " is in the headline , when it states or implies the method , and when it glorifies the suicide or the grieving family and friends . <p> All the same , says Gluckman , " it 's possible to have quite serious discussions of suicide without talking about those things " . <p> He has been concerned by shows such as 13 Reasons Why , and by some recent media stories containing details about specific suicides . Yet he is happy that the community is having a conversation about suicide and depression in a way it never has before . <p> A DEFENDER <p> One enthusiastic defender of Beautrais 's approach is Nigel Latta . He is in the curious position of being someone in media who fronted an entire documentary episode about suicide , complete with interviews with grieving relatives and mournful recitations of worrying statistics , that did n't get any criticism from Beautrais . <p> In fact , says Latta , not only did he interview Beautrais on-camera as New Zealand 's acknowledged suicide-prevention expert , he also gave her full editorial control over the final edit of the entire episode -- a very unusual concession . He did that to ensure the documentary was n't going to risk the very lives that it was trying to help save . <p> Latta says Beautrais has been " burnt " when trying to talk to media before , which has made her cautious , but the criticisms of her are often unfair . <p> " She gets attacked as if it 's her opinion . People describe it like it 's some sort of cult -- the Beautraisians or whatever -- but this is not ' Annette Beautrais makes up a bunch of s ... and just says it ' . She 's part of international panels , and has published , and reads the actual journals . It 's all very well for someone who does n't read those journals , or who does n't go to those conferences , to say ' I disagree ' , but I 'm putting my money on her . " <p> Like Beautrais , Latta gets frustrated when media attempts at " balance " lead to a false equivalence between scientific fact and ill-informed rebuttal . It 's a bit like the bad old days of climate change coverage , where a statement from the IPCC about global temperatures might be " balanced " with some distorted nonsense from climate global warming denier Lord Christopher Monckton . <p> Suicide 's an emotive subject , says Latta , " so you have a lot of people who speak passionately and eloquently , and that becomes ' There are two sides to this ' . But there are n't . Research is n't perfect , but what we have to do is take the most conservative path . " <p> What about the accusation that Beautrais and like-minded academics are out of touch with the public , and with the families who just want to talk about their suffering , without being told to shut up ? <p> Nothing could be further from the truth , says Latta . During the time he spent with her making the documentary , Beautrais was doing " crazy " hours , running around doing suicide-prevention workshops , boots on the ground stuff . <p> " It pains me that she 's painted as this dispassionate academic when she is the opposite : she 's a deeply compassionate person . " <p> King says Beautrais wants to stifle debate about suicide . From where he sits , it looks like she thinks " that if you say the S-word people will pick up on it and they will try to kill themselves " . <p> MacLean says calls for media silence about suicide remind him of the mystery and confusion of HIV/Aids in the 1980s , where there was " suppression and burying of heads in the sand " -- a situation that improved only when people challenged the silence and started talking about practical responses to the epidemic . <p> But Latta insists Beautrais 's goal is n't silence at all . Yes , she wants to block those aspects of suicide reporting that are dangerous . But his and Beautrais 's central message in the doco was that certain conversations about suicide should be actively encouraged . <p> In her interview with Latta , Beautrais taught viewers the signs that someone might be suicidal , and suggested they directly broach the subject with that person , even providing a rough script to follow : " One of the most simple and effective methods is to say , ' I 've noticed this , this and this about you , and people like this might be thinking about suicide . Are you ? ' " <p> Talking about suicide like this , it turns out , is not just safe -- it 's actively helpful . <p> After Latta handed editorial control to Beautrais , she vetoed specific shots , even specific choices of music . Some bits that would have made " great telly " did n't get used . A decision was taken not to show images of suicide victims . Yet a lot of sensitive material did pass muster , including a tearful interview with a woman who 'd attempted suicide as a child . <p> There seems a great distance between Latta 's version of Beautrais as the cautious expert who recognises the power of media to drive a fruitful public conversation about how to talk about suicide , and Beautrais as perceived by King , or MacLean , and some reporters : a prickly and incommunicative expert who wants to shut down debate and grows haughty when challenged . Could she perhaps do with some pointers on how to get the media onside and use them more effectively , rather than treating them with suspicion ? Maybe Latta , who 's demonstrated a powerful ability to use primetime TV to communicate with the public on difficult , complicated issues , has some tips for her on how to get media onside and use them as an ally rather than antagonist ? <p> Latta says Beautrais told him she was of the view that media can play a positive role in this issue , " but she just has a low trust , based on actual experience , that that will happen " . <p> And yes , says Latta , " It is important to communicate science in a way that people can understand and make sense of . But she 's someone who 's been personally attacked for a long time , and she 's only human ... <p> " If she cared less , she 'd just go and do something easier . She 's doing this because she thinks it 's important that we follow the evidence and do the stuff that 's safe . "
