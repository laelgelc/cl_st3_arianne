<h> Science <h> Why climate change could kill huge numbers of people in Asia <p> Extreme heatwaves that can kill people within hours will become commonplace across vast areas of India , Pakistan and Bangladesh by the end of the century unless dramatic action is taken to cut carbon emissions , scientists have warned . <p> But why are so many people facing these apocalyptic-sounding heatwaves and why they are so dangerous ? <h> How did we get here ? <p> Since the dawn of the Industrial Revolution the world has pumped ever-increasing amounts of carbon dioxide , methane and other greenhouse gases into the atmosphere . <p> Once there , they can stay for hundreds of years trapping vast amounts of heat , which has pushed up the temperature across the world . <p> Although estimates vary , scientists generally accept that the average global temperature has increased by at least 1C in the past two centuries -- with some regions , including parts of Asia , seeing much bigger increases . <h> Why is the situation particularly bad in India , Pakistan and Bangladesh ? <p> It comes down to the " wet-bulb " temperature , which is the air temperature taken when a wet cloth is wrapped around the thermometer . <p> This is always lower than the dry-bulb temperature -- but how much lower depends on the humidity , which determines how easy it is for water to evaporate . The more humid it is , the more moisture there is in the atmosphere and the harder it is for water to evaporate . <h> Why does this matter ? <p> Because the higher the temperature the more you need to cool down , which you do by sweating . But the more humid the air the harder it is to sweat . <p> Sweat helps cool you down two ways . First , it makes your skin feel cooler when it 's wet . And when it evaporates it removes some heat from the body . <p> Scientists say humans can survive up to a wet-bulb temperature of about 35C ( 95 degrees Fahrenheit ) , beyond which the human body has difficulty sweating to cool down , or sweat does n't evaporate . <p> This can lead to heat stroke and ultimately death within just a few hours -- even in shaded , ventilated conditions . <h> Who is most effected ? <p> Most of those at risk in India , Pakistan and Bangladesh are poor farmworkers or outdoor construction laborers . They are unlikely to have air conditioners -- up to 25 percent in of India 's population still has no access to electricity . In some areas that have been deforested for industry or agriculture , they may not even have very much shade . <p> " This brings us to the threshold of survivability and anything in the 30s is very severe , " said study author Dr Elfatih Eltahir , from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the US .
