( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> The first truly global disaster resulting from climate change may come from rising sea levels . <p> The United Nations Intergovernmental Committee on Climate Change has projected sea level rise of 1 to 3 feet by the end of the century , and more recent estimates by NASA and other scientists have projected a rise of up to 8 feet . <p> In Massachusetts , the rising sea will mean more frequent flooding , more severe storms , and dramatic change . <p> It 's a problem we will share with every coastal community on every continent . A bit farther down the Atlantic Coast , there 's a place that 's on a faster track to where we too may be headed . <p> ' These Beaches Are Doomed ' <p> Leonardo Da Vinci wrote that " water is the driving force of all nature . " <p> Fascinated by great storms and terrible floods , DaVinci would have loved the Outer Banks of North Carolina . Nearly 200 miles of low-lying , shifting sand islands link into a high energy system of waves and storms . <p> A view of Rodanthe Beach on North Carolina 's Outer Banks . ( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> " These beaches are doomed , " says Orrin Pilkey , a professor at Duke University and an octogenarian prophet . " The buildings are doomed too . " <p> Traveling through a shanty town of triple-decker McMansions on stilts , motels and condos too close to the sea , we stop in the town of Nags Head . Pilkey predicts catastrophe in vacationland . <p> " The future is one of retreat , " Pilkey says . " It 's the only way to save the beach . It 's the only way to save the buildings . " <p> Orrin Pilkey , of Duke University , examines the health of a handful of sand on Nags Head Beach . " These beaches are doomed , " Pilkey predicts . " The buildings are doomed too . " ( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> The islands of North Carolina 's Outer Banks want to migrate west , and normally that happens when waves and wind along the island 's ocean side carry sand over the dunes to the back side , where the waters are quiet . Trouble begins , though , when people build big houses and motels close to the shore along the ocean side , like the Comfort Inn at Nags Head Beach . <p> Pilkey says the hotel is a symbol of the problem . <p> " There is a one , two , three , four , five , six story building that 's right next to the beach , " he says . But he missed the ground floor , which makes seven stories . Either way , the Comfort Inn is too big to move . Past storms swallowed both its pool and its deck . And Pilkey says there 's no comfort from the prospect of future storms . <p> Orrin Pilkey of Duke University , says this seven-story Comfort Inn , built on Nags Head Beach , is " a future disaster . The time will come when that will be a good offshore fishing reef . " ( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> The shoreline in the Outer Banks has lost 1,500 , in some places 3,000 feet in the last century and a half . Hurricanes and nor'easters routinely swallow homes . <p> As for the Comfort Inn , Pilkey calls it " a future disaster . The time will come when that will be a good offshore fishing reef . " <p> Retreat Or Entrench ? <p> The old-timers used to live on the backside of the barrier islands . Coastal geologist Stan Riggs , of East Carolina University , has spent half a century studying the dynamics of the Outer Banks . He says those old-timers knew that building on the ocean side of the islands is like building on railroad tracks . To protect themselves from that train , current owners and investors place their hope in engineering . But it 's the storms that bring sand in the first place . <p> " You can engineer this system , but at some point you do n't have a barrier island , " Riggs says . " And you surely wo n't have a beach unless you pump sand . " <p> Down the chain of islands , at Buxton Beach , they 're doing just that : Bulldozers are moving sand that 's spewing out of a pipe . Riggs points offshore to a dredge ship 2 miles away that 's piping it in . <p> " It comes from that dredge across the bottom , " Riggs explains . " That 's what he 's sucking out of the bottom out there . " <p> On Buxton Beach , bulldozers move sand being piped in from a dredge ship 2 miles off shore . This so-called " beach nourishment " project will cost over $7 million a mile -- $22 million in public money in all . The engineers guarantee a life of five years , but Stan Riggs says the sand is generally gone in two . ( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> It 's called " beach nourishment , " as if it 's therapeutic . But it 's a high-cost prescription to replace the sand that 's gone from the natural beach . This project costs over $7 million a mile -- $22 million in public money in all . The engineers guarantee a life of five years , but Riggs says the sand is generally gone in two years . <p> " These land masses are moving , " Riggs says . " They 've always moved , they always will move . They 're storm dependent systems . They have to have storms . If you do n't let them breathe , you 're going to kill them . " <p> Stanley Riggs , of East Carolina University , stands on the edge of the Rodanthe Beach and Highway 12 . ( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> The principle is no different for barrier beaches in Massachusetts like Crane Beachand Plum Island . Ipswich author William Sargent has written prolifically on marine ecology and sea level rise , and he says North Carolina is instructive as to what might happen here . <p> " I think it is instructive because things are happening faster down there , " Sargent says . " Places like Cape Cod are going to start breaking up . Within the last 10 years there have been areas of the outer beach on Cape Cod that have broken through . Places like Chatham will be hit hard . " <p> So will Marshfield , Scituate and Hull , as well as Sconset Bluffs on billionaire row in southeast Nantucket . <p> " As you get more and more storms , you get more and more erosion , " Sargent says . " People have to decide what they 're going to do . " <p> Decide whether to retreat , or to entrench . <p> A Bridge To Nowhere <p> In North Carolina , as we drive down Highway 12 , coastal geologist Riggs shows us where the road has been buckled , broken , breached and rolled over by storms . <p> At Oregon Inlet we watch the construction of a new bridge , 3 miles long and at the cost of nearly $250 million . A hundred year bridge that connects two islands that wo n't be here in a hundred years , Riggs says . He warned against building it and called for a ferry system instead . <p> Construction is underway to replace a bridge that caries North Carolina 's Highway 12 over the Oregon Inlet at a cost of nearly $250 million . A hundred year bridge that connects two islands that wo n't be here in a hundred years , says Stan Riggs . He warned against building it and called for a ferry system instead . ( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> From there we drive to Rodanthe , where a real estate sign close to the beach beckons : Dare To Dream The Impossible Dream . On a normal tide the surf here reaches the stilts of a high rise home that stands alone like a man awaiting the firing squad . <p> Farther south , at Buxton Beach , Riggs shows us where five homes once stood and now there is nothing but narrow beach at the base of a steep bank . <p> " This is the first time in two weeks I 've been able to sit on this beach , because the water has been right up here , low tide and high tide , every single day , " says homeowner Freve Pace . <p> Freve Pace stands in front of the house she once owned but sold because the ocean was encroaching . She bought another home down the street and now she 's starting to worry about that one too . ( Jesse Costa/WBUR ) <p> The beach used to be 200 yards wide , says one of the anxious neighbors , and Pace has one big question of Riggs about nourishing the beach : Will it work ? <p> " We do n't know , " Riggs replies . <p> Nearby , you can throw a rock from one side of the island to the other . Riggs tells Pace if the beach nourishment does work , it will buy her a little time . <p> The last stop Riggs brings us to is Seagull Drive , back in Nags Head . <p> " They were probably 75 houses here , " Riggs recalls . " Fifty of them which are in the ocean and gone . " <p> Sandbags stacked up to their decks could n't save them . A lone house , leaning derelict , is nearly ready to go -- its septic tank exposed on the beach like a sunbather on her towel . <p> If the sea level rises 3 feet by the end of the century , Riggs says , no amount of sand nourishment will provide even a temporary stay for the Outer Banks .
