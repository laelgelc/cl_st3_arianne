Plus receive unlimited digital access at Richmond.com. $19 per month after six-month introductory offer . <h> Schapiro : Rivals alike in their selective representation of facts <p> Neither Ed Gillespie nor Ralph Northam lets facts get in the way of a good rant . <p> For purposes of self-preservation , self-perpetuation or both , the candidates for Virginia governor engage in selective representation of the facts : Republican Gillespie , on the link between climate change and sea-level rise that threatens the state 's eastern coastline ; Democrat Northam , on public funding for women 's health services , including abortion , supplied by one of his biggest backers . <p> This should come as no surprise in an era of sharply polarized politics . Gillespie and Northam must play their respective bases because , increasingly , so little remains in between . <p> It flies in the face of Virginia political tradition , one defined by a broad independent streak that drove voters to support for elective office the person rather than the party . <p> That allowed Republicans to win for president and governor while Democrats lorded over the statehouse . It was slightly schizophrenic , but a consequence of the strong center-right reflex among voters that is now largely restricted to rural Virginia , where Republicans harvest a bumper crop of votes but not enough to win statewide . <p> Now , Democrats win for president and governor while Republicans lord over the statehouse . Virginia is shedding its schizophrenia and embracing -- albeit , incrementally -- a growing center-left tendency driven by rapid population growth and accelerating diversity , both of which are concentrated in the eastern half of the state and largely tie Democratic fortunes to 10 localities with populations of 200,000 or more . <p> For a guy who acknowledges climate change , Gillespie has a gift for hiding it . <p> Is that by design ? Gillespie 's campaign is n't saying , though on Tuesday it affirmed his view that human activity is contributing to climate change . <p> Skepticism within the Republican Party over climate change has been super-heating Virginia politics since 2010 , when an attorney general and gubernatorial wannabe named Ken Cuccinelli alleged that global-warming research by a scientist , formerly at the University of Virginia , may have been a scheme to defraud taxpayers . <p> Last week , Gillespie announced his plan for managing sea-level rise in Hampton Roads , where the phenomenon has deadly potential , threatening 1.6 million people , their businesses and homes and a national defense hub that includes the world 's largest naval base . <p> Gillespie 's plan is a response to sea-level rise ; for example , low-interest loans to local government to finance flood prevention and transportation improvements . <p> Gillespie 's plan does not acknowledge what science contends is a reason for sea-level rise : climate change , attributed to man 's reliance on carbon fuels , which produce heat-trapping emissions that warm the planet . <p> Look to the southwest corner of Virginia -- the seat of the flagging coal industry -- for a possible explanation for Gillespie 's omission . <p> There are n't a lot of voters out there , but they 're disproportionately Republican and taken by President Donald Trump 's vow to make coal great again . Gillespie ca n't pile up those votes by saying or doing anything that puts down coal . That includes unnecessary references to his view that climate change is real . <p> Gillespie ca n't win on rural votes , alone . He needs to break into the suburbs . His pronouncement on sea-level rise could provide entry to Hampton Roads , which is Northam 's home base and the second-largest pool of votes behind Northern Virginia , where Gillespie lives . <p> Hampton Roads may over-perform for Northam , but necessity demands Gillespie compete there and in Virginia 's other blue-trending metropolitan areas . Sometimes that requires splitting a few hairs . <p> It 's a practice to which Northam is given , too . <p> Turning up the volume on his support of abortion rights , Northam pounds Gillespie for promising to deny Virginia tax dollars to Planned Parenthood , a women 's health organization that provides abortion services -- and which last week pledged to the Democrat $3 million in digital and radio advertisements , direct mail and field staff . <p> Northam 's attack on Gillespie -- one targeting women , who make up the majority of voters -- is largely speculative . It is tied to a federal law , signed by Trump in April , that reversed an Obama administration ban on state and local governments withholding from agencies , such as Planned Parenthood , federal dollars for reproductive-health services , regardless of whether they performed abortions . <p> But is Northam 's attack substantive ? What public funds Planned Parenthood receives from Virginia barely exceed $26,000 , according to the organization . Under a contract with the state , Planned Parenthood provides HIV testing and treatment at three Planned Parenthood centers -- in Richmond , Roanoke and Norfolk . Last year , they served about 1,700 people . <p> The organization , at one time , could seek reimbursement from the state for abortion services in cases of rape , incest or gross fetal deformity for low-income women ineligible for Medicaid , a health care program for the poor . Typically , the women were illegal immigrants and represented a tiny number within the pregnancy-ending population . <p> Further , Gov . Terry McAuliffe in February vetoed Republican legislation cutting off what few dollars Planned Parenthood receives . It 's been a peg for Northam 's pledge to stand as McAuliffe said he would : as a " brick wall " against further assaults on abortion rights . <p> All of which diverts attention from a larger point : That Northam favors Medicaid expansion to significantly increase funding for Planned Parenthood -- an idea resisted by the Republicans who are still likely to control the legislature after November . <p> Facts getting in the way -- again . <p> Contact Jeff E.
