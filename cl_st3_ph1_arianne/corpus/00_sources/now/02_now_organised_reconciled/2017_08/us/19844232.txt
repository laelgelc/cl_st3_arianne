( Ashley Velez/The Root ) <p> Paulette Richards has lived in Liberty City for almost 40 years . In that time , the 57-year-old community organizer has seen some things in the close-knit and vibrant historically black community , located in northwest Miami-Dade County . <p> She 's seen young mothers struggle to feed their babies despite working multiple jobs . She 's seen kids suffering because of a lack of resources . But recently , there are some people she has n't seen -- some of her former neighbors and friends . <p> Advertisement <p> " I can get emotional even thinking about it . Where is such-and-such ? " she told The Root recently . <p> Like many predominantly black communities across the country , Liberty City is experiencing a rise in gentrification . But in the case of Liberty City and some other black Miami neighborhoods , gentrification may include another interesting factor : climate change . The idea of climate gentrification expands upon traditional gentrification by focusing on environmental issues , such as rising sea levels , as key factors in driving out , and oftentimes pricing out , those situated in areas that were previously deemed less valuable . <p> In the first of a three-part series , The Root will be exploring the ways in which race , climate and gentrification intersect and impact black communities in Miami . <p> Richards became especially passionate about climate gentrification years ago while on a trip to Washington , D.C. , to discuss environmental issues with government officials . Unfortunately , conversations surrounding climate change and rising sea levels are not being had in her neighborhood . <p> Advertisement <p> She says that residents are focused on more immediate needs like affordable housing , jobs , transportation and safety . <p> " They do n't have time for climate change . It 's at the bottom of the totem pole , " she said . <p> On the corner of Northwest 62nd Street and Northwest Seventh Avenue in Liberty City , Miami , new high-rise developments ( right ) sit across the street from Rasta Paradise and Liberty City Beauty Supply stores . The area 's current median income is little over $26,000 . <p> Nonetheless , while Liberty City may be less fortunate in some areas , it is incredibly valuable in others , especially considering that it sits 10 feet above sea level . Compare that with Miami Beach , where the median income is $164,000 and is approximately 5 feet above sea level . <p> Higher ground becomes essential -- and more valuable -- as coastal communities in the United States battle chronic flooding as a result of rising sea levels . A recent study showed that while 90 coastal regions in the U.S. are currently dealing with chronic flooding , that number is expected to increase to 170 communities in less than 20 years . Although sea level rise is a naturally occurring phenomenon , George Eberli , a professor at the University of Miami who specializes in sea level changes , says that the acceleration is alarming . <p> Higher ground becomes essential -- and more valuable -- as coastal communities in the United States battle chronic flooding as a result of rising sea levels . <p> " Wheresea level rise was during the last century , it was like 2 or 3 millimeters per year . And in 60 years , 10 inches , or in 70 years , a foot higher . So it is pretty fast , even if it does n't seem like a lot per year . She first planned a climate change art contest to see what kids in her area knew about the subject and to pique their interest in order to get conversations started in their homes . <p> She also had the idea to create a " Climate and Me " summer program for the young people in her neighborhood and saw that as a good opportunity to further disseminate information on climate change . <p> Advertisement <p> Richards said that she has to do whatever she can to help save her community : " I am a part of that village . I have to look over them . " <p> Though he has noticed some hot spots in Liberty City where high-elevation properties have been bought , he says it is difficult to state conclusively that it is solely because of climate-based gentrification . <p> " Or is the market still being moved by the crazy South Florida cycle ?
