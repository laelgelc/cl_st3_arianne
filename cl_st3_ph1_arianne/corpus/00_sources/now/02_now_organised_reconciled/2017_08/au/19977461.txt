<h> Key points : <p> They chose oranges because 60 per cent citrus eaten in WA was imported from SA , Egypt or the US <p> Orchard produces up to $20 million in turnover and creates up to 50 jobs , said Ms Middleton <p> Nine years ago , they established a 170,000-tree citrus orchard on a sheep property at Moora . <p> The presence of underground water gave them and their business partners the confidence to invest $20 million in the project , which has created jobs and pumped millions into the local economy . <p> " If this was still a sheep paddock it would produce about $150,000 worth of income a year and there 'd be less than one job , " Ms Middleton said . <p> " As a horticulture business it will produce $15 to 20 million in turnover a year , plus 40 to 50 jobs , so the difference is absolutely extraordinary , and for the community of Moora that means it 's been future-proofed as well . " <p> The council hopes Moora Citrus ' success would encourage others to switch to or invest in horticulture . <p> " Everything for the last 100 years has happened in the old traditional form of cropping and mixed farming which is low human input -- Moora Citrus has been a trailblazer and shown what can be done , " said councillor Colin Gardiner . <p> " We are optimistic there are projects in the wind where that water can drive intensive horticulture , which gives you so much more potential for jobs and that ripple effect which goes into your whole economy . " <h> Climate change sparked move to citrus <p> Ms Middleton and Mr Brennan are broadacre farmers with a cropping , sheep , grain milling and pork operation at Wongan Hill , two hours north-east of Perth . <p> Their move into citrus began nearly 20 years ago after a report made dire predictions about how climate change would affect their region . <p> " What they were talking about was by 2050 we would lose about 20 per cent of our rainfall and have increasing severe summer events , " said Ms Middleton . <p> " We decided to really push the irrigation side of the business and we looked at a number of things from olives and avocados , but oranges came up trumps , " said Mr Brennan . <p> They chose oranges because 60 per cent of all of the citrus eaten in WA was imported from South Australia , Egypt or the US . <p> " It was an import replacement strategy that we 'd come in and fill that part of the market , " said Ms Middleton . <p> " And we 'd always have the freight advantage and be able to supply to the market in a shorter time period so the fruit was fresh into the market , " Mr Brennan added . <p> " The market 's very parochial as well , Western Australians love to eat Western Australian food and they 're really connected to WA producers , so we knew we 'd have that advantage as well , and it 's quite a competitive advantage . " <h> Company inundated with orders <p> The couple have taken advantage of their proximity to market by promoting their fruit as tasting like it did in the old days . <p> " A navel orange shipped from the US might be 40 days old , and SA fruit could be a few weeks old by the time it hits the shelves , " said Ms Middleton . <p> " We aim to get our fruit on the shelves three days from picking . <p> " We pick at the point where acid and sugar levels are right , we get it to the customer when it 's absolutely at its premium so it tastes sensational , and you 've got 10 to 12 days to have it in your fruit bowl because we have n't taken 10 days to get it to you . " <p> Supermarket manager Tim Jagot said he could taste the difference and so could his customers , who started asking for the fruit weeks before picking season started in May . <p> " This is as close as you are going to get to actually having a tree in your back garden and growing them yourself , " he said . <p> " Eighty per cent of my sales are done on looks and price , and 20 per cent on the actual taste . <p> " Once you 've tried it , then customers come back week after week and they know the name , they look for it every year . " <p> The couple said they achieved their goal to take a share of the domestic WA market off South Australians earlier than planned because so much eastern states fruit was going to the booming China market . <p> Moora Citrus has tapped into that market too , with a third of the crop now exported . <p> Orchard manager Shane Kay said the company had been inundated with approaches for orders " we just ca n't supply " . <p> " We 've been exporting about 30 per cent of the crop , and 70 per cent domestic , and I see that doing a complete flip where there will be 70 per cent going offshore , " he said . <p> " Perth 's domestic market 's always been the focus and we 'll always service that first , but reality is the volumes we 're producing and other growers are producing in the state as well we 've really got to develop that export market . <p> " We 're aiming for 12,000 to 14,000 tonnes at full production , and we 're about 50 per cent of that , so we 've still got a fair bit of growth ahead of us . <p> " The orchard 's coming of age , production 's coming on and the demand is there so the timing is great . "
