CASEY KRINGLEN <p> by DAVID ALLEN <p> Yuval Sharon has instigated a mobile opera involving 126 performers , 24 limousines and six composers . He 's produced a headphone opera , set among commuters passing through one of the country 's busiest train stations . He 's encased Debussy 's ill-fated lovers , Pelleas and Melisande , in a box of frosted glass . He 's organised a recital with a twist , staging Schubert songs and short Beckett plays on a single stage . <p> And , in 12 months , he 'll become the first American to direct a production at the Bayreuth Festival in Germany , founded by Richard Wagner in 1876 and still devoted to his operas . Its 2017 season opened on July 25 ; Sharon 's new " Lohengrin " will be the centrepiece of the 2018 proceedings . <p> " At the risk of sounding really cheesy , it is a dream come true , " Sharon , 37 , said over lunch earlier this year . <p> Wagner courses through his artistic blood . Seeing " Siegfried " as a boy was a formative experience . He was Achim Freyer 's assistant for a controversial " Ring " cycle in Los Angeles in 2009 and recently directed " Die Walk ? re " in Karlsruhe , Germany . <p> In September , the Cleveland Orchestra revives Sharon 's part-live , part-animated production of Janacek 's " The Cunning Little Vixen " , and makes history by taking it to Vienna in October , in the first full staging of an opera at the storied Musikverein . There have been acclaimed engagements at European houses , including Peter Eotvos ' " Three Sisters " at the Vienna State Opera and John Adams ' " Doctor Atomic " in Karlsruhe , which won the Gotz Friedrich Prize in 2014 . <p> There is his pioneering work leading the Industry , an experimental Los Angeles company he founded in 2012 . It drew particular notice for " Hopscotch " , a breathtakingly complicated drama in 36 chapters in 2015 that was set in and around cars roaming the city 's streets . And , unusually for a director , he has partnered with symphony orchestras : His three-year term as an " artist-collaborator " of the Los Angeles Philharmonic began this past season . <p> " What I actually wanted to call him was disrupter in residence , " said Deborah Borda , the departing chief executive of that orchestra . " And then , after further contemplation , I realised that people would focus only on the name and not on the remarkable work Yuval does . " <p> Next season , building on their recent reincarnation of Lou Harrison 's " Young Caesar " , his partnership with the philharmonic will take on Mahler 's " Das Lied von der Erde " ; Andrew Norman 's new children 's opera " A Trip to the Moon " ; and a sprawling new adaptation of Orson Welles ' " War of the Worlds " , a propaganda parable , with music by Annie Gosfield . <p> In 12 months , Yuval Sharon will become the first American to direct a production at the Bayreuth Festival in Germany , founded by Richard Wagner in 1876 and still devoted to his operas . EMILY BERL <p> In September , the Cleveland Orchestra revives his part-live , part-animated production of Janacek 's " The Cunning Little Vixen " , and makes history by taking it to Vienna in October , in the first full staging of an opera at the storied Musikverein . <p> Sharon grew up in Chicago and , after studying English and dramatic arts at the University of California , Berkeley , was planning a career in the theatre . <p> " At that time , I was already getting into opera , but I thought of it as a hobby , because I never really thought about it as a viable theatrical form , " he said . <p> A spell in Berlin in 2001 and 2002 teaching English and seeing opera most nights changed that . " I was exposed to so many different approaches that it totally blew my mind , " he said . <p> The trick was to find a place open to new ideas . Sharon felt that New York , where he worked at the New York City Opera and led its VOX program for new opera , was too set in its ways . He moved to Los Angeles in 2010 , thinking consciously of that city 's history as a hotbed of musical experimentation . <p> Real disruption can be difficult in a genre that 's as comfortable in the past as opera . In the past half-century , directors have tried to shake up the art form simply by recasting standard works as provocations . <p> " My European colleagues the generation before were about shock tactics , " Sharon said . " And there 's something to it , that there 's something so incongruous with what you hear , that it wakes you up . " <p> Even so , he continued , " If you 're woken up , how does that connect you to the content , and not just to how clever the director is ? " <p> For Franz Welser-Most , the music director of the Cleveland Orchestra , avoiding mere cleverness is what makes Sharon special . " He is highly musical , " Welser-Most said , " which you ca n't say about every opera director . He has a way of going right to the core , the essence of a piece , and not just putting an idea onto a piece . He draws it out of a piece . " <p> For Sharon , then , disruption is not destruction . " Because I do things like ' ' Hopscotch " , " he said , people " expect me to be like Pierre Boulez and say that all traditional theatres should be blown up because they no longer have resonance , but I do n't think that 's true . " <p> Instead , he sees opera as a form that has to change with the times . Hence the Industry . Often commissioning composers to write to a concept provided by Sharon , the company finds innovative , site-specific ways to present new work , and with more philosophical and aesthetic rigour than the host of companies that have tried to take opera out of the opera house . <p> It 's a deliberately collaborative endeavour -- although one in which Sharon is clearly the boss -- that is committed to contemporary composers , particularly those whose work is not an easy fit for calcified institutional structures . ( Think of Meredith Monk , who has given Sharon permission to mount the first revival of her 1991 opera , " Atlas " , and who sits on the Industry 's board . ) <p> Composer Christopher Cerrone 's gorgeous " Invisible Cities " did not fit " the framework of a traditional opera company " , Cerrone said in an interview . <p> But in 2013 , Sharon gave it a production at Union Station in Los Angeles . The performers were disguised as members of the public , and the audience wandered around , hearing the music on headphones . It was a popular and critical success ; " Invisible Cities " was named a finalist for the Pulitzer Prize in 2014 and has been released on DVD on the Industry 's own label . <p> " I had maybe come to think that there was n't necessarily a place for me in the opera world before I met Yuval , " Cerrone said . " And now I feel like there is . " <p> Sharon understands that the Metropolitan Opera , say , has pressures he does n't , but he still intends the Industry 's productions to be a kind of dare to traditional opera houses . We " are challenging them to realise they can not do business as usual " , he said . " We 're facing sea changes in our daily life , and our world is in tumult , and our engagement with the world is so radically different than it was even 10 years ago . We ca n't just go into the opera house and imagine we went into this time machine . <p> " There 's a lot of fear in the opera world , " he said , drawing on a recent talk he gave as a board member of Opera America . " There 's fear about the new . There 's fear about the audience . There 's fear of the donor base . " Perhaps that fear is , on some level , rational . But , Sharon insists , " all fear is manufactured " . <p> Bowing to an audience 's perceived desires is no answer , he argues . " As arts leaders , we 're supposed to be the ones that create our audience , " Sharon said . " I see a resistance to that . It 's the idea that , ' Well , it 's what our audiences want . ' Well , it 's not always about what the audience wants . It 's about what the audience does n't yet know they want . " <p> With the preternatural optimism of someone who hunts for funding for the Industry 's projects and cajoles government officials to help put on his ambitious shows , he prescribes unyielding hope . Yet Sharon believes that hope today is challenged by the current political situation and a Trump administration that he described as " reptilian " in an essay in January . <p> What can be opera 's role in protest ? " An artwork is not a person on the barricade , " Sharon said . " But it can be the thing that led that person to the barricade . That 's what Brecht said , too : Art is a catalyst for change . " <p> That , in turn , can require a certain reshuffling of priorities . " If " Hopscotch " were planned for this fall , I 'd probably cancel it , because we do n't need a piece like " Hopscotch " right now , " he said . " We need pieces that are going to speak to this huge upheaval in our lives . " <p> The Industry 's current project is a staging of Brecht 's " Life of Galileo " -- about the battle between dogmatism and science , between conscience and persecution -- around a bonfire on Cabrillo Beach in San Pedro , with a score by Andy Akiho . That stretch of sand is at risk of disappearing because of climate change and is a mere five miles from a golf course bearing the name of the president of the United States . <p> Sharon knows that the Angelenos who attend his " Galileo " , originally scheduled for September but now due in May , are unlikely to need much political persuading , but he sees no trouble preaching to the choir . <p> " It happened 400 years ago with Galileo , " he said . " And humanity prevailed , and reason prevailed , and truth prevailed . To tell that story now reminds us that , like the great line in the piece , ' The triumph of reason is the triumph of reasonable people ' . I ca n't think of any better way to offer an antidote to what 's happening right now . "
