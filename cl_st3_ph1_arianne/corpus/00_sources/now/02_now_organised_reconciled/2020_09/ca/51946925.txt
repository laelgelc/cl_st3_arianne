<p> A coastal pond in Atwood 's Brook , Shelburne County has all but dried up , due to the lack of rainfall in southwestern Nova Scotia this summer . - Kathy Johnson <p> Last weekend , the grudging skies of western Nova Scotia dropped a bit over an inch , or 25 mm , of rain , thanks to storm Laura winging by . It would have missed us as nearly all rainstorms do in summer in recent years , except that another low pressure on its tail apparently sucked some moisture out and dropped it . <p> At any rate , where I 'm at , 15 km east of Yarmouth , that was the first rainfall of over an inch , inadequate as it was , since roughly early March . <p> This is not a one-time thing . Summers are increasingly dry throughout the southern Maritimes , with the southern tip , essentially Yarmouth and Shelburne counties , as ground zero . Back in 2016 , when it hardly rained at all from June to November , the dipping place at my local gaspereau brook , that runs with four feet of water in early spring , was reduced to a small puddle of green slime . There 's still a trickle now , but it 's as though a tropical-style wet season/dry season pattern is setting in . <p> What we 're talking about is climate change , the real challenge beyond the coronavirus , Trump and whatnot . We have provoked the Fates with our destructive excess , and they 're playing with us with fearsome power . As we keep blowing by the scientists ' proclaimed climatic tipping points , no matter where we are we can now only await what the four grim horsemen -- drought , flood , wind , fire -- have in store for us . <p> Particularly disturbing is the speed with which all this is happening . The southwest tip is a case in point . <p> I semi-retired here to my place of origin , the village of Belleville , 18 @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ some ice -- enough to fish smelts on the Tusket River . Now , except when the polar vortex occasionally spins downward , there 's no more ice ( and no more smelts either ) , only temporary glazes , kin to the great polar ice sheets collapsing . <p> In my youth , the lakes would freeze a foot thick , starting in early December and staying put until early April . Until the early 1970s , people were still ice fishing smelts before Christmas on saltwater inlets that required a lot of freezing . <p> The seasons have gone all wonky -- a lot like the rest of the southern Maritimes , but more so . Summer , except for the droughts , seems like the most normal season -- except that it never quite ends . There 's no old-fashioned autumn any more , with crisp frosts in October . Sometimes there are very few before Christmas . Winter is mostly a dank , muddy thing with little or no snow . Other stuff it brings is not so good -- including the insects that weak winters ca n't kill -- ticks , earwigs , various tree parasites , notably the woolly adelgid that killing off the hemlocks , Nova Scotia 's oldest and most majestic trees , with warnings of other destructive parasites on the way from the U.S. northeast . <p> Indeed the forest itself is an issue . Federal studies indicate that Maritime softwood forests will decline as the climate warms . If you add drought to that , it gets worse . This is unscientific , but my observation is that hardly anything is growing anew on the clearcuts of the last 10-20 years around me , except drought-hardy bushes and ferns and the suckers of well-rooted maple stumps ( making the spraying of clearcuts to kill off hardwoods all the more absurd ) . Indeed , spruce turning suddenly brown and dead is unusually common here . I 've spent my last few winters cutting some down , on my property and others . The flip side of summer drought ( and unusually calm days ) is that every weather front that comes through from December to March seems to try to reach 100 km an hour , with trees down and shingles flying . <p> Perhaps the small mercy is that this is not , in the main , an agricultural area , although it 's damnable to us gardeners , having to replant , sometimes several times , in cold springs and lug water if it does n't rain . <p> It 's hard to come up with any upbeat conclusion here . What we need is a global determination , deeply rooted in every society , to face climate change head on . But with deniers still high in the saddle , especially in the U.S. , that 's hard . Another reason why the U.S. election is critical . Getting the denier-in-chief out of there is the world 's best chance of starting something positive .
