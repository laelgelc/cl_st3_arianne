<h> DALE SMITH : A lasting legacy within the premier 's grasp <p> This mature sugar maple forest in Colchester County is protected land , but not all of Nova Scotia 's forests are so lucky . There 's much to be done ensure responsible stewardship of our public lands , writes Dale Smith . - Contributed <p> DALE SMITH <p> Robin Wilber 's Aug. 30 call for Premier Stephen McNeil to enhance his legacy by finding a way to resurrect Northern Pulp brought to mind the paradoxical messaging embedded in a mid-1980s movie title , Back to the Future , or perhaps in an unmade sequel , Forward to the Past . Either way , an effort that would bolster industrial control and exploitation of the province 's Crown lands is not a record the premier could wear well with pride . <p> A more fitting lasting legacy for the premier would be to have led a fundamental transition to public land stewardship in recognition of 21st-century challenges , including climate change and biodiversity loss . When released in 2011 , the resources strategy was the outcome of the most comprehensive and credible public review of resources policy ever attempted in Nova Scotia and , although less progressive than many had hoped , it was generally accepted as a positive step in the right direction . And the 2013 Parks and Protected Areas plan was the culmination of province-wide planning and consultation extending back to the early 1990s , almost 25 years of concerted effort and public support . <p> The low point was reached in October 2016 , when the government abandoned its commitment to reduce clearcutting by 50 per cent . Faced with a strong public backlash and a looming spring election , the Liberal election platform promised an independent review @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of the resources review ) and the introduction of biodiversity legislation . <p> These promises are the building blocks of a reimagined approach to the responsible stewardship of our public lands , but they must be pushed over the finish line to qualify as the stuff of legacy . <p> The independent review , conducted by Professor Bill Lahey , advanced the concepts of ecological forestry and the triad model of public land management . Although generally well-received , Lahey 's recommendations failed to recognize that ( 1 ) forestry activity on Crown land must be managed within an overriding framework of public land use policy and planning , and ( 2 ) the prevailing structure of government , specifically the conflicting responsibilities and perspectives of the forestry and environment departments , effectively dooms the triad model to failure from the get-go and thereby sucks the life out of the promise of ecological forestry . <p> The current draft of the biodiversity legislation , although introduced in the provincial legislature and having reached the law amendments review stage , is too limited in purpose , scope and strength , and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Areas plan remain yet to be delivered . <p> So , legacy-building work remains to be done , including : <p> updating the Crown Land Act to establish land-use planning and coordination capability as needed to implement the three zones of the triad -- protected areas , high-production forestry lands and an intermediate zoning category in which conservation and forestry objectives are to be " blended ; " <p> improvement of the draft biodiversity legislation , and integration of biodiversity requirements into a new ecological forestry act designed to guide forestry activity where permitted within the essential Crown land zoning framework ; <p> designation of additional protected areas to meet the government 's 13 per cent commitment , and increase of Nova Scotia 's land protection target to at least 17 per cent in recognition of evolving national and international standards ;
