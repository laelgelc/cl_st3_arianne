Here 's what experts say we can expect <p> VANCOUVER -- The thick , acrid smoke that 's blanketed much of Western Canada in recent days due to wildfires in the U.S. should not be viewed as a temporary irritant but as part of a worsening , climate change-propelled trend that could have far-reaching impacts on how we live -- and how long we live , health and environmental experts said Monday . <p> Care to envision that future ? <p> Think more " clean-air shelters " for people with respiratory ailments to take refuge , experts say . Think more homes and office buildings being retrofitted with better air filtration systems . Think competitive sporting events normally held in the summer potentially being rescheduled for other parts of the year . Think kids ' summer camps having to consider alternatives to outdoor activities . <p> And , on a grimmer note , think of the potential for diminished life expectancy . <p> " For most people , this 'll be a short-term event , they 'll recover . There wo n't be any long-lasting impacts , " said Michael Brauer , a professor of environmental health at the University of British Columbia . <p> " But this event right now will probably increase our annual air pollution in Vancouver around 30 per cent . If we count on that , if we 're going to say our annual burden of air pollution has increased by 30 per cent because of fires every summer , that 'll have a long-lasting impact on the health of the population , the same kinds of things we see when we compare a clean city like Vancouver to a more polluted city like Los Angeles . " <p> After sitting for several days at or near the top of a list of the world 's major cities with the worst air quality , Vancouver was expected to get a bit of a reprieve with an infusion of Pacific marine air , forecasters said Monday . <p> But experts say the long-term projections are clear . <p> " We know there is a very clear link between climate change and wildfires , because hotter , drier conditions allow fires to spread more widely , " said Steve Easterbrook , director of the University of Toronto 's School of the Environment . <p> " So in the future , as climate change escalates , we can expect not just more summers like this , but also summers that are far worse . That does n't mean every year will be worse than the last , but , on average , the conditions will get steadily worse . In a decade or two , we might look back on this summer as relatively mild in comparison . " <p> Do n't expect to return to any kind of " normal " in our lifetimes , he added . <p> " When we stop burning fossil fuels altogether , whatever temperature the planet has reached at that point will become the new normal . " <p> Armel Castellan , a Victoria-based meteorologist at Environment Canada , prefers to avoid the term " new normal " entirely . <p> " We should expect the unexpected , or we should at least expect high variability in our weather as a result of this changing climate , " he said . " We 're going to see more highs , more lows ... when it comes to precipitation patterns , when it comes to smoke . " <p> So what will that mean for us on a practical , tangible level ? <p> In the same way public health officials have for years been issuing alerts and advisories related to extreme heat , we will likely see more public communication about air quality during the summer fire season . <p> " We need to enhance that , get that out to many more communities and keep the messaging , because these are not one-offs , " Brauer said . <p> Office buildings , hospitals and seniors ' homes could be nudged to improve air filtration systems . People we already know are at greater risk , such as those with asthma or heart disease , pregnant women , and individuals with Type 2 diabetes , could be urged to invest in portable air cleaners for their homes . <p> And for those who ca n't afford them ? <p> " Many communities are now establishing the kinds of things we have for heat events . Instead of cooling shelters we have clean-air shelters -- places where people can go and get some relief from the smoke if they ca n't do that in the home , " Brauer said . <h> Loading ... <p> Loading ... It 'll also be imperative for organizers of summer athletic events to build in contingency plans to take into account the likelihood of smoke or fire events , he added . <p> " I remember a few years ago , the Ironman in Penticton , B.C. , there was a smoke event . Do you hold this event or not ? " <p> The same kind of thinking will need to go into children 's day camps and family vacations . <p> " Every time I go to the Okanagan this time of year , you 'd better be thinking about the possibility that there could be smoke or , even worse , fires -- be ready to pivot . " <p> Castellan said society needs to start conceptualizing the costs of annual wildfires not only in terms of structures lost , communities evacuated and firefighting resources dispatched . <p> You also have to consider the impact of residual smoke and the number of people touched by that , plus the related uptick in hospital visits and premature deaths . <p> " Those numbers are starting to mushroom to orders of magnitude larger than the actual firefighting efforts , " he said . <p> Just look at the devastating wildfires in B.C. in 2017-18 , he said . <p> " At the end of the day , when we see 2018 wildfire season and there 's fires in all four quadrants of B.C. there 's no option to evacuate a community , because where are you going to evacuate them to ? There 's smoke everywhere . " <p> Miriam Diamond , a professor in the University of Toronto 's department of earth sciences , said she also worries the recurring wildfire disasters are exacerbating divisions between the rich and poor . <p> " Poor people ca n't afford to pick up and move and relocate -- they do n't have summer houses and cottages , " she said . <p> " Individuals with low socio-economic status already have a higher probability of having health concerns . Then , add on to that the challenge of really bad air quality . Double whammy .
