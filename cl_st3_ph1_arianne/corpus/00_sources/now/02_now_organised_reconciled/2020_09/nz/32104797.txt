<h> Back to the future <p> The discovery of a box of what looked like white powder at Dunedin Art School has led artist Tim Barlow back to his old art school for a residency . He tells Rebecca Fox about rediscovering old construction methods . <p> Excavating through a box of what turned out to be icing sugar , giant jellied animals that artist Tim Barlow had made in the 1990s were re-discovered . <p> " I 'd left it for the Dunedin Art School in 1994 . It was a box of sweets , giant-sized . They were animals that were introduced pests . In the early ' 90s they were wreaking havoc on the country -- possums , stoats , rats that sort of thing . I 'd been producing an ecological , environmental installation for my honours year and this was part of the exhibition . " <p> That box was significant for two reasons -- it led to the art school tracking down Barlow as part of its 150th celebrations search for alumni and it marked Barlow 's first foray into works with an environmental @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ that first approach , came the idea for him to take part in the artist 's residency programme at the school . " <p> It has also , thanks to Covid-19 delaying the residency , tied in nicely with this weekend 's " Mapping the Anthropocene in Dunedin -- climate change , community and research in the creative arts " symposium and art exhibition , allowing him to present progress on his project . <p> The project is centred around lime production as an alternative to concrete production , which is a major contributor to CO2 emissions . <p> " Lime production has a fascinating history from early New Zealand colonial times going back thousands of years to the Egyptians , who used this beautiful product .
