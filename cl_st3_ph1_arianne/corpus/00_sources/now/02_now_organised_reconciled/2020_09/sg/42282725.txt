<h> Labour and fertiliser shortages due to the pandemic , combined with heavy rains and diseases , have damaged tea yields and quality , cutting incomes for small-scale farmers . <h> Share <p> In the 25 years he has grown tea in eastern Nepal , Yuvraj Gautam never had a yield this low . <p> First , diseases such as leaf curl and black tip attacked the crops on his three-hectare ( seven-acre ) farm in Ilam district - and when it was time to harvest the tea that survived , workers were hard to find due to Nepal 's coronavirus lockdown , he said . <p> Then , in July , heavy rainfall across much of the country caused a landslide that swept away a portion of his crop . <p> As a result , Gautam expects to bring in 200,000 Nepalese rupees ( $1,700 ) less than he did last year . <p> " If production continues to decline and marketing becomes difficult , it will be harder for me to pay the labourers , " said the 40-year-old farmer . <p> Already struggling with rising pest infestations @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ hit this year as severe rains since May and the pandemic 's ripple effects have slashed their yields and diluted the quality of their product . <p> So far in 2020 , Nepal has seen a 30-40 per cent decline in its tea harvest compared with a year ago , according to Indra Adhikari of the state-run National Tea and Coffee Development Board ( NTCDB ) . <p> Adhikari , who is regional manager for Ilam , one of Nepal 's main tea-producing regions , noted that tea plants grow in " flushes " , harvested several times a season . The initial flushes are higher quality than the later ones . <p> In recent years , the dormant periods between flushes have lengthened , giving the plants less time to re-grow in each cycle , Adhikari said , pointing to climate change as the cause . <p> On top of that , short bursts of intense rainfall have become more common , water-logging fields and causing defects in the tea plants , he added .
