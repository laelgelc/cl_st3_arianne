A modeling study estimates the distance within which metro centers could meet food needs if they tried to feed themselves locally . Some -- but not all -- could rely on nearby agricultural land , and dietary changes would increase local potential , according to the study . <p> Share : <p> FULL STORY <p> Some but not all U.S. metro areas could grow all the food they need locally , according to a new study estimating the degree to which the American food supply could be localized based on population , geography , and diet . <p> advertisement <p> The modeling study , led by Christian Peters at the Gerald J. and Dorothy R. Friedman School of Nutrition Science and PolicyGerald J. @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ at Tufts University , is published today in Environmental Science &amp; Technology . <p> The model estimates whether 378 metropolitan areas could meet their food needs from local agricultural land located within 250 kilometers ( 155 miles ) . Local potential was estimated based on seven different diets , including the current typical American diet . <p> The results suggest : <p> * Metro centers in the Northwest and interior of the country have the greatest potential for localization . <p> * Large portions of the population along the Eastern Seaboard and the southwest corner of the U.S. would have the least potential for localization . <p> * Surplus land existed under all diet scenarios , raising questions about the best use of land for meeting health , environmental , and economic goals . <p> " Not everyone lives near enough agricultural land to have an entirely local or even regional food supply . Most cities along the Eastern Seaboard and in the southwest corner of the U.S. could not meet their food needs locally , even if every available acre of agricultural land was used for local food production @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ country are surrounded by ample land to support local and regional food systems , " said Peters , senior author and associate professor at the Friedman School , whose research focuses on sustainability science . <p> Peters and his team also modeled seven different diets to estimate whether dietary changes could make a difference in the potential to produce sufficient food for a metro area . The diets ranged from the current typical American diet , which is high in meat , to vegan . Reducing animal products in the diet increased the potential to produce all food locally , up to a point . Diets with less than half the current consumption of meat supported similar levels of localization potential , whether omnivore or vegetarian . Consumption of meat ( beef , pork , chicken and turkey ) for the baseline typical American diet was estimated at roughly five ounces per day . <p> " There would be different ways to do it . In the current American agricultural system , some farmland is used for biofuels and export crops . The researchers point out that if metro centers focused on eating locally , many agricultural areas would face new questions about local land use priorities . <p> " It would be important to make sure policies for supporting local or regional food production benefit conservation and create opportunities for farmers to adopt more sustainable practices .
