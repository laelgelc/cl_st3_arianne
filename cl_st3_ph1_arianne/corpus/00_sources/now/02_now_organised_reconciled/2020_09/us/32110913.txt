<h> Sharing <h> Journals weigh up double-blind peer review <h> Article tools <p> Evaluating research papers without seeing the authors ' names could reduce the effects of conscious or subconscious bias . <p> In efforts to increase fairness in science publishing , some journals are experimenting with the idea of ' blinding ' reviewers to the identity of the authors . <p> Some researchers have long worried that manuscripts submitted for publication are judged not on the quality of the work but on the reputation of the author submitting it . Although authors are rarely told who is reviewing their work , reviewers generally are informed of whose papers they are evaluating . <p> But last week an article in Conservation Biology1 revealed that journal would be considering ' double blind ' peer review -- in which neither the reviewer nor the reviewed knows the other 's identity . Double-blind peer review is common in the humanities and social sciences , but very few scientific journals have adopted it . <p> Mark Burgman , a biologist at the University of Melbourne in Australia and editor-in-chief of Conservation Biology , says @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ year and found that it has " overwhelming support " , especially among younger and minority scientists . In one study2 , otherwise identical CVs were ranked higher by tenure-track academics if they came with male names than if they came with female names . <p> " I 'm really not pointing the finger , " says Darling , who wrote the new Conservation Biology article . " We all hold biases . But supporters say that although this is inevitable in some cases , in others the guesses will be wrong , and that the element of doubt increases fairness . <h> Fair trial <p> A trial of double-blind peer reviewing is going on at Nature Publishing Group ( NPG ) , which owns Nature . Since June 2013 , Nature Geoscience and Nature Climate Change have offered double-blind peer review as an option for those submitting manuscripts . <p> Philip Campbell , editor-in-chief of Nature and Nature-branded journals , says : " There is a widespread concern out there that referees may be biased by the authorship of a paper .
