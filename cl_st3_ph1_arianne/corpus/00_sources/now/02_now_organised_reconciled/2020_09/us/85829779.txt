<p> As the world heats up , cities with heat-trapping asphalt and little tree cover have left residents sweltering and breathing in more air pollution . <p> Asphalt is releasing hazardous air pollutants into communities , especially when hit with extreme heat and sunlight , according to research published in the journal Science Advances on Wednesday . Researchers found that asphalt in California 's South Coast Air Basin emitted more secondary organic aerosols in the summer than gas and diesel motor vehicles combined . <p> While vehicle emissions are likely to decline in the future , asphalt emissions will likely become worse as cities expand and climate change accelerates . <p> The new findings are critical as more frequent and intense heat waves roast neighborhoods that have lots of asphalt and little to no cooling vegetation . <p> Over the past 60 years , every decade has been hotter than the last and 2020 is set to be the hottest year on record . The heat and air pollution disproportionately affect poor and marginalized people who are more likely to live in neighborhoods without tree cover but abundant with asphalt @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ more people than any other weather event , Black and Latino people are more likely to reside in hot areas . <p> Researchers heated asphalt to temperatures from 104 degrees Fahrenheit to 392 degrees Fahrenheit and noticed that asphalt emissions persisted at a steady rate after heated with summertime temperatures , which suggests that asphalt continues to release air pollutants even after the summer sun and heat pass . <p> " While emissions from some other sources might decrease in the future , the current consumption of asphalt materials and their emissions may remain similar or increase with elevated summertime urban temperatures driven by climate change and urban heat island effects , thus affecting their relative impact on urban air quality over time , " said Drew Gentner , a Yale University professor and author of the study .
