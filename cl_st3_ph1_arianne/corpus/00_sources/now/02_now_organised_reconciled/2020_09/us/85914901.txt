<p> While Chicagoans have been working from home or not working at all , the economy that fuels their city has changed , perhaps permanently . <p> This is more than the predictions that many of us wo n't be going back to offices , or that vast tracts of commercial real estate will stand vacant , or that the restaurants and stores that live on Loop traffic will shrivel and shutter . <p> Advertisement <p> Instead , the way Chicago earns its living has shifted . The city , like other major cities around the world , is a global city , a hub in the global economy , and that economy is on the ropes . <p> As everyone knows , globalization is no unalloyed blessing . Rather , it is a powerful economic force that richly rewards some and impoverishes others , creating deep divides between countries and within countries . The chasm between the globalized North Side of Chicago and the left-behind South and West sides is a perfect example . For many , the death of globalization would be good news indeed . <p> Advertisement @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the global economy is the only economy we 've got . Like the industrial economy before it , it is flawed , often cruel , but it pays the bills . If Chicago is to mend its divisions , it will have to do it with the money it reaps from its status as a global city . <p> Globalization is the direct descendant of the death of industrialization . Forty years ago , I wrote a series for the Chicago Tribune , called " City on the Brink , " describing the civic impact of the departure of the mills and factories that kept the city alive for a century . Chicago , like many of its workers , had lost its job and had no way to pay its bills . <p> The global economy came along and saved the city , or at least part of it . If it goes , Chicago will be unemployed . Look at once-mighty cities like Detroit or Cleveland to see what that means . <p> The pandemic has accelerated this process , but the cracks in globalization were visible before @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ economy ungoverned by national laws , but dependent on rules and cooperation among its many players , both governmental and corporate . <p> The two biggest players by far are the United States and China , and they no longer play by the same rules or cooperate to keep this economy functioning . Trade wars already flare and both countries are even preparing for real war . <p> Nationalism , epitomized by the Trump administration 's " America First " policies , is eroding the trust that undergirds all strong economies . <p> An early attempt at globalization , in the late 19th and early 20th centuries , fell victim to the two world wars . It took 60 years to recover the degree of international integration that we knew in 1914 . It could be happening again . <p> Chicago today lives on global commerce -- all the city 's law firms , markets , accounting firms , consultants , the many companies and employees who are plugged into the global economy . <p> It also lives on its great universities , which rely on students from around the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ China who pay full tuition . <p> It lives on business travel -- all those meetings and conventions and trade shows that fill its hotels and restaurants . If these travelers forsake air travel for Zoom , O'Hare will become an empty white elephant . <p> Increasingly , it relies on tourism , millions of persons from around the globe flying to Chicago to see the sights , attend the concerts , fill the museums -- and spend their money . <p> It relies on immigration , the hundreds of thousands of persons , rich and poor , who have flooded into the city since its economic rebirth . They have come for only one reason -- jobs , from investment banking to valet parking . Once here , they have enriched and revitalized the city .
