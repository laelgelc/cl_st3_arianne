<h> Shawn Vestal : We have met the cause of the wildfires , and it 's usually one of us <p> UPDATED : Thu. , Sept. 10 , 2020 <p> Spokane County Fire District 8 crews were busy battling fires on Trails Road west of Spokane , a wildfire burning near Rosalia , and dispatched crews as part of a strike team to tackle a wildfire burning in Whitman County on September 7 , 2020 . ( Photo courtesy of Spokane County Fire District 8 . ) <p> Campfires and cigarettes . Sparking machinery and fireworks . Slash piles and debris burns that grow out of control . <p> And , yes , even gender-reveal parties gone wrong . <p> Our sudden , explosive wildfire season is just one more way that we manage to prove Pogo 's credo correct : We have met the enemy and he is us . The fires that increasingly plague the West -- growing bigger and more destructive as the climate warms , droughts increase and the forests dry -- are almost always started by something one of us did . <p> As we @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ another season of extraordinary cost to homes and families and state government , we should make time to consider how to do it less . <p> That would require more time and energy and thoughtfulness when the fires are n't burning . It would require listening to the fire suppression officials when the skies are n't orange . There 's no question : If we can attack human carelessness , improve vigilance and compliance , and instill protective practices on the front end , we can start fewer fires . <p> " We really need the people of Washington to help us out , " said Janet Pearce , the communications manager for wildfire for the state Department of Natural Resources . <p> We 're still in the smoky midst of wildfires that have been mostly set by humans in Washington and all around the West . One California fire was indeed caused by a pyrotechnic device at a gender-reveal party , officials said this week ; it was a tragicomic case , perfectly made for social media . But most of the time , the causes are more routine @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ bounds . <p> Our current burst of wildfires came in a rush on Labor Day , Pearce said . <p> " We had 58 fire-starts in one day , and we had no lightning and no volcanic explosions , " Pearce said , " which means they were human-caused . " <p> A mere two days later , more than 500,000 acres had burned statewide , four times last year 's total . More like a bomb than like a fire . <p> Most of those were related to outdoor recreation -- campfire embers catching the wind , mostly , but other activities can also lead to fires , such as riding four-wheelers without the right spark arresters , she said . Overall , though , the most common cause of wildfires in Washington is people burning debris on their property and failing to keep it controlled . <p> There 's now a ban on such burning , but that does n't mean it 's disappeared . <p> So far this season , DNR has reported 853 fires . Of those , 232 are either of undetermined origin or under @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ fires -- 222 -- were caused by debris burning . <p> Recreation-related causes , such as campfires , account for 107 fires so far this year . <p> Just 29 were caused by lightning -- the same as arson . Smokers caused 10 . <p> Federal statistics show that between 2000 and 2017 , nearly 90% of wildfires were human-caused -- from negligence , the use of machinery or equipment , campfires or smokers , or arson . Human causes include starts that are not negligent or purposeful . When a tree falls on a power line and that sparks a fire , it 's considered human-caused . If a piece of farm machinery sends a spark to the right fuel , it 's human-caused . <p> Wildfires have been intensifying for years , and this is shaping up to be a record year of destruction around the West . With climate change and fuel buildup in the forest , conditions are n't improving . As the fires and acreages grow , that expense has grown -- the average for the past five years is $2.3 billion . <p> The sudden burst of wildfires this week pushed state firefighting resources past their limit . We spend an average of $153 million a year on firefighting in Washington , and officials say they need more . <p> They also need more from the people of Washington . More caution . More care .
