<p> Heavy smoke from wildfires that choked much of California in recent weeks was more than an annoyance . <p> It was deadly . And it almost certainly killed more people than the flames from the massive fires themselves , health experts say . <p> Between Aug. 1 and Sept. 10 , the historically bad concentrations of wildfire smoke were responsible for at least 1,200 and possibly up to 3,000 deaths in California that otherwise would not have occurred , according to an estimate by researchers at Stanford University . Those fatalities were among people 65 and older , most of whom were living with pre-existing medical conditions such as heart disease , diabetes and respiratory ailments . <p> By comparison , through Wednesday , 26 people have died directly in wildfires this year statewide . <p> " Clean air is much more important than we realize , " said Marshall Burke , an associate professor of earth system science at Stanford who calculated the impacts . Known as " PM 2.5 , " for particulate matter that is smaller than 2.5 microns , the microscopic soot particles are so small that it would take 30 or more to span the width of a human hair . <p> Generated by diesel trucks , power plants , fireplaces and other sources , the tiny particles can travel deep into the lungs , even entering the bloodstream , when people breathe them in high concentrations . <p> In mild levels they cause itchy eyes and sore throats , coughing and a tight feeling in the chest . In more severe instances , they can trigger asthma attacks , heart attacks , strokes or respiratory failure , particularly in the elderly , infants and people with heart and lung problems . <p> Smoke levels broke all-time records in California . The Bay Area Air Quality Management District called 30 " Spare the Air " days in a row from Aug. 18 to Sept. 16 .
