<h> Amir Lebdioui <h> September 1st , 2020 <h> The case for a green recovery in post-COVID Latin America <p> The impact of COVID-19 will be more serious in Latin America than anywhere else in the world . To mitigate the damage and improve long-term prospects , the region needs to target a green recovery based on environmentally friendly growth and an expansion of renewable energy , writesAmir Lebdioui ( LSE Latin America and Caribbean Centre ) . <p> The combination of COVID-19 and collapsing oil prices is expected to cause Latin American economies to contract by an average of 7.2% in 2020 . Bouncing back from this massive shock will be no easy task . There is , however , a growing realisation that a return to the status quo will not be enough , and momentum is building towards the idea of " building back better " . <p> To date , most of the debate around the idea of a green recovery ( and previously the Green New Deal ) has taken place within a handful of advanced economies , with only minor exceptions like the " @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ context . The irony is that Latin America has great need and great potential for a green recovery , not least because its rich legacy of development thinking has increasingly incorporated indigenous perspectives that promote development in harmony with nature ( especially in Bolivia and Ecuador ) . <h> Why does the post-COVID recovery need to be green ? <p> One crucial advantage of green recovery programmes is that they offer an opportunity to tackle several interrelated socioeconomic objectives at the same time : economic development , job creation , decarbonisation , and the kind of improved public health that also increases resilience to pandemics . In essence , green stimulus packages aim to address the so-called " triple crisis " of interrelated economic , social , and ecological problems by creating a vibrant , climate-resilient economy with broadly shared benefits for all . <p> Green stimulus programmes need to be prioritised for three main reasons : <p> Environmental protectionAlthough the pandemic led indirectly to positive short-term environmental outcomes , several international organisations and experts have warned that this is no silver lining . Cars have also been favoured over public transportation as citizens seek to reduce contamination risks . Urgent action is needed if emissions are to be reduced sustainably in the long term . <p> Public healthCOVID-19 has had an impact on the environment , but the environment also has an impact on pandemics . Energy transitions are a public health issue too , as they can generate long-term health benefits and bolster resilience to pandemics . The cleaner air associated with cleaner energy can help reduce COVID-19 deaths . Environmental degradation and climate change also contribute to the transmission of diseases from animals to humans . Building long-term resilience to pandemics will therefore require stricter environmental regulations . <p> Recovery rather than rescueAs emphasised by Lord Stern of LSE 's Grantham Research Institute on Climate Change and the Environment , a rescue without a recovery has no future . A recent study by the Inter-American Development Bank revealed that Chile 's pension and sovereign wealth funds would have reaped even higher returns through green investing . The idea that there is always a trade-off between sustainability and financial returns is a fallacy . <p> In the post-COVID-19 context , stimulus packages provide an opportunity to leverage the socioeconomic benefits associated with energy transition , but relying on market forces alone wo n't be enough . These include supplier-development programmes , fiscal incentives , price-control mechanisms , support for research and development ( R&amp;D ; notably through long term patient capital ) , and improved education and training programmes . <h> Climate change and Latin American development <p> These issues are particularly crucial to the future of Latin America , because the region 's trade is intrinsically linked to climate change . Several Latin American countries are dependent on fossil fuels that are at risk of becoming stranded assets as the world decarbonises its economic systems . The growing popularity of Green New Deal proposals in the United States and the European Union will inevitably bring regulatory changes that will reshape consumption patterns . Developing countries need to anticipate these " green " trade regulations and sustainability standards , shifting their productive capabilities towards export of green goods and services that will enjoy long-term access to the largest consumer markets . <h> The Latin American response so far <p> While Costa Rica has recently launched a " bioeconomy strategy " that seeks to address the challenge of COVID-19 via promotion of a green knowledge economy , most of the region @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ other way , opting for fiscal austerity and increased environmental degradation . <p> Martha Alicia Benavente , from Tucur ? in Guatemala , shows off parts from her solar-lamp business after retraining as a solar engineer ( Ryan Brown/UN Women , CC BY-NC-ND 2.0 ) <p> In Brazil , the recent intensification of efforts to dismantle environmental protections came alongside a scaling back of environmental enforcement during the coronavirus outbreak . In Ecuador , the government has announced that it will cut the education budget in response to the pandemic ; in the long term , this will have a negative impact on Ecuador 's accumulation of skilled human capital and therefore also its ability to upgrade into higher-value added activities . <p> Such responses are simply unsustainable in the long run . A country 's ability to escape the middle-income trap largely depends on its ability to build innovative and productive capabilities , which requires skilled human capital and R&amp;D support . In 2014 , just four countries accounted for three quarters of the renewable energy patents filed worldwide : China , the United States , Japan , and Germany . The lagging share of spending on low-carbon R&amp;D in developing regions ( including Latin America ) will have important implications for domestic firms ' ability to make best use of the window of technological opportunity provided by energy transitions . <h> Is a green stimulus risky or unrealistic ? <p> Critics may argue that green industries are beyond the efficiency frontier of Latin American firms , or that policy interventions might be too costly . These kinds of ambitious programmes are also likely to face opposition from fossil-fuel lobbies and their domestic allies , meaning that governments favouring green transitions will need to be skilful in bringing broad alliances and state-business coalitions onside .
