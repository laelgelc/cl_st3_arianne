<p> There are few things more forlorn than discarded balsam firs littering the snowbanks of the city post-New Year 's Day . They signal the end of a happy holiday season , the death of a short but productive arboreal life and , for some , an environmental waste . <p> The good news is that in Montreal they find a second useful existence , albeit one that involves incineration . Compared to sending them to the garbage dump , where they rot and create planet-warming methane gas , their end is at least somewhat beneficial . <p> Every year , firms contracted by the city collect roughly 25,000 trees . The 350 tonnes of holiday refuse are trucked to locations in Laval and Ch ? teauguay , where they 're converted into wood chips that are then sold to firms -- often pulp and paper mills -- that burn and use them as energy sources instead of oil . Montreal pays $43 a tonne , or $15,000 , every year for the trees to be picked up . <p> " We are doing it for the environment -- even if we have to pay for the service , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , so it 's a small price to turn a biomass into a product that can be of use , " city spokesman Philippe Sabourin said . <p> Other municipalities turn them into mulch to be spread on gardens as a soil conditioner or as a weed suppressant . Local sales are not tracked , and thus are hard to estimate . <p> " There were a few years , from 2004 to 2014 , where it was very difficult for us -- we were selling trees below the cost of production , because we had a surplus , " he said .
