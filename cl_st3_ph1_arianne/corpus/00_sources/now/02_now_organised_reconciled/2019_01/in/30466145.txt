<p> New Delhi : The findings of a new study could prove to be crucial in understanding the gap between scientific and public consensus on issues such as climate change and genetically modified organisms ( GMO ) . <p> A paper published in Nature Human Behaviour found that the staunchest opponents of GMO foods usually knew the least about genetics , and also overestimated their ability to understand the subject . <p> The study departs from earlier hypotheses that tried to explain the opposition to GMOs based on political affiliation . <p> Researchers asked a demographically diverse group of 500 US residents to rate their level of concern with and opposition to GMOs . Most respondents were wary of GMOs because they were uncertain about biotechnology . More than 90% reported concern and a similar number were also opposed to it . <p> However , the study found that opposition was not significantly different among self-reported liberals , moderates and conservatives . <p> Questions were also included to test if respondents understood science in general and genetics in particular . And another pattern emerged here . While people with lower levels of opposition assessed their understanding well , those who were strongly opposed overrated their knowledge . <p> The scientists concluded , " For extremists , knowing less is associated with thinking one knows more . " This is called the Dunning-Kruger effect , a cognitive bias where people rate their abilities to be better than they actually are . <p> The researchers then expanded the study to Europe , conducting a similar study in Germany and France . Here , too , the findings were similar . Literacy about genetics was inversely related with opposition to GMOs . However , here , the stronger opponents did not overestimate their knowledge of genetics like their American counterparts had . <p> Using the European Commission 's Eurobarometer survey , the researchers then tested 1,000 people in each EU country about their opinions of GMOs .
