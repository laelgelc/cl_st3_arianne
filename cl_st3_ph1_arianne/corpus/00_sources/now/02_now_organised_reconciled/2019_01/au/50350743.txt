<h> Ignore the averages , we 're not necessarily due for a sharemarket crash <p> By Ross Barry <p> 13 Jan 2019 -- 11:00 PM <p> It is that time of the year again when we turn a weather eye to the horizon and make predictions on all things from the climate , the cricket and , of course , the sharemarket . It 's a good time to make forecasts -- when the hot sun and surf make us a little more philosophical and before we all get too busy to remember who said what . <p> Predicting share market crashes is fraught , however , especially for the perennial doomsdayer . Since the collapse of railroad stocks in the late 1840s , there have been just 15 major share market crashes -- that 's one every 11 years on average . True , the last one did begin at the start of 2008 , exactly 11 years ago . <p> But averages can be deceiving . Perhaps the difficulty of distinguishing the Trump presidency from a modern Hollywood blockbuster adds to the current sense of gloom . But there is much to remain confident about . Most developments in 2018 , in my view , did more to preserve the normal functioning of share markets than to undermine it . <p> The return of volatility and normal pricing of risk is a good thing . Events that act as checks and balances on the natural human tendency to over-confidence are important negative feedback loops that prevent markets entering more speculative manias . Such manias are characterised by positive ( reinforcing ) feedback loops -- think margin lending , herding , index-tracking and big investments in ill-conceived capex projects -- and always end badly . <p> In 2018 , the widespread use of surplus corporate cashflows , notably in the US , to fund share buy-backs rather than off-piste development ventures , tempered the likelihood of a crash , as did the recent retracement in commodity prices and the softly-softly approach to rate rises by the Fed . The natural tendency of networks is toward concentration and the rise of Apple , Google , Facebook et al was arguably entering a worrisome phase at the beginning of 2018 . <p> Indeed , during the year , the tech sector rose to over 26 per cent of the total capitalisation of the S&amp;P 500 index . Mathematically , it follows , any major crash in US shares would require a crash in those big tech stocks . <p> As an example of how the rise of these so-called FAANGs has distorted capital allocation decisions , several " deep value " global share portfolio managers I spoke with this year went overweight in Amazon at a P/E ratio of 120 times . This was on the grounds it now had such a dominant , structural advantage in the global distribution system that these managers were not comfortable to own any other competitor . <p> While markets were shaken , the combination of leverage , misaligned remuneration and misrepresentation of risks has nearly always been present in stock market crashes . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ make the system more resilient and less vulnerable to an eventual crisis . <p> For longer-term investors , a useful early warning system might give regard to what I first articulated back in 2011 as the five " slow burners " . These are deeper socio-ecological undercurrents that are less visible on the surface but more likely to dictate our future path . <p> They are climate change , demographic change ( the declining share of the working age population ) , the geopolitical rise of China , technological disruption and the ballooning level of sovereign debt . I have had little reason to change any of them since and all five loomed into sharper relief this past year . <p> In fact , 10 years ago , I was laughed at for suggesting climate change might be the biggest driver of long-term returns on shares .
