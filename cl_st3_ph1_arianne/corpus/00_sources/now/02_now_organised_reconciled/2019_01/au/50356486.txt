<h> Hoping for a more accountable government this year ? Do n't bet on it <h> By Colleen Lewis <p> 15 January 2019 -- 11:34pm <p> With the forthcoming federal election front of mind , the major political parties are gearing up to do battle with the winner 's prize being government . Minor parties , including the Greens and Pauline Hanson 's somewhat misnamed One Nation Party , are preparing to try to win the balance of power in both Houses of Parliament but may understand they , along with other minor parties and independents , will have to settle for exercising control of the Senate . <p> Even though the election is probably some five months away , it is already being suggested that central issues will include the continued health of the economy , education and health policy , protecting the environment and addressing climate change , power prices , immigration numbers , refugee policy and acknowledging and acting on the concerns of " Aussie battlers " . <p> While all of these are important matters , there is an overarching issue that is paramount to the delivery of good government and governance that is not @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . It must be . I am referring to integrity in public life , because integrity should act as a cloak over all public policies . If , as some suggest it already does , it is in many respects an opaque cloak . <p> Accountability , openness , transparency and truthfulness are component parts of integrity and prioritising them is key to addressing the ever-widening trust deficit between Australia 's elected representatives and the electorate . <p> We often hear integrity-related platitudes from parliamentarians and political parties . They promise to act to restore faith in MPs and the political system more broadly . But their words rarely translate into meaningful action . <p> Two examples that highlight the symbolic nature of many integrity related policies and proposals have to suffice , for now . They are the National Integrity Commission model recently put forward by the Morrison government and the unacceptable lack of progress in relation to reform of the federal political donations regime . <p> I have deliberately omitted the conduct of MPs here , as Australians are well aware of the improper and offensive behaviour of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ lax rules parliamentarians too often establish in relation to their own behaviour . <p> The proposed National Integrity Commission is flawed in many respects and in some instances the document put forward for discussion by the Attorney General 's department seems to be comparing apples with oranges . But clearly the most obvious flaw relates to those who fall under the jurisdiction of the " Public Sector Division " , which encompasses non-law enforcement public servants including all MPs and their staff . They will be excluded from public hearings . Members of these two groups are the very same people who ultimately devised the flawed model . One could be forgiven for thinking that the exclusion rule is designed to protect both groups from transparency and openness . <p> Loading <p> In an effort to justify the protections they want to afford themselves , a highly debatable argument has been put forward . The claim is that those undertaking law enforcement roles are more likely to be involved in corrupt conduct so only they should be subjected to public hearings . Former parliamentarians currently serving prison sentences for corruption @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ not , to the best of my knowledge , law enforcement officers . <p> Reform of the Federal political donations regime drags on with only one significant change being enacted to address the regularly criticised regime and that is primarily in relation to foreign donations . This is despite reports , submissions , evidence from independent experts that have argued for more than 10 years that , among other things , there needs to be a cap of about $1000 on all political donations ; that real-time disclosure of donations ( days not weeks ) is essential if the electorate is to cast an informed vote , and that a ceiling must be placed on the amount of money parties and individual candidates can spend on an election campaign if the election spending " arms race " is to be controlled . <p> It is worth noting that addressing the " arms race " was one of the reasons put forward by MPs in 1984 for introducing public ( taxpayer ) funding for election campaigns . <p> It is highly likely that Australians will go to the polls with a @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to deliver the transparency , openness and accountability the electorate has made clear it wants . <p> Loading <p> Pressure to make integrity the no.1 issue in the forthcoming election needs to be exerted on all candidates and on the political parties to which the vast majority belong . However , it is highly doubtful they will respond positively unless the electorate and the media ( the Fourth Estate ) campaign tirelessly to have integrity elevated to its rightful place . <p> Every candidate and political party should be required to state precisely what actions they will put in place to demonstrate that they understand and accept unconditionally that public office is a public trust and that , if elected , they will ensure that the public interest underpins all their actions . <p> It will be telling to observe with a keen eye and to report on which candidates and political parties respond positively , with actions not mere words , to the integrity challenge . <p> Dr Colleen Lewis is an adjunct professor at the National Centre for Australian Studies , Monash University .
