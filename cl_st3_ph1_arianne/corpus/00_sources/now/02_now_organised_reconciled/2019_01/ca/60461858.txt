Under a legal doctrine known as inverse condemnation , utilities in the state must cover insurance claims for damages from fires caused by their equipment -- even if they have n't broken any safety laws . The doctrine is in force in just two states -- California and Alabama . <p> PG&amp;E had lobbied unsuccessfully for years to get relief from that liability , repeatedly warning that rising fire-related claims could cripple the company . Geisha Williams , who resigned this week as chief executive of PG&amp;E , had long complained that inverse condemnation was making power companies the default fire insurer for the entire state . <p> The main problem , the company argued , is not its equipment , but shifting climatic conditions beyond its control . <p> " Years of drought , extreme heat and 129 million dead trees have created a ' new normal ' for our state , " the company said . <p> Some of the company 's problems are no doubt self-inflicted .
