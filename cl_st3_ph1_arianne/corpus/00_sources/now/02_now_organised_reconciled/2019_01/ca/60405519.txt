<h> Increasingly common extreme weather events resulting in need for BC Hydro to adapt <p> An uprooted tree lies across a road in Vancouver 's west side neighbourhood of Kerrisdale on Dec. 20 , 2018 , after a windstorm that hit Southern British Columbia . <p> A severe windstorm that left hundreds of thousands of British Columbians without power over the holidays has left some asking whether the province is doing enough to prepare for an increase in extreme weather events due to climate change . <p> Michael Byers , who holds the Canada Research Chair in Global Politics and International Law at the University of British Columbia and whose work focuses in part on climate change , was among those on Salt Spring Island who were without power for more than a week . <p> " It certainly made me think about whether we should change our infrastructure to be more resilient , " Mr. Byers said , citing underground power lines as an example . <p> Story continues below advertisement <p> " Maybe the B.C. approach made sense 20 or 50 years ago , but does it make sense in a future of increasingly severe storms ? @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ capital investment to protect our power source against these kinds of weather events ? At its peak , some 756,000 BC Hydro customers were without power . <p> The company had 900 field workers labouring around the clock to repair infrastructure , including some brought in from Alberta and the East Coast ; it called the weather event the " most damaging " in its history . <p> BC Hydro spokeswoman Tanya Fish said the company uses underground lines in parts of the system , such as in downtown Vancouver , where overhead lines are not possible owing to space limitations . But she said there are no plans to bury lines on a wider scale .
