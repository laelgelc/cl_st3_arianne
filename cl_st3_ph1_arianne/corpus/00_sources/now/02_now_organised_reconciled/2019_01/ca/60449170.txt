<p> More than half of all wild coffee species are at risk of extinction , according to two ground-breaking papers , published Wednesday in Science Advances and Global Change Biology . The new research from the Royal Botanic Gardens , Kew , found that upwards of 60 per cent of the world 's wild coffee species are in danger of being eradicated " due to deforestation , climate change and the spread and increasing severity of fungal pathogens and pests . " <p> Wild coffee species form the foundation of the multi-billion-dollar coffee industry . As such , the health of the global coffee trade is dependent on their survival . " A figure of 60 per cent of all coffee species threatened with extinction is extremely high , especially when you compare this to a global estimate of 22 per cent for plants , " says Eimear Nic Lughadha , senior research leader in Kew 's Conservation Department and lead scientist for Kew 's Plant Assessment Unit . <p> Arabica coffee , which represents 60 per cent of the world 's coffee production ( Robusta comprises the remaining 40 per cent ) , was classified as endangered on @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Red List in July 2018 , primarily because of climate change projections , says Kew . This newest study , written by Kew and Ethiopian scientists , estimates that Arabica 's natural population will diminish by at least 50 per cent by 2088 . " Ethiopia is the home of Arabica coffee , where it grows naturally in our upland rainforests , " says Tadesse Woldermariam Gole , senior researcher for Environment , Climate Change and Coffee Forest Forum and Arabica coffee expert . " Given the importance of Arabica coffee to Ethiopia , and the world , we need to do our utmost to understand the risks facing its survival in the wild . " <p> Arabica coffee growing in Southwestern Ethiopia.Jenny Williams <p> This new work builds on more than two decades of intensive coffee research at Kew . Previous findings include a 2012 collaboration with scientists in Ethiopia , which revealed that climate change could result in Arabica-growing regions shrinking by as much as 85 per cent by 2080 . " Among the coffee species threatened with extinction are those that have potential to be used to breed and develop the coffees of the future , including those resistant to disease and capable of withstanding worsening climatic conditions , " says Aaron Davis , head of coffee research at Kew and lead author of the Science Advances paper . " The use and development of wild coffee resources could be key to the long-term sustainability of the coffee sector . Targeted action is urgently required in specific tropical countries , particularly in Africa , to protect the future of coffee . " <p> Davis , a botanist , has been working on coffee since 1997 . " The more I study coffee and other crop plants , what I really sincerely believe is that an understanding of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the crop plants themselves , " he said in a 2018 interview with the National Post . " That fundamental biological information is just so important . Our niche in the coffee research world is that we do have a deep understanding of the wild relatives , and particularly of where they grow and their climactic tolerances -- what we call the climate envelope . " <p> " We hope our findings will be used to influence the work of scientists , policy makers and coffee sector stakeholders to secure the future of coffee production -- not only for coffee lovers around the world , but also as a source of income for farming communities in some of the most impoverished places in the world , " says Davis .
