<h> Grey seals thriving again at Strangford Lough <p> Hugh Thurgate , National Trust lead ranger for Strangford Lough and the Ards Peninsula , on the dock of Strangford Lough Yacht Club <p> Published:00:01Friday 27 December 2019 <p> Share this article <p> Share this article <p> Sign Up To Our Daily Newsletter <p> Strangford Lough is an increasingly popular location for tourists and this year it saw record numbers of another type of visitor -- the grey seal . <p> The largest sea inlet anywhere in the UK or Ireland is proving the perfect nursery for the species , with 181 pups born in its waters this autumn -- the most since numbers were first counted in 1992 . <p> There were also 282 adults recorded in the lough at the peak of the breeding season in the autumn . <p> It is a remarkable turnaround for the species , given that back in the early 1990s , when the National Trust started proactively recording the population , there were only around six or seven grey seal pups born each year . <p> Hugh Thurgate , the National Trust 's lead ranger on Strangford Lough , said several factors have contributed to the growth . <p> He said the lough is benefiting from significant population increases in Scottish waters , with seals being forced to seek out new places to breed . <p> He explained that the shelter of Strangford is a strong draw for seals on the look-out for alternative spots to " haul out " on shore during the breeding and moulting seasons . <p> He said : " From a security point of view and favourable habitat , it 's perfect for them . <p> " The seals are looking for a safe and protected place to raise their young -- somewhere that provides protection from the elements and is relatively free from disturbance -- and Strangford Lough offers them that . <p> " Pups are quite vulnerable . Seals give birth on the beach and if you get stormy weather and tidal surges , high spring tides , the pups are n't capable of thermo-regulating properly when they are young and you get high pup fatalities because of weather . <p> He said : " In the early 90s , you would have been lucky to get six or seven pups . <p> " We started counting seals in 1992 . So in 93 , 94 , 95 you were getting six or seven , maybe 11 -- so very low numbers of grey seal pups . <p> " Then slowly but surely they increased and then by the mid-noughties they were into the 40s . <p> " And then it jumped quite significantly and by 2015 we got 100 pups for the first time . And then in 2017 they went past 150 -- there were 172 that year . <p> " So they went from just over 100 to 172 pretty rapidly , and this year was an all-time high at 181 . " <p> But it is not all good news for Strangford seals . While the greys are thriving , another species -- the common/harbour seal -- is struggling . <p> It used to be the more prevalent species in the lough , with an estimated population of around 850 in the mid-1980s . <p> But the common seal numbers were more than halved by a devastating distemper virus that swept through Europe around that time and the species has never really re-established itself in Strangford . <p> This year the total population was below 100 , with an average of 25-30 pups born annually . <p> Mr Thurgate counts himself as very fortunate to have the job of monitoring the Strangford seals . <p> He said : " If you like the outdoor life it 's a fantastic job and I know I 'm very lucky . <p> " If you look at my job description there 's lots of people who would like my job , but I 'll hang on to it for the time being . " <p> The grey seal baby boom is recorded in the National Trust 's review of 2019 for Northern Ireland -- a year that saw several other types of wildlife thrive in Strangford Lough and the Ards Peninsula . <p> Populations of terns , brent geese and otters also showed an increase . <p> On dry land it was another good year for the resurgent red squirrels , whose population is now up to around 120 as a result of efforts by conservationists to keep the dominate greys out of the peninsula . <p> Warm spells of weather in the early half of the year saw migrant species of butterflies set course for Northern Ireland , with significant numbers of Painted Lady butterflies arriving en masse for the first time in a decade . <p> This was mirrored by a drop in sightings of native species , such as the common white , which are struggling as a consequence of intensive land use , habitat fragmentation and climate change .
