<h> Flood-hit Venice landmark works to preserve historic features <p> St Mark 's Basilica suffered at least five million euros ( ? 4.2 million ) in damage during last month 's floods in Venice , officials said as they described efforts to preserve its historic features . <p> " Every stone is a treasure , " said Giuseppe Maneschi , technical director of the basilica 's vestry board , indicating the prized gold-leaf mosaics overhead , the inlaid stone pavement and the marble clad walls of the 923-year-old masterpiece . <p> Many are vulnerable to the infiltration of sea water during the lagoon city 's ever-higher tides . <p> Constructed atop two previous churches on a site that early Venetians believed was among the most secure in the Canal City , the basilica suffered substantial damage during November 's devastating great tides . <p> An inside view of St Mark 's Basilica in Venice ( Antonio Calanni/AP ) <p> The first , on November 12 , was the highest in 53 years , followed by two above 1.5 metres ( 4.9ft ) , a series of severe inundations never before recorded . <p> Though the highest was seven centimetres less than the famed 1966 flood of 1.94 metres , St Mark 's chief caretaker Carlo Alberto Tesserin said : " We say this was the worst . " <p> Unlike other natural disasters , like for example an earthquake that leaves images of collapsed bell towers and fallen walls , fresh damage from the Venice floods is so far not visible to the naked eye . <p> " Someone who comes to Venice to see the high water , and who goes to St Mark 's Square the next day , sees tables in the square , says , ' Hey , look , the orchestra is playing . Nothing is wrong here ' . While in reality , what is hidden is everything we have verified in these days , " said Mr Tesserin , who submitted the damage estimate earlier this month to city and national officials . <p> Peaking at 1.87 metres ( 6.14ft ) above sea level , last month 's great tide was accompanied by wind gusts of up to 120kph ( around 75mph ) that pushed the waters even higher , flooding through the windows in St Mark 's crypt of patriarchs . <p> " The disaster is inside , where we can not see . But we can monitor with new technology . " <p> Past damage , compounded over the years , is evident throughout the basilica in brittle marble benches and cladding eaten away over the years , in some places exposing the brick walls . <p> Gauze has been placed over vulnerable sections of peacock mosaics in the pavement , which also suffers under the footfall of around five million visitors a year . <p> Now , architects suspect that concrete barriers built in the 1990s to prevent water from entering the crypt from beneath the basilica were damaged by the force of last month 's floods . <p> Mr Tesserin said they believe the water flooding in from the crypt windows was actually a blessing in disguise , creating pressure that prevented the lagoon rising beneath the basilica from shattering those concrete barriers , called " vasca " , or Italian for " tub " . <p> Workers last week were removing the crypt 's marble flooring , which lies 20cm ( 8in ) below sea level , to observe whether there are indeed cracks allowing water to infiltrate . <p> It may seem crazy to a modern eye that such a precious basilica was established at Venice 's lowest point . <p> The piazza outside floods at 80cm ( around 30in ) , and water passes the narthex into the church at 88cm ( reinforced from a previous 65cm ) , floods the Zen Chapel at 1.2 metres and the baptistery goes under at 1.3 metres . <p> But Mr Tesserin said that when the third basilica was built , " it was in the position that was considered most safe " . <p> It has become vulnerable with the passage of centuries , due to the subsidence , or sinking of the land , accompanied by a sea level that has risen 12 centimetres over the last 50 years , and climate change , which has made forecasting high tides in Venice more difficult . <p> Damage can be seen on the bottom of a column of precious Aquitaine marble in the narthex . <p> The capitals are carved with images of lions and eagles , indicating they are of imperial origin and not religious , and therefore believed to have been sacked from Constantinople during the fourth Crusade , Mr Maneschi said . <p> Analysis only this year indicates that the capitals were made even more ornate by gold leaf covering and lapis lazuli inserts -- which have long disappeared . <p> The base of one of the decorative columns is badly corroded . <p> But the dark Aquitaine marble prized by ancient civilisations can no longer be found . <p> " The day it falls , we will replace it with another marble .
