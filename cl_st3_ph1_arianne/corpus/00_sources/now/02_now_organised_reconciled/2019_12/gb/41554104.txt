<p> Professor Bert Bolin was a multi-award-winning Swedish meteorologist who spent much of his life as a pioneering scientific statesman and was instrumental in developing and promoting climate research . Bolin worked tirelessly on the subject during the Sixties and Seventies . Then in 1987 he contributed substantially to the 500-page Brundtland Report that served as a warning to the world of the rise in global temperatures and helped persuade the United Nations to create the Intergovernmental Panel On Climate Change . You probably have n't heard of him . <p> You know Greta Thunberg , though . The 16-year-old Swedish activist has significantly raised worldwide awareness of the climate crisis , met with ( and chastised ) world leaders and appeared on the cover of Time magazine , which described her as a " next-generation leader " . Thanks to Thunberg , millions of young people and adults from 150 countries have taken part in Global Climate Strikes . And she has achieved all that in the space of just over a year . <p> Inspired by their fellow Swedes , Gothenberg-based Volvo has committed to doing its bit . From next year , the carmaker 's target is that no one should be killed or seriously injured in a new Volvo and intends to reduce the top speed of all its cars to 112mph . Volvo is also the first major premium car company to commit to having only hybrid or fully electric powertrains for all its vehicles and by 2025 plans to use recycled material to produce at least 25 per cent of its in-car plastics and for its global manufacturing operations to be carbon neutral . All of which are fine sentiments , but they would n't mean much if Volvo did not have a track record of acting responsibly and making cars that people actually want to drive . Fortunately , Volvo does . Like Bolin , Volvo also quietly spent decades working on what it believed in , such as developing safety systems including the first three-point seatbelt ( 1959 ) , rear-facing child seats ( 1972 ) , side-impact protection ( 1991 ) and a blind spot information system ( 2004 ) -- all of which would become industry standard on most new cars . Like Thunberg , Volvo has challenged the public 's perception and convinced people to take notice . <p> Volvo is the first major car company to commit to having only hybrid or fully electric powertrains <p> And the XC40 is the model that has taken the brand to the next level . Named European Car Of The Year in 2018 , it is a compact SUV that manages to be both clever and the epitome of Scandi cool . For a start , it looks great . Not Ferrari great , of course , but practical , muscular and sleekly understated without being generic . Its two-tone styling works well ( especially if you plump for the exterior styling kit ) , as do the 20in double-spoke wheels . It is as handsome as the daddy of off-road daddies , the Range Rover Evoque . Inside , it is even better . It feels far bigger and more refined than any compact car should and the large Sensus infotainment screen that pretty much runs the car ( Buttons ? Switches ? Do n't be a knob ) is intuitive and complemented by Apple CarPlay and a Harman Kardon surround-sound system . <p> Then there is all the stuff you ca n't see but would n't want to be without : pilot assist , city safety , run-off road protection , oncoming lane mitigation , cross-traffic alert ( with brake support ) and a 360-degree camera . What does all this stuff do ? Keeps you safe , overrides your driving errors and makes parking an absolute doddle . <p> It is n't perfect , of course . The driving experience is reliable not radical and the 34mpg figure is optimistic to say the least . Most Volvo drivers care about this stuff -- they will be delighted with the former ( and they 're right you do n't need supercar handling on the way to the recycling centre ) , but less impressed with the latter . But do n't worry : a fully electric XC40 will be here next year . When you go to buy one ( and you should ) , tell them Greta sent you ...
