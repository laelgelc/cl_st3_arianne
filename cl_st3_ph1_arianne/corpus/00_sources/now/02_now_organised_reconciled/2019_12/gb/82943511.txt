<p> The electoral map of British politics has been dramatically transformed - returning the Conservative Party with a large majority and increased share of the vote for the third election in a row . <p> While the promise to " Get Brexit Done " was at the heart of Boris Johnson 's campaign , the roots of how Britain voted can be traced back to long before the vote to Leave the EU on 23 June , 2016 . <p> In the election , the Conservatives made significant advances across the country - most notably in Leave-voting towns across the Midlands and the North of England , and in Wales too , winning seats held by Labour for generations that previously would have been thought guaranteed . <p> The Conservatives made their biggest gains in larger towns , while their vote in the UK 's major cities bucked the national trend , falling very slightly . Labour lost support everywhere but its vote collapsed most steeply in towns - a problem that some in the party have been highlighting for some time . <p> In Scotland , the SNP again proved an unstoppable electoral force , winning 48 seats not far off the heights of 2015 . The Conservatives were far behind in second place on just six seats , and Labour 's gains in 2017 were decimated leaving it with just one MP north of the border . <p> As the moment of Brexit nears , the clamour for Scottish independence will not go away any time soon . <p> Britain 's new divides <p> The election exposed the new dividing lines of British politics . Brexit has blown apart old loyalties and seen the emergence of new electoral strongholds . <p> Repeating the pattern observed in 2017 , the Conservatives made their largest gains in areas that had voted to Leave in the EU . Some of Labour 's heaviest defeats were in constituencies that had voted for Brexit by a margin of more than 60% . <p> But this election is not only a story about Brexit . The result reflects the changing nature of places and fundamental shifts in the basis of party support . <p> It was once said " class is the basis of British party politics , all else is embellishment and detail " . <p> In 2019 , the sharpest divide of all was not class , but education . The largest swings from Labour to the Conservatives were in constituencies with fewer graduates and more people with no qualifications . <p> A similar pattern is observed for age , with the largest swings from Labour to the Conservatives in constituencies with older populations . <p> This partly reflects the greater propensity for younger people to also have higher levels of education than older generations , but also generational differences in values and identities - on issues such as Brexit and climate change . <p> The long-term realignment of British politics <p> As the Labour vote has been swept away , the character of the Conservative vote has changed beyond recognition . <p> The 2019 election delivered the result that many had expected in 2017 . British politics has been remade . While 2019 was the Brexit election , the changes to our electoral landscape will reverberate for some time . <p> Those changes reflect the realignment of party support over a much longer period than just the last three years . <p> Looking back over the last five elections , since 2005 , the largest swings from Labour to the Conservatives have been in constituencies where more than 50% of people voted to Leave the EU in 2016 . <p> Labour had already started to lose its grip on those traditional heartlands well before Brexit - due to demographic changes that have seen the populations of towns getting older , while cities have become younger and more diverse . <p> Education is perhaps the foremost factor behind this realignment of British politics . <p> People with university degrees tend to be more socially liberal and likely to vote for Labour , whereas the Conservatives - once upon a time the party of the professional classes - have increasingly gained support in areas with larger numbers of voters with no qualifications . <p> Will Jennings is an election analyst for Sky News and professor of political science and public policy at the University of Southampton .
