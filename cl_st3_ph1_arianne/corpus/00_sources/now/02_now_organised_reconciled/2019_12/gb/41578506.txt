<p> The early AIDS activists wrote the book on how to stand up to -- and work with -- Big Pharma . Now cancer patients are borrowing a page from that playbook . <p> It goes beyond the red ribbons traded in for pink to raise breast cancer awareness . Whether it 's access to new medicines or involving patients in drug development , the AIDS community rewrote the rules of the pharmaceutical industry . As cancer treatments become more expensive and complex -- and as both diagnoses become chronic conditions rather than death sentences -- cancer patient groups are explicitly coordinating with HIV organizations to learn their tactics . <p> " Every time I came up with a new idea , they more or less had done that years ago , " said Jan Geissler , president of the Leukemia Patient Advocates Foundation in Switzerland and CEO of patient advocacy think tank Patvocates . <p> However , as my colleague Carmen Paun discovered in her latest article for this series , cancer patients are n't replicating the AIDS community 's success at this point . A big obstacle : Innovative cancer therapies increasingly rely on complex analyses of an individual patient 's DNA . They 're anything but one-size-fits-all . So if cancer patients can figure out how to drive those prices down , it may be the AIDS community 's turn for an organizing lesson . Personalized medicines are an increasingly popular target in the search for an HIV cure . <p> Parallel cures : There have only been two documented cases of an HIV cure -- meaning the virus is n't merely suppressed , but eliminated from the body without ongoing medication . Both people had blood cancer as well as HIV . Their treatments for cancer , which involved bone marrow transplants or a transplant of the stem cells that produce blood and immune cells , appeared to eliminate HIV in their systems . Their levels of the virus are undetectable even though they 've stopped taking antiretrovirals . It 's not a solution for most HIV patients yet , but given some of the specific mutations involved , the discoveries offer promise that genetic therapies could be part of the HIV solution . <p> CAR-T for HIV ? You may have heard about so-called CAR-T cells as the breakthrough new cancer treatment . They 're a type of immunotherapy -- training the body 's immune system to attack a cancer . So it 's intuitive that immunotherapy could cure the human immunodeficiency virus . Read more about the event here . <p> Existential questions <p> For all its influence , there 's a sense in the HIV community that its activism has become stagnant . At a roundtable I hosted in Paris last month with experts and activists , the fear that HIV is falling into political irrelevance long before the problem is actually solved was palpable . In a conversation held under Chatham House Rules to encourage candidness , we isolated three existential questions for today 's HIV leaders : <p> Prevention vs. stigma : Concepts like PrEP and U=U ( undectable = untransmissible , meaning that people who are HIV-positive but fully virally suppressed through treatment ca n't pass the virus to someone else ) are helping the HIV community to fight against stigma more than ever before . But how do you combine these messages -- not to mention the reassurance that people can live relatively normal lives on treatment -- while still promoting prevention ? " We have n't solved it , " one of the participants acknowledged . " It 's maybe more of a communications issue and maybe having that penetrate down to health care workers and their conversations with patients , empowering those people to have the information that they need to make good decisions about their own lives and sexuality and prevention that they want to take . " Still , in the age of Twitter , this nuance is getting harder and harder to convey . <p> Privacy vs. connection : For a cohort that 's been so innovative in its activism , the HIV community can be surprisingly skeptical of advancements . A vaccine would be a one-and-done preventive . That 's ideal , right ? Well , maybe not : If people are coming in for PrEP regularly , they 're probably getting tested for other sexually transmitted infections -- possibly with funding set aside for AIDS to offer services that are n't more broadly available . <p> Likewise , some experts are calling for more at-home HIV tests as a way to boost diagnoses . Given the ongoing , aforementioned stigma , and the fact that about half of cases around the EU are diagnosed late , we should embrace any tool , right ? Well , maybe not . " I was really conflicted about it , " said one of the participants , to a murmur of agreement from another . " Are people , once they have the knowledge , are they able to access services ? " the person explained . Self-testers , the person continued , might not know how to get help if they 're positive , and if they 're negative , would they believe that means " carte blanche never needing to use a condom or never needing to use PrEP ? " <p> Old vs. new : HIV activism evolved into other major movements -- one participant argued that we would n't have same-sex marriage today without the energy and advocacy that developed in the 1980s . But young people are n't picking up the baton on HIV specifically , one participant lamented . " That could be a sign of this is not the topic of the day ... it 's climate change , it 's somewhere else , " the person posited . " But it could also be that the older leaders are not letting go . So while everybody talks about it and says , ' Oh , we need to create space for young people , ' the older leaders cling on with white knuckles ... because this is their raison d ' ? tre . " <p> As the conversation worked its way back around to stigma , it was clear that while there 've been huge scientific advances in fighting the virus , our understanding of how to eliminate the discrimination and hate that go along with it has hardly advanced . HIV affects sexual minorities , migrants , poor people , drug users : " You 've got this baseline discrimination and then HIV kind of has a layer on top of it , " as one person put it . <p> This could be the new frontier for the movement : " HIV is n't the thing anymore , " said the person concerned about young leadership . " HIV becomes a metaphor for these other things , and that will bring in the new leadership . " <p> Where do you think the balance should fall on these major questions ? Send me a note .
