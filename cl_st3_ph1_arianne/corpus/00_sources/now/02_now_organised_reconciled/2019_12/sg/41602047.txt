<h> Ancient fossil plant reveals Canadian tundra was once rich forest <h> Now covered in ice and snow , Canada 's northernmost islands were rich forests 56 million years ago <p> The heady aroma of magnolia blossoms and lotus flowers might have wafted to your nostrils if you had gone for a walk 56 million years ago in the lush green forest which covered Canada 's northernmost islands . <p> Now covered in ice and snow , present-day Ellesmere and Axel Heiberg islands in Nunavut were once home to a vibrant , temperate forest , according to fossil research just published by University of Saskatchewan ( USask ) scientists . <p> REUTERS <p> " It 's very surprising how similar these ancient polar forests were to some of our modern forests . I identified fossil plants related to many modern temperate trees : birch , alder , elms--even plants belonging to the grape family . Some of the fossils are related to trees now found only in east Asia , " said paleobotanist Christopher West , a recent USask PhD graduate . <p> Clue to our future forests <p> " The presence of these forests gives us an idea about what could happen over long periods of time if our modern climate continues to warm , and also how forest ecosystems responded to greenhouse climates in the distant past , " said West , who examined more than 5,000 fossil samples to develop the only comprehensive analysis of fossil plants ever undertaken from the Canadian Arctic . <p> Christopher West hunts for fossils using a rock hammer to split apart shale on Ellesmere IslandMarkus Sudermann <p> " This research is the cumulative effort of almost 40 years of work on fossil plants of the Canadian North undertaken by me and my students , including 20 field seasons on Ellesmere and Axel Heiberg islands , " said USask geologist Jim Basinger , who co-supervised the work with David Greenwood , USask adjunct professor of geology and Brandon University biology professor . <p> The results published today in the highly respected journal Palaeontographica B include identifications and detailed descriptions of 83 types of plants from high arctic latitudes in Canada during the early Eocene epoch , around 56 million years ago . <p> 56 million years ago <p> " We wo n't see a return to a forested polar region in our lifetimes , but it is important to remember that we as humans have become agents of climate change , and that our warming climate will have potentially dramatic effects on our modern ecosystems , " said West . <p> Christopher West in the midst of 4,000 fossils from the USask fossil collectionDaniel Hallen/USask <p> While Earth was considerably warmer during the early Eocene , the continents were mostly situated where they are now , and northern latitudes would have had lengthy periods of darkness . Despite the nearly total lack of light , the forests persisted , likely because of just how warm it was . <p> Responding to global warming <p> " If we are able to understand how ecosystems long ago responded to global warming , we may be able to better predict how our own modern ecosystems will respond to our own rapidly warming climate , " said West . " This research will also help climate modelers as they use data from the past to better understand our own climate . " <p> The next steps in the research are to examine fossils from Axel Heiberg Island from a slightly younger vintage -- roughly 45 million years old -- to understand better the ancient impacts of that climate change .
