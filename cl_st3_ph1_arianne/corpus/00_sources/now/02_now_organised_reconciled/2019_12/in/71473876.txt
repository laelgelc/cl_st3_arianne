<h> Climate Emergency CoP 25 : The Paris Agreement is tilting into a downward spiral <h> Last Updated : Wednesday 11 December 2019 <p> Photo : Getty Images <p> Two significant developments at Madrid on December 9 , 2019 , indicated that countries are willing to sacrifice the Paris Agreement if it does not meet their aims . <p> The day was about transitioning issues between technical negotiators and political representatives . The technical bodies -- known as the Subsidiary Bodies -- have to formally close their deliberations , and hand over text . The political bodies -- the Conference of the Parties chief among them -- have to formally accept the text and open their own deliberations . <p> The closing plenaries of the Subsidiary Bodies went on well past midnight . Progress on sensitive items like markets and loss and damage was presented , some disappointment was expressed , but text was eventually handed over , allowing deliberations to continue . <p> Towards the end of the plenaries , the issue of ' transparency ' came up -- a term referring to how countries will report their national emissions . On the face of it , this is a discussion about form rather than substance -- a common shorthand to refer to this item is ' Common Tabular Formats ' ( CTF ) . <p> Form is quite critical in climate policy , where even the choice of unit can mask huge gaps in effort . Nevertheless , the sense on this discussion was that it would proceed relatively smoothly -- largely technical recommendations , which would be handed over to political negotiators with minimum fuss . <p> China had other plans . It objected to continuing deliberations , agreeing that this was an important item , but arguing that it was taking focus away from more critical items that political negotiators needed to deal with . Many developing countries , including India , expressed support for this position . <p> China took the extra precaution of asking the chair to affirm that this item would not be on the agenda for the second week . After an extended back and forth , the chair conceded that , procedurally , the item had to be postponed to next year . <p> While it referred generally to other agenda items , China 's key grouse is the issue of " Long-term Finance " . This is the question of how to review progress on the Paris promise of $100 billion per year in finance by 2020 to developing countries , and how to set a new long-term finance goal for the post-2020 period which goes beyond $100 billion . <p> Long-term finance discussions have been frustrated by the US ' and other developed countries ' insistence that there is no need to review efforts towards the $100 billion and no need to set a new post-2020 target . There has also been general kvetching about " duplication of efforts " , based on the fact that there are existing rules on financial reporting . <p> Reporting rules exist , but they do not specify a financial target , which is why the discussion on long-term finance has taken on such significance . The fact that it is seen as significant enough to block discussions on transparency is hence shocking , but not surprising . <p> The linking of these issues sends a deeper message about the Paris Agreement . A big achievement which developed countries took back to their domestic audiences was that all countries would now play by the ' same ' rules -- all countries needed to submit nationally determined contributions ( national targets ) and all countries need to submit periodic reports on their emissions . <p> This missed a lot of nuance -- there are provisions for differentiated reporting rules for developed and developing countries littered through the Agreement -- but it was fundamentally true . What developed countries failed to emphasise to their people was that this result was only achieved as a result of some very specific promises on finance . In one sense , this finance commitment is equity 's last stand . <p> The retaliatory blocking of discussions on transparency -- which is rooted in Article 13 of the Paris Agreement -- signals a willingness to hack away at some commitments in the Agreement if delivery on other commitments is not up to scratch . If this tactic becomes a norm , and the CoP is prevented from taking decisions which flesh out the Paris Agreement 's broad commitments , the Agreement is as good as dead . <p> The procedural theatre was accompanied by some ominous signs from high-level representatives . India 's Union Minister for Environment , Forest and Climate Change is on record in Madrid as saying that it is " futile to talk about new targets right now , futile to talk about new ambitions or new programmes , unless we are able to implement our current targets under the Paris Agreement " , and that he would like developed countries to meet their targets under the Kyoto Protocol . <p> Coming on the eve of 2020 -- the year that countries have to submit an upgraded second round of targets under the Paris agreement -- this statement is a dampener . It is perfectly fair to contrast the non-performance by developed countries -- on Kyoto-era targets , as well as the first round of targets under the Paris Agreement -- against India 's relatively positive track record since Paris . <p> But there was a possibility of framing this point as a rallying cry for ambition -- that India stands prepared to upgrade its own ambition , if other countries ' ambition exceed some minimum thresholds . The choice was made to frame it in different terms -- terms that reject the possibility of future ambition making up for past non-performance . <p> Signs like these point to an increasing fatigue with the Agreement . Consensus on difficult items like markets and loss and damage could turn the narrative around , but that will require political representatives working -- in one delegate 's words -- some " magic " . <p> We are a voice to you ; you have been a support to us .
