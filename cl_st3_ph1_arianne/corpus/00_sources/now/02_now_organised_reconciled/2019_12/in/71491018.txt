<h> Change in spring arrival of migratory birds due to climate change <p> Migratory birds are adapting to climate change , say researchers after analysing 24 years of radar data from the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration ( NOAA ) . <p> The researchers , including those from the University of Massachusetts Amherst in the US , said atmospheric temperature and migration timing were closely aligned , with the greatest changes to the latter occurring in regions warming most rapidly . <p> Due to global warming , birds migrating during spring are likely to pass certain stops earlier now than they would have 20 years ago , says a first-of-its-kind study examining the impact of climate change on the timing of avian migration . <p> The findings of the study , published in the journal Nature Climate Change , revealed that timing shifts were less apparent in fall , than during spring . <p> According to the researchers , the nighttime migratory behaviours of hundreds of species -- representing billions of birds -- is " critically important " to understand the shifting migration patterns . <p> " To see changes in timing at continental scales is truly impressive , especially considering the diversity of behaviours and strategies used by the many species the radars capture , " said study co-author Kyle Horton from Colorado State University ( CSU ) in the US . <p> Horton added that the observed shifts do not necessarily mean that migrants are keeping pace with climate change . <p> The study 's senior author Andrew Farnsworth from Cornell University said bird migration evolved largely as a response to changing climate . <p> " It 's a global phenomenon involving billions of birds annually . And it 's not a surprise that birds ' movements track changing climates . But how assemblages of bird populations respond in an era of such rapid and extreme changes in climate has been a black box , " he said . <p> According to Farnsworth , capturing scales and magnitudes of migration in space and time had been impossible until recently . <p> The researchers said the team 's ability to arrive at its findings was possible thanks to its access to radar data , and its use of cloud computing . <p> " To process all of these data , without cloud computing , it would have taken over a year of continuous computing . Instead , the team crunched the numbers in about 48 hours , " Horton noted . <p> The bird flight records used in the study were acquired over decades of constantly scanning weather radars by the US National Weather Services ' network . <p> The data had been mostly out of reach for bird researchers , because of the sheer magnitude of information , and the lack of tools to analyse it . <p> The researchers finally overcame this barrier with a new machine learning ( ML ) tool called " MistNet " which they developed to extract bird data from the radar record . MistNet , whose name refers to the fine , almost invisible , " mist nets " that ornithologists use to capture migratory songbirds , also allows researchers to estimate flying velocity and traffic rates of migrating birds . <p> According to the study , MistNet uses computer vision techniques to differentiate birds from rain on the radar images -- a major hurdle that had challenged biologists for decades . <p> " Historically , a person had to look at each radar image to determine whether it contained rain or birds . We developed ' MistNet , ' an artificial intelligence system to detect patterns in radar images and remove rain automatically , " explained study co-author Dan Sheldon from the University of Massachusetts Amherst , who developed the tool . <p> The researchers said they were surprised by the lack of change in fall migration patterns , though it tended to be a " little bit messier " during these months . <p> " In the spring , we see bursts of migrants , moving at a fairly rapid pace , ultimately to reach the breeding grounds , " Horton explained . <p> " However , during the fall , there 's not as much pressure to reach the wintering grounds , and migration tends to move at a slower , more punctuated pace , " he said .
