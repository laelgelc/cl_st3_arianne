<h> David I Gustafson : Carbon atoms are forever : And in the form of soil organic matter , might be Mother Earth 's best friend . <p> Carbon . It 's all around us and in us . We are what we eat , and our food is full of carbon , even when it does n't contain " carbs . " Although our bodies are about half water , carbon makes up nearly 20% of our mass , and it 's an essential element in all organic matter . <p> Some inorganic forms of carbon of increasing purity include coal , gasoline and , finally , graphite and diamonds ( both essentially 100% carbon ) -- all with vastly different utility but each highly valued . <p> However , the most valuable form of carbon may ultimately prove to be the form that sits in cropland soils around the world . For it is here that farmer choices can result in " carbon-negative farming , " a concept that might be powerful enough to remove all excess carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and thereby avoid the worst that climate change has to offer . <p> Understanding this requires a bit of knowledge about the carbon cycle , how carbon atoms are transformed as they circulate our world , making all life possible . Through photosynthesis , plants absorb the carbon dioxide we exhale -- at a scale sufficient to pull more than 100 gigatons of carbon out of the air each year , although most of that carbon currently cycles back to the air . <p> Atmospheric gigatons is now at 410 parts per million , or just 0.04% . But that 's higher than it 's been for at least a million years and has the same warming effect as moving the earth a million miles closer to the sun . This excess carbon ( around 850 gigatons ) is the fraction remaining airborne after we started burning carbon-based fuels that had been stored over many millions of years -- representing an unprecedented disruption of the carbon cycle . <p> But that 's where soil carbon could become our saving grace . We 've been tilling soil ever since we were banished from the Garden of Eden , but now we 're overdoing it . Currently , much unprotected cropland soil is lost by wind and water erosion , particularly when a field has been extensively tilled . This takes soil carbon with it , as does tillage-accelerated surface degradation of the unharvested portions of our crops ( roots , stems , leaves , etc. ) -- organic matter that might have added to the soil carbon bank . <p> The net effect of all that tillage is that around half the North American cropland soil carbon bank has been depleted . But the good news is that practical and cost-effective farming techniques can reverse those losses and put more carbon into the soil , where it can do much good . <p> I recently attended the Sustainable Agriculture Summit in Indianapolis , along with nearly 700 experts from leading agri-food companies and other organizations concerned with environmental and socioeconomic outcomes throughout these supply chains . One speaker after another spoke about the need for regenerative farming and circular supply chains -- working to ensure that soils at harvest time are not further degraded but actually enhanced for the next season . <p> This has become an increasingly popular message in farm country because soils rich in carbon are more valuable to farmers . They sustain higher yields and bring many other desirable physical characteristics , including the capacity to store more soil moisture for when it is needed later in the season . Accordingly , agricultural land values are higher when soil organic matter is higher . <p> Ecosystem services markets are now developing to further add to the value of soil carbon , by directly compensating farmers for actions that sequester more carbon -- based on its future value to all of us . We now know that planting winter cover crops and adopting reduced-tillage systems can replenish the soil carbon bank without busting farmer budgets . <p> Our planet was endowed with a fixed number of carbon atoms . As diamonds , they may sparkle forever , but only an infinitesimal fraction is in that form . Most is in the soil beneath our feet . Soil carbon is the real " diamond in the rough . " Let 's appraise it for its true value -- for its unique power to sustain future life on Earth . <p> David I. Gustafson , Ph.D. , is an independent scientist who uses modeling to help food systems meet human nutrition needs in more sustainable ways . He is founder of the St. Louis Reconciliation Network , which is working to heal regional race relations .
