<p> Canadians like to think of ourselves as global good guys : inventors of UN peacekeeping , supporters of multilateralism , human rights and the rule of law . Our flirtation with being global climate pariahs ended when we acceded to the Paris Climate Agreement in 2015 . We welcome refugees fleeing persecution . We 're the ninth-largest donor country in net U.S. dollars and the eighth-largest donor of programs promoting or integrating gender equality , according to the OECD . So when we decide to campaign for one of the non-permanent United Nations Security Council seats with a two-year term , we expect to succeed . Last week 's throne speech repeated that belief . <p> Unfortunately , our competition for the two available " Western European and Other " Security Council seats , Ireland and Norway , are extremely popular UN members with impressive credentials of their own . Ireland , despite its much smaller population and resources , has supported UN peacekeeping continuously and consistently since 1958 , while Canada gradually slipped to near oblivion on the peacekeeping front -- until our Mali mission that ended last year despite UN pleas to extend our commitment . <p> When it comes to development assistance , Norway puts us to shame , contributing more than three times what Canada contributes as a percentage of gross domestic product . Norway also has an impressive commitment to peace initiatives , most notably as the architect of the Oslo Accords between Israel and the PLO . Both Ireland and Norway carry less international " baggage " than Canada does as a close ally of the United States , and both have many friends in Europe and the developing world sure to support their candidacies . <p> Perhaps , given this state of affairs , we should withdraw our candidacy ? The Security Council itself has been much maligned in recent years , with its anachronistic , post-war permanent membership of five veto-wielding states : the U.S. , Russia , China , France and the United Kingdom . Instead of contributing to global peace and security , the stalemate between the five permanent members has contributed directly to the prolonged war in Syria , among others , and to the steady erosion of the rule of law and human rights globally . So why should Canada even wish to be part of that imperfect club ? <p> When it comes to development assistance , Norway puts us to shame , contributing more than three times what Canada contributes as a percentage of gross domestic product . <p> Simply put , because the UN Security Council , however flawed , is the equivalent of a global board of directors on peace and security issues . It 's akin to owning shares in only one company and either having some say in how that company is directed , via its board , or having to trust entirely in others to lead it in the direction you wish . We 'd rather be present in the room where the most important decisions are made , even if only temporarily . <p> There is one approach that could remove the current uncertainty surrounding our Security Council bid ( and a potential repeat of the embarrassing loss like the one the Harper government suffered to Portugal in 2010 ) . We could negotiate a shared two-year term with Norway or Ireland -- as Italy and the Netherlands have done recently -- or even propose a first-ever three-way sharing of the two-year term between our like-minded , multilateral institution-supporting countries . <p> Such a seat-sharing arrangement could include some common objectives relating to combatting climate change , and new , enhanced , concrete commitments to promoting human rights and gender equality , for example . <p> If Canada wants to be seen as a significant player on the international stage , a place on the Security Council in such an innovative shared arrangement with Norway and Ireland might even do more to enhance the impression that Canada is truly back on the world stage than would an outright victory for a Security Council term . <p> Some might even be tempted to call such a compromise deal quintessentially Canadian . <p> Louis Gentile is a former Canadian and United Nations diplomat from Toronto with more than 30 years of experience on four continents .
