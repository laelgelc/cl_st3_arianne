<p> When her husband , Bill , died six years ago , Michele Zawadzki squared her shoulders to the grief . <p> They had been together for 47 years -- since high school , when they were prom dates -- so she knew that life without him would be trying . She worried about taking care of the car . When a pipe broke in her toilet , spraying water all over , Zawadzki , 68 , did n't know what valve to turn off or whom to call . Mail for him kept coming to their suburban Chicago home . <p> What she did n't expect , though , was how difficult it would be to turn on her stove . Or how it would feel to walk supermarket aisles , past the foods he loved . <p> In the checkout line , she 'd watch the clerk scan produce she knew would rot and bread she knew would go stale ; she was still shopping for two , but eating for one . When her freezer filled with excess food , she started throwing out meals -- throwing out his portion , really , because he was n't there to eat it . <p> The connection between food and mourning runs deep : In almost every culture , people bring dishes to the survivors in the weeks or months after a death . But for a spouse , accustomed to sharing every meal with a partner , the grieving can go on long afterward , renewed constantly by the rhythms of shopping , cooking and eating . <p> " It 's almost like the sixth stage of grief is cooking alone , " said Jill Cohen , a grief counselor in New York , referring to the theory of the Five Stages of Grief . She said many of her patients bring up eating issues in therapy . <p> A 2016 study focused on the links between eating and widowhood . ( Bereavement counselors now use " widow " as a gender-neutral term , like actor or waiter . ) Grocery shopping and preparing meals alone could be painful and overwhelming , the study found , and could often lead widows to skip meals or eat in unhealthy ways . <p> " Cooking and mealtimes are some of the most overlooked aspects of grief , " said Heather Nickrand , lead author of the study . " How many people are actually asked : ' How is the cooking or grocery shopping going ? Are you eating OK ? ' " <p> In response , she founded Chicago-based Culinary Grief Therapy , which uses demonstrations and group discussions over meals to teach participants how to cook , eat and shop for one . She runs training sessions and attends conferences , helping other bereavement groups develop their own versions of the program . <p> Zawadzki regularly attends the gatherings , which are held in a large commercial kitchen where the participants learn straightforward recipes with minimal ingredients . <p> " In the beginning , I just did n't want to cook . I 'd make a bowl of cereal , " said Diane Kantak , 78 , who shared a cooking station with Zawadzki at a recent session . Kantak had been married for 54 years when her husband , Francis , died in 2013 . <p> The two women chatted as they chopped . Zawadzki helped an older woman near her station pry the lid off a jar , and ducked cheerfully as someone passed a colander over her head . When the group sat down to eat , many spoke about how hard it could be merely to plan a menu . <p> For partners who were n't the main cook , especially older men , widowhood poses a new set of challenges . Many know only a few recipes . <p> " I 've still got her spices in the cabinet , " said Johnnie Footman , who is in his 70s , at a recent meeting of a bereavement group for men in New York City . " I leave them there as a memento , even though I do n't use them . " <p> This meeting specifically dealt with food issues , but the men have been gathering for years , sharing the details of their lives . <p> Sitting around the table together , talking about what happened during the day : This is what many widows say they miss the most . <p> Some do n't even eat their meals at a table anymore , opting to eat on the couch or at restaurants . Without a spouse sitting opposite , the kitchen table can feel unbalanced , a seesaw for one . <p> " That has to be relearned , " said R. Benyamin Cirlin , executive director of the Center for Loss &amp; Renewal , a bereavement practice in New York . Eating solo " is really a sign of one 's changed identity . " <p> That sentiment was seconded by Deborah Stephens , 64 , of Irmo , S.C. , whose husband , David , died nearly two years ago . " I am trying to find out who I am , " she said . <p> Like many widows , she lost the taste for food . For months , Stephens would go entire days on a cup of coffee in the morning and a cheese stick in the afternoon . " Food was the last thing that I wanted , " she said . <p> Hard to let go <p> For Jeanne Heifetz , the New York home she shared with her husband , writer Juris Jurjevics , is the place where he is most present -- and most absent . Jurjevics died unexpectedly last year at 75 . Since then , Heifetz , 59 , has been struggling to adjust . <p> In her refrigerator , a large hunk of Latvian bread , which Jurjevics ' sister brought them a few days before he died , still sits unfinished . <p> There is also a bag of grapes , which he was halfway through eating ; they 're probably raisins by now , she said , but she ca n't bring herself to throw them out . <p> To help widows through their grief and sense of identity loss , Nickrand , founder of Culinary Grief Therapy , hands out a cheat sheet , now released as a book , to class participants to help them cook again . <p> One suggestion is " Change routines : Consider having your meals at a different time of the day , in a different room , or serving foods you typically did not have . " <p> Food will always be a part of Zawadzki 's relationship with her late husband . Every year , on the anniversary of their first date , she goes to the same little restaurant they went to when she was 15 . She orders a chocolate ice cream soda and a turkey BLT , which they ate on that day more than 50 years ago , and sits there , thinking about him . <p> " He 's probably looking down saying , ' Really , Michele ? Really ? ' " she said , laughing . " But it works for me . I 'm holding onto those memories , and I 'm finally able to laugh with him again .
