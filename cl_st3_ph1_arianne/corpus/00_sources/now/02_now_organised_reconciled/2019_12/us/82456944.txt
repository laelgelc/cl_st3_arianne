<h> MIT Engineers Develop A Battery That Absorbs Carbon Dioxide <p> Researchers have developed a new battery that absorbs carbon dioxide while charging . The device is cheaper and consumes lesser energy compared to other methods of carbon absorption . <p> Methods to absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere help reduce carbon emissions and control climate change . Most methods can only absorb carbon dioxide released in high concentrations from power plants and factories . But the new battery can also work in low concentrations removing the gas from surrounding air . <p> The battery can trap carbon dioxide from the air that passes over the surface of its electrodes while it is charging . While discharging the battery gradually releases the concentrated carbon dioxide . The electrodes of the battery are coated with polyanthraquinone -- an organic compound that has been composited with carbon nanotubes . <p> The battery does not require intermediate chemical processing steps operating at room temperature and pressure . The electrodes have a binary nature of affinity for carbon dioxide -- it can be high or low depending on whether it is charging or discharging . <p> The author of the paper published in Energy and Environmental Science , Sahag Voskian explained : " This binary affinity allows capture of carbon dioxide from any concentration , including 400 parts per million , and allows its release into any carrier stream , including 100 percent CO2 . " <p> The researchers have found that the battery can last for at least 7,000 charging-discharging cycles and incur a 30% loss in efficiency over its life . The researchers think it is possible to increase the life by 20,000 to 50,000 cycles . <p> The researchers explain that the concentrated carbon dioxide that is released during discharge can be used for carbonation of beverages or in greenhouses . It can also be converted to fuel using electrochemical processes or just disposed off . <p> T. Alan Hatton said , " This carbon dioxide capture technology is a clear demonstration of the power of electrochemical approaches that require only small swings in voltage to drive the separations . "
