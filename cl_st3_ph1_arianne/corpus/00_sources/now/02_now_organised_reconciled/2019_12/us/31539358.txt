<h> Copy/paste the article video embed link below : <p> On a Baltic Sea island once used by the Nazis for biological weapon research , German scientists are developing virus vaccines that could save lives . <p> Rabies , African swine fever , Crimean-Congo virus , Ebola ... the list of pathogens is long . What makes it special : The Federal Institute for Animal Health tests the world 's most dangerous viruses on large animals . <p> Access to the island of Riems is highly restricted . <p> Scientists who work there have to have disinfectant showers when entering or exiting and wear bodysuits . <p> And dozens of animals , including sheep and cows , have been deliberately infected with viruses as part of the research to monitor the effects of the diseases . <p> " We really are the Alcatraz of viruses , a kind of prison for viruses , " Franz Conraths , deputy head of the island 's Friedrich Loeffler Institute , said . <p> Located south of picturesque Ruegen Island , Riems has become a global centre for the study of pathogens such as rabies , African swine fever and Ebola . <p> Formerly exotic infections are coming closer and closer due to climate change and globalisation . Animal diseases are spreading across borders . <p> Visitors have to undergo security controls and the laboratories holding the most dangerous viruses as well as the stables with the infected animals are on security level 4 - the highest in Germany . <p> " We do everything possible to ensure they do not spread , " Conraths said of the viruses on his island . <p> " It 's very important for our work . " <h> Preparing for an outbreak <p> The Friedrich Loeffler Institute is the oldest centre for the study of viruses in the world . It was founded in 1910 by Loeffler , a pioneering German scientist . <p> There is no installation like it in Europe , although there are similar facilities in Australia and Canada . <p> The institute used to be based in a single building on the island but has now expanded and covers almost the entirety of the 1.3-kilometre long outcrop . <p> After World War II , the centre found itself in communist East Germany and began to develop vaccines . <p> In the 1970s , it was linked to the mainland by a dam . <p> The government has invested some 300 million euros in the institute since 2008 to upgrade the infrastructure and there are now 89 laboratories and 163 stables . <p> " Our biggest task is working out preparations for an outbreak , " said Conraths , giving the example of African swine fever which has been detected in Central Europe , raising concerns among German pig farmers . <p> " We have to expect it any day , " he said . <p> There are between 80 and 100 large animals on the island : alpacas , boars , cows , goats , and sheep . <p> The researchers said that when the animals get too sick they are put down so as not to let them suffer . <p> " We do our best to carry out our research without having to do tests on animals , " said Martin Beer , head of the institute 's diagnostic department . <p> But he added that " only by infecting an animal " could researchers work out why animals get sick , how the illness develops and how the animals react . <p> Since the tests are for vaccines that can save millions of animals , protect farmers ' livelihoods and alleviate hunger , Beer said they were " justified " . <p> From time to time , Riems becomes a little less dangerous : if an animal disease such as rinderpest is considered eradicated , then , according to an agreement , research institutions around the world destroy the pathogens - including the Friedrich-Loeffler Institute . <p> Euronews is no longer accessible on Internet Explorer .
