<p> When we flip the light switch , we expect the light to come on . And , when we pick up our smartphones , we expect to be able to connect to the Internet , watch cat videos , and even research complex subjects , all without plugging in to anything . <p> For years , the Nobel Committee and John Goodenough , currently a University of Texas professor , did n't appreciate the impact his lithium-ion research would have on the world . But that changed this year when the Nobel Committee honored Goodenough with a Nobel Prize in Chemistry for refining a commercially viable lithium-ion battery in the 1970s with fellow prize co-recipients Akira Yoshino and M. Stanley Whittingham . <p> The breakthrough untethered us from our desks and made the entire world more productive , and for that accomplishment , Goodenough , 97 , is a finalist for 2019 Dallas Morning News Texan of the Year . <p> Goodenough grew up near New Haven , Conn. , not far from Yale University where his father , a respected scholar on the history of religion , was noted for a 12-volume work , Jewish Symbols in the Greco-Roman Period . It was a far cry from the path of science that his son would take en route to the Nobel Prize . <p> As a youngster , Goodenough struggled with dyslexia , a learning and reading disorder that involves difficulty in identifying speech sounds and how those relate to letters and words . He overcame that challenge to graduate summa cum laude in mathematics from Yale . <p> He later studied at the University of Chicago in the 1950s where famed nuclear physicist Enrico Fermi stunned him with a 32-hour exam -- eight hours a day for four days -- to qualify for the Ph.D . program . He passed the test on his second try , and instead of pursuing nuclear physics , he pursued solid state physics . He would go on to a stint at MIT 's Lincoln Laboratory and eventually to his revolutionary lithium-ion research with Whittingham at Oxford years later . <p> Researchers had long struggled to marry mobility and powerful storage batteries . Several battery technologies could store power , but most were n't small enough , powerful enough or rechargeable to be practical for many of the tasks we routinely take for granted today . Lead-acid technology was fine for bulky car batteries , and non-rechargeable zinc-carbon and nickel-cadmium had been standard battery technologies for consumer electronics . Each technology had significant and obvious limitations . <p> At Oxford , Goodenough noticed that his work and Whittingham 's research in battery cathodes could produce a more powerful battery . He was right , and after more work , the end result was what the Nobel Committee aptly described as a " decisive step towards the wireless revolution " of laptops , smartphones and other mobile devices . <p> So what does Goodenough , the oldest recipient of the Nobel Prize , do for an encore in the twilight of an amazing career ? What he has always done -- think big thoughts . In an interview with Quartz , he said he is interested in putting the world on a path toward creating better and more powerful batteries that can become alternatives to the global dependence on fossil fuels that he sees as the catalyst for wars and a contributor to climate change . <p> Goodenough 's intense devotion to curiosity propelled him toward a monumental breakthrough that defied even his imagination .
