" <h> Epic dust storms <p> Phoenix was in the grips of a dry spell in early July 2011 . On July 5 , there had been strong thunderstorms with winds and lightning but very little rain . Storms in eastern and southern Arizona began to die out and outflow winds from those decaying storms collided and pushed up massive walls of dust in the desert south of Phoenix . <p> The National Weather Service estimated that the dust wall was about a mile high and close to 100 miles wide . It was moving at close to 50 mph . <p> Drivers coming into the area from the south said it felt as if the haboob was chasing them . The dust storm grounded flights at Sky Harbor Airport for about an hour . Some planes were diverted to other airports . When it was still raining by daybreak , it was clear something entirely different was happening . <p> There was widespread flooding . Some neighborhoods in Mesa and south Phoenix were especially hard hit -- water breached the thresholds of some houses . Floodwaters left some motorists stranded as the rain collected in free underpasses and turned normally passable washes into torrents . <p> The rain occurred before Sept. 30 , so it counted toward the monsoon season total . That made the 2014 monsoon the seventh wettest on record with 6.34 inches of rain . <h> Heat season is getting longer <p> The latest 100-degree day in Phoenix was Oct. 27 , 2016 . That broke the record of 100 degrees on Oct. 23 , 2003 . <p> Those late-season triple-digit highs are part of a trend : The heat season is getting longer , according to research compiled by the National Weather Service in Phoenix . In a 2016 interview , meteorologist Paul I ? iguez explained the research that showed those temperatures are occurring earlier and lasting longer than when the weather service first started keeping records . <p> For example , the last 85-degree day of the year in Phoenix is occurring four days later than it did a century ago . That date is usually sometime in early November . Meanwhile , the last 110-degree day is showing up 31 days later than a century ago , going from late July to the beginning of September . <p> " It 's not people who have been here a long time 's imagination , " I ? iguez said . " It does get warmer earlier and it does stay warmer later . That 's just how it is . " <p> The main reasons are global warming , regional warming in the Southwest and localized warming ( the urban heat-island effect ) in the Phoenix area , I ? iguez said . <h> Summer of July 2017 was hot <p> Remember the summer of 2017 when Phoenix made national news with a week of high temperatures that put a temporary halt to some flights out of Sky Harbor , saw folks try to fry eggs on the sidewalks and prompted businesses to put wrappers on door handles so people would n't burn themselves ? <p> Temperatures did n't reach the all-time record of 122 degrees set in 1990 -- they did n't even top 120 degrees -- but we did get a high of 119 degrees on June 20 , which tied for the fourth hottest day on record . Three high-temperature records fell in Phoenix that week when you factor in the 118 degrees on June 19 and 117 degrees on June 21 . <p> High-temp records also were set on July 7 ( 118 degrees ) and Sept. 12 ( 110 degrees ) . Ten records for warmest low temperatures also were set that summer . <p> It 's no wonder that 2017 topped the list of warmest years for Phoenix . <h> Drought : It was really dry in 2018 <p> When it comes to drought , two main measurements are important here . The high for that day was only 47 degrees , breaking the record of 54 degrees set in 1897 . <p> In fact , winter 2019 wound up being one of the coldest on record for Phoenix . The coldest winter during that period -- 1997-98 -- had an average temperature of 55.9 degrees . <p> Another unusual occurrence for winter 2019 was the appearance of snow in town .
