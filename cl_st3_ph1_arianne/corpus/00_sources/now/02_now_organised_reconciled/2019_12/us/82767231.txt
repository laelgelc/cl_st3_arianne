<h> The man who lived <p> Warning : This story contains graphic images and descriptions that some readers may find disturbing . <h> I <p> Angel Perez could n't stand the smell . It was fetid , rotten . <p> And it was coming from him . <p> It was July 2018 , and Perez lay helpless in his hospital bed at Cooper University Hospital in Camden . Tubes and wires were draped across his chest , as machines and monitors provided a ceaseless chorus of beeps and hums . <p> His arms and legs had turned gangrenous , a side effect of the drugs keeping him alive after deadly bacteria seeped into his flesh two weeks earlier . The Millville man had been wading in a tributary of the Maurice River near the Delaware Bay when a scratch on his ankle allowed the pathogen inside . <p> " He 's going to die , " said one doctor , hovering over Perez in his hospital bed . <p> Perez heard the comment , but was too weak to respond or protest or beg for an explanation . <p> Angel in his hospital bed at Cooper University Hospital in Camden . His hands and feet were rotting as his body fought a bacterial infection . <p> What was happening was a perfect storm of medical crises : The infection he contracted -- from Vibrio vulnificus , sometimes termed " flesh-eating bacteria " -- had sent him into septic shock , leading to kidney failure . His other organs were also at risk of shutting down . <p> Yet the potent drugs doctors were using to keep his organs alive were simultaneously killing his limbs , starving them of blood supply , causing them to wither and blacken and decompose . <p> Perez would frequently stare at his blackened hands in horror , compulsively peeling off chunks of flaky skin like paint chips off a wall . <p> " Do n't let them take my hands ! " he cried out to his daughter . <p> Doctors were baffled . Infections from V. vulnificus are among the rarest a person can contract . Before 2017 , the hospital had n't seen a case in nearly a decade . But that year brought three cases to Cooper , and Perez would be the first of two there in 2018 . All but one patient contracted the bacterial infection from the same waters of the Delaware Bay , where V. vulnificus would n't be expected to thrive . <p> Dr. Katherine Doktor , an infectious disease specialist on the team treating Perez , remembered her reaction as , " Oh wow , another case . " For Doktor and her colleagues , Perez 's arrival sparked concern of a surge in cases , something seemingly unheard of so far north along the East Coast . Were warming waters caused by climate change creating new opportunities for a deadly pathogen ? <p> For Perez 's wife , daughter and extended family , the confusion was compounded : How could an ordinary day spent crabbing go so horribly wrong ? How could the waters they had visited for years incubate something now killing the man they loved ? <p> " There was so many days when I walked in , and I had nurses coming up to me , telling me , ' Just put him on morphine . Just let him go . ' Doctors telling me , ' Just let him go , ' " recalled Perez 's daughter , Dilena Perez-Dilan . <p> But Angel Perez was n't about to go down without a fight . <h> II <p> Journey down Route 55 out of Vineland , all the way to the southern edge of New Jersey in Cumberland County , and eventually , you 'll come to Matts Landing in Maurice River Township . An idyllic sprawl of pristine and undisturbed marshlands and tributaries , it 's here where the fresh water of the Maurice River ( pronounced Morris , like the county ) meets the salty water of the Delaware Bay . Commercial fishing vessels dock at the few nearby marinas , a small marker of the bustling history of these waters , once known as the oyster capital of the world . <p> Matts Landing in Maurice River Township runs along the marshlands and tributaries of the Maurice River , which feeds into Delaware Bay . It was near this idyllic spot that Angel contracted the bacterial infection that threatened his life . <p> Angel Perez arrived here with his family just after dawn on July 2 , 2018 , the temperature already on its way into the upper 80s . The group -- which included his wife , Haydee , and her cousin and aunt -- set up beside the water and unpacked its gear : crab nets , traps and buckets . Angel tossed in a line as he waded shin-deep into the waters . <p> For Angel , a short , stocky man with the rugged , round face and square jaw of a boxer , this was more than a mere pastime . It was a way for him to recall better days , before his diagnosis of Parkinson 's disease , when his body was entirely his own . Born in the small , mountainous town of Barranquitas , Puerto Rico , he always had been a tactile person , good with his hands . As a kid , he became fascinated with cars and engines and soon began fixing them alongside his neighbor . His ambition was to run his own repair shop when he got older . <p> " That was my fantasy , " he recalled decades later . <p> He moved to New Jersey by himself at 16 to help his older sister during her pregnancy . In 1989 , he had a daughter , Dilena Perez-Dilan , though his relationship with her mother did n't last . In the late 1990s , he met and married Haydee . And for two decades , Angel got to live his modest dream , operating a small used car dealership and auto body shop in Millville . <p> Angel and Haydee on their wedding day in the late 1990s . He lived his dream for 15 years -- owning a used car dealership and auto body shop . <p> Today , all that 's left of the business can be found in his backyard , where more than 40 unsold vehicles -- rusted Hondas , Nissans and Toyotas -- sit idly behind his home , arranged in neat rows and at haphazard angles like dusty trophies from the old days . <p> In 2004 , Angel 's body started betraying him : unexplained tremors , spasms , stiffness in his muscles . He was diagnosed with Parkinson 's a year later . The physical toll of mechanical work soon became too much to bear , and he shuttered the shop . <p> By 2018 , he had trouble walking and driving . His once-confident speech sounded halting and gravelly . <p> He turned to his faith around the time of his diagnosis , attending Temple of Faith Church in Vineland two , even three times a week with Haydee . He trusted God had a plan that would see him through his physical decline . <p> His other refuge was the bay , crabbing the waters along Matts Landing . On a calm day , the only real sound comes from the chirping shorebirds perched in a line of trees across the way . The setting brought him a sense of spiritual and physical peace . The water loosened his muscles and calmed his tremors and spasms . <p> Angel , right , and his wife 's cousin crabbing near the Delaware Bay . The waters that flow into the bay were Angel 's sanctuary after he was diagnosed with Parkinson 's disease . <p> If only for a few hours , he felt like the person he once was . <p> " It was like his sanctuary , " his daughter said . <p> The morning of July 2 , 2018 , felt particularly satisfying . <p> Angel 's trips to the water had grown infrequent over the years . But Haydee said she 'd bring him on a few outings that week . Christian music played on a speaker they 'd brought , as the four laughed and joked and took turns tossing in throw lines . Angel proudly held up his net full of crabs to show everyone knew who was boss of those waters . Haydee , who has jet black hair , olive skin and a shy , reserved manner , held up a crab of her own , one that was as big as her face , before tossing it in one of the buckets with the rest . <p> Angel did n't know it yet , but it would be the last time he would ever feel the water wash over his fingers and feet . <h> III <p> Under a microscope , it looks almost like a computer mouse -- a curved rod with a squiggly tail protruding from one end . <p> It thrives in warm , brackish waters , where it incubates and multiplies . It can enter through an open wound , as it did with Angel , or through the consumption of raw or undercooked shellfish . While it works its way through the body , it can evade the immune system and offer few clues to its presence , other than perhaps a few benign-looking symptoms such as redness and swelling on the skin . <p> V. vulnificus , as it 's medically known , is one of about 100 Vibrio species -- roughly a dozen of which can cause illness -- and is part of the same family as cholera . It is usually harmless to people with a healthy immune system . Some may experience only mild symptoms akin to food poisoning before the body naturally eradicates the bacteria . <p> But for others , especially those who are immunocompromised , there are risks of life-threatening complications . In rare cases , when V. vulnificus enters through a scratch or cut , toxins released by the microbes can cause the wound to develop necrotizing fasciitis , an illness more commonly known as " flesh-eating disease , " which corrodes and kills soft tissue and can spread throughout the entire surface of the body . <p> In those instances , it is n't necessarily " flesh-eating disease " that ends up killing patients . Instead , sepsis -- a blood infection that can occur when the body is attempting to fend off serious infection -- often causes a drastic drop in blood pressure and leads to multiple organ failure . <p> On the evening of July 2 , 2018 , Haydee cooked a batch of crabs they 'd caught that morning . The couple , married for 21 years , then enjoyed a quiet evening together . <p> Nothing seemed out of the ordinary . <p> The first signs of trouble arrived the next morning , when Angel noticed his right ankle was red and swollen . He did n't think much of it at first , but decided to get it checked at a nearby urgent care center . A doctor there said it looked like cellulitis , a common bacterial skin infection . He prescribed antibiotics , which Angel filled on the way home . <p> But the pain creeping up his leg was getting worse . The soreness grew into a burning , stabbing pain in his ankle . <p> This small scratch on Angel 's right ankle marks the beginning of his ordeal . Rare bacteria seeped into the scrape and caused a perfect storm of medical crises . <p> " It felt like my leg was going to burst , " Angel recalled . <p> Seeing her usually composed husband near tears , Haydee called an ambulance , which rushed him to an emergency room in Vineland , the next town over . But again , the doctor he saw there was n't especially alarmed . <p> Continue with the antibiotics , he told Angel , and come back if it gets worse . <p> That night , Angel 's leg swelled so much it looked like a fleshy tube with no ankle . Blisters were forming on the skin . <p> Haydee rushed him to the emergency room , this time in Elmer , in neighboring Salem County . But doctors still had few answers . Even though V. vulnificus infections are covered in medical textbooks , they are not something most clinicians are accustomed to seeing in New Jersey . <p> " It was always a Florida sort of infection , " said Dr. David Cennimo , an infectious disease expert at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School . <p> But for clinicians in New Jersey , he said : " This is one of the things that you now have to think about . " <p> In the examination room at the hospital , Angel could barely speak from the pain . Doctors started him on broad-spectrum antibiotics , which are used when the source of a bacterial infection is unknown . An operation to drain the leg of rapidly building fluid would be essential . <p> Cooper University Hospital was the fourth and final medical facility that treated Angel . <p> In the early morning of July 4 , 2018 , Angel was rushed by ambulance to Cooper University Hospital , his fourth and final medical facility in 24 hours . His blood pressure and oxygen levels were dropping . A breathing tube was inserted down his throat as his lungs struggled for air . He was placed into a coma and wheeled into the operating room . <p> That first procedure , called a fasciotomy and done in the hopes of salvaging the rapidly deteriorating leg , was just the start of Angel 's odyssey . Cultures soon would explain what was happening inside his body . <p> The diagnosis : V. vulnificus infection , complicated by sepsis and flesh-eating disease on his leg . <p> The death rate for such patients ? <p> More than 50% . <h> IV <p> Every medical story is , in effect , a mystery . <p> Doctors play the role of detective -- asking questions , considering test results , eliminating possibilities -- until arriving at a diagnosis . In the case of a possible epidemic , there are other mysteries : How was the illness contracted ? Who might be susceptible ? How can it be stopped before it infects others ? <p> For much of the previous year before she encountered Angel Perez , Doktor , the infectious diseases specialist at Cooper University Hospital , was wrestling with a mystery : Why had three cases of V. vulnificus infection turned up in her South Jersey hospital ? <p> " Why now ? " Doktor remembered asking herself . <p> Vibrio vulnificus , sometimes called " flesh-eating bacteria , " is part of the same family as cholera . It is usually harmless to people with a healthy immune system . But some face life-threatening complications . <p> Such infections are rare to begin with -- only about 200 cases are reported in the U.S. each year , according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention . Most occur in states near the warm waters of the Southeastern U.S. and Gulf Coast . Until recently , V. vulnificus infections were almost nonexistent in New Jersey . <p> The first case Cooper saw came in July 2017 , when a 38-year-old man presented at the hospital with V. vulnificus in his bloodstream and flesh-eating disease on his left calf . How the man contracted the bacterial infection , though , was never clearly established : He told specialists he worked at a seafood restaurant , but insisted he had n't been crabbing or fishing before he got sick . The man survived . <p> Dr. Katherine Doktor , an infectious disease specialist on the Cooper University Hospital team treating Angel , was surprised to see so many V. vulnificus-related infections in her hospital . <p> Two more cases arrived at Cooper in September 2017 , just weeks apart . A 64-year-old man died in the hospital from an infection he contracted after crabbing in the Delaware Bay . And a 46-year-old man was admitted with an infection and flesh-eating disease after cutting his left leg while crabbing in the same area . ( He survived . ) <p> By the time Angel arrived the following July with an eerily similar story -- a day crabbing in waters that fed the Delaware Bay and with a cut on his leg -- the connection was unavoidable . <p> " We were questioning , ' Is this ( area ) now a habitat for them ? ' " said Doktor , who has auburn-brown hair , a soft voice and a notable sense of humor about her last name . " Before , the cool temperature of the water was not very hospitable for their growth . So was the Delaware Bay becoming warmer and now making a new home for the bacteria to grow ? " <p> It 's one of the least considered aspects of climate change -- that a warming planet and rising sea temperatures can lead to the spread of deadly bacteria . <p> But that 's what appears to be happening , according to experts . <p> " The Delaware Bay has been free of infections -- maybe once in a decade or something -- but it 's changing , " said Rita Colwell , professor at the University of Maryland at College Park and Johns Hopkins University Bloomberg School of Public Health . <p> " In my opinion , yes , something definitely is happening , " she added . <p> Some researchers see the bacteria 's changing behavior as a " barometer of climate change " -- an early warning sign for the years ahead , said Craig Baker-Austin , senior research scientist and director of the UK National Reference Laboratory . <p> " Very few human pathogens have that kind of relationship with the environment , " Baker-Austin said . <p> Infections from V. vulnificus bacteria have been rare in New Jersey waters . But that seems to be changing due to climate change . <p> V. vulnificus flourishes in the warm , salty water found in bays and estuaries , which also provide just enough fresh water for the bacteria to grow . First isolated and identified in 1976 , V. vulnificus always has been present in the Delaware Bay . But , historically , New Jersey never had to worry . The cooler waters surrounding the state had never been suitable enough for the bacteria to pose a threat . <p> That appears to be changing . According to Baker-Austin , V. vulnificus and other Vibrio species have been detected in the ice-cold waters of the Baltic Sea , and infections from the bacteria have emerged in Denmark , Finland and Sweden . <p> In New Jersey , air temperatures have risen 2 degrees Celsius over the past 125 years , the third-highest increase in the nation , according to an August analysis by The Washington Post . Much of that increase has occurred within the past three decades . <p> The Delaware Bay has warmed 1.2 to 1.9 degrees Celsius over the past 16 years , according to Matt Oliver , the Patricia and Charles Robertson associate professor of marine science and policy at the University of Delaware . <p> Every degree warmer boosts bacteria 's ability to divide and multiply . The increased melting of glaciers is also producing more fresh water in the oceans , allowing bacteria to venture beyond their typical range . <p> " Two degrees is extremely significant , " said Jim Oliver , a professor of microbiology at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte . " It 's going to mean that a lot of places that have never seen this bacteria will now see it . " <h> V <p> In his hospital room at Cooper , Angel Perez and his family knew none of this . <p> The medical mystery of how and why this happened had taken a back seat to an increasingly urgent challenge : keeping Angel alive . <p> A group of a dozen or so friends and family members had settled in to keep vigil , sleeping on the floor , an air mattress and even in chairs in the hospital waiting room . <p> Angel 's pastor and members of his church visited almost daily . With one hand on Angel 's forehead , the other raised in the air , the pastor delivered a prayer as the others followed along , arms raised above their heads , palms tilted to the ceiling . Angel would try to follow along , but his speech was often garbled and slurred . <p> " There were so many people just praying for him ... and just praying for a miracle from God to come upon him , " said the Rev. Israel Delgado of Temple of Faith Church in Vineland . <p> Angel 's arms and legs withered and blackened from gangrene as his doctors fought to stop the infection . He faced a bitter crossroads : lose his hands and feet to amputation or die . <p> The most immediate threat to Angel remained sepsis and his dangerously low blood pressure . To get blood pumping to the heart , brain and other vital organs , doctors administered a potent class of drugs called vasopressors , or pressors . The drugs divert blood from elsewhere in the body -- non-vital organs and the arms and legs -- and rush it to the vital organs . <p> Siphoning blood from the arms and legs , however , puts the appendages at risk of developing gangrene . Doctors hoped the death of tissue would n't go beyond his fingers and toes , where discoloration initially emerged . <p> " You 're having to weigh daily , are the benefits worth the risks ? " said Dr. David Fuller , one of Angel 's primary doctors , who serves as director of upper extremity surgery at Cooper . <p> After the first week , doctors brought the bacteria under control and staved off the flesh-eating infection on his leg . But the gangrene was showing no signs of abating . By the middle of July , it had spread halfway up his forearms and shins . Angel 's rotting limbs almost certainly would become a feeding ground for new bacteria and further infection . The only option would be amputation . Yet his doctors did n't believe he was strong enough to survive surgery . <p> At the center of all this , attempting to serve as her father 's advocate and her family 's spokeswoman , was Dilena Perez-Dilan , Angel 's daughter . ( While Haydee was supportive of the reporting of this story , she said she was shy and did n't want to discuss Angel 's plight at length . Both Angel and Dilena later speculated that talking about it was simply too upsetting for Haydee . ) <p> Standing 5 feet , 4 inches , with olive skin and dark brown hair , Dilena was regularly talking to doctors after Haydee had designated her as their point person . She had taken leave from her job as a social worker and had n't slept at her home in New Castle , Delaware , since her dad was admitted to Cooper . And she had barely seen her 6-year-old son . <p> Dilena always had been close to her father . She saw him as stoic and strong . As a child , she rarely saw him cry . <p> Dilena Perez-Dilan serves as her father 's advocate and the family spokeswoman as his story made headlines around the world . <p> But now it was Dilena who refused to cry in front of her father . <p> " He felt what was going on , and I did n't want to show him ( my tears ) , " she said . <p> The situation was growing dire . His hallucinations were becoming more intense -- delusions of men trying to break into his room and kill him . <p> He 'd scream at the top of his lungs : " Hallelujah ! Hallelujah ! " <p> Dilena recalled a day in the hospital when she was just staring at her father 's arms and legs , almost awed by the grotesque transformation . <p> " The color was just black , " she said . And the smell pervaded his room . <p> " The closer you got to it , the more powerful the smell was , " she said . <p> Near the end of July , according to Dilena , the care team told the family her father still was n't strong enough for surgery . But unless his limbs were removed , he would have a little more than a week to live -- days of continued anguish and decline . <p> Angel began summoning family into his room around this time , telling everyone he loved them and basically " giving a verbal will to everybody , " Dilena said . <p> " He was making amends with people . He brought me in and told me everything he wanted to tell me , " she said . <p> He told Dilena to take care of her son and to buy a home . <p> " He talked about how proud he was of me and that he was grateful to have me as his daughter and that he tried his best with me , " she said . <p> Around Aug. 1 , the family consented to put Angel in hospice in another wing of the hospital -- though staff had called it " comfort care . " With all the information being hurled at them , Dilena and Haydee did n't fully understand treatment would cease , except for the slow drip of morphine to dull the pain until Angel 's body gave out altogether . <p> Not long after settling in the new room , Dilena and Haydee panicked . Had they made a terrible decision ? Within an hour , the pair went to Angel 's bedside to explain exactly where he was . <p> He had been slipping in and out of delirium for weeks . But at that moment , Angel gazed up and in his frail , fragile voice said he wanted to resume treatment . <p> " We went to my dad -- me and my stepmom -- and we said , ' Dad , what do you want to do ? What do you think you can handle ? ' " Dilena recalled . <p> " He was like , ' No , I want to fight . I want to keep fighting . ' So , once he said he was going to keep fighting , we got up , we walked over and we told the lead nurse . ... ' We no longer want to be on this comfort care . My dad does n't want to do this . He wants to be treated . ' " <p> All told , Angel spent four hours in hospice care . <h> VI <p> In medicine , talk of miracles is often discouraged . It can raise false hope in dire circumstances , and it minimizes the hard , practical work doctors do to combat illness . <p> There 's a reason , though , the word endures in medical conversations . In many recoveries , doctors ca n't entirely explain why some recover and others do n't . <p> Dr. David Fuller , director of upper extremity surgery at Cooper University Hospital , was one of Angel 's primary doctors . <p> When Dr. David Fuller , who is tall , thin and has gray hair , first met Angel , he did not think the patient would survive . But then , " He started getting a little stronger . His blood pressure , heart rate -- things started becoming more stable , and he was requiring less and less pressors , " Fuller said . " And I think that 's what really turned the tide in his recovery . " <p> Ultimately , even Fuller was surprised by Angel 's turnaround . <p> " What was it that allowed him to survive ? I do n't think any of us can answer that question , " Fuller said . <p> The gangrene seemed to have run its course , stopping 5 or 6 inches below both kneecaps and a few inches below Angel 's elbows , with a gruesome line on the flesh showing the separation of the dead , black tissue and healthy tissue . The split , known medically as the " line of demarcation , " is something the body produces on its own , signaling to surgeons where they should amputate a gangrenous limb . <p> A week after Angel left hospice , doctors told him they would have to amputate his limbs . The surgery to remove both forearms was scheduled for Aug. 8 . Fuller explained the risks to Angel and his family . He told them there was still a chance Angel would die during surgery . <p> And then Fuller presented Angel and his family with a consent form . <p> Angel did nothing . <p> He would n't speak or answer the doctor . He would n't give consent . The family was hesitant to sign on his behalf , believing it was his call . <p> " We did n't want to make a decision without him saying yes or no , " Dilena said . <p> The surgery was scrapped . <p> Several days passed in uncertainty . Fuller tried to emphasize to Angel the significance of the opportunity . Dilena pleaded as well . For the first time , her exhaustion and worry spilled over into frustration . <p> Angel 's hands could not be saved . When he knocked them together , they made a haunting sound " like two rocks banging together . " <p> " I told him , ' Dad , you need to let us know what you want to do , because when the time came for us to make the decision and give our word , we asked you , and you did n't respond , ' " she recalled saying to him . <p> She later added , " I think he was very scared . " <p> Then one night , Dilena recalled , her father knocked his hands against each other . They made a gruesome sound . <p> " ( It was ) like two rocks banging together because they were so , so dried out , " she said . <p> For weeks , Angel 's family and friends had known there would be no saving his limbs . But Angel kept believing otherwise , that somehow they might be regenerated , the way a spider can regrow its legs . <p> " Do what you got ta do , " Angel told the doctors , finally consenting to surgery . <p> The first surgery took place on Aug. 15 . An anesthetized Angel lay flat under the beaming LED surgical lights , eyes closed , a breathing tube down his throat . Fuller and the team went to work , carefully amputating his arms a few inches below the elbows . After the limbs were removed , skin flaps were sown . His nubs were wrapped , and he was moved to recovery . <p> The entire procedure took roughly six hours . But it was only half the battle -- a second surgery to amputate his legs would still need to be scheduled . <p> Angel remembers little from the hours and days that followed . He was groggy and disoriented from the anesthesia . His flickering nerves were still telling his brain his hands were there . <p> For her part , Dilena became obsessed with the idea she had to get her dad outside , if only to the hospital courtyard , before his next surgery . He had been living inside a hospital room for weeks . She believed she owed him a breath of fresh air . <p> The hospital would n't authorize it because of the risk of further infection , but a nurse the family befriended said she 'd help . Before dawn on a late August morning , the nurse and Dilena hoisted Angel into a wheelchair , hurried him down the hallway , past security and out to the courtyard , where a garden and gravel paths encircle a stone tile waterfall . <p> The sun had n't come up . Dilena recalled the crisp dawn air , the chirping birds . Her father would have his legs amputated soon . But at that moment , all concerns faded . <p> When the sun began to rise , they both started to cry . After nearly two months , this would be the moment Dilena finally let her father see her tears . <p> " He just stared at the sky , " she said . " At one point , he fell asleep because it was so early , and then he would wake back up , and his eyes got big . " <p> About a week and a half later , Angel underwent his next surgery . Both of his legs were removed a few inches below the kneecaps . <h> VII <p> There 's a playbook for most illnesses , dreadful but familiar . No one wants to watch a loved one fight cancer or recover from a stroke . But there is comfort , at least , in our understanding of the contours of those battles . We know what triumph over such illnesses looks like , just as we have learned to recognize the warning signs of likely defeat . <p> But what happened to Angel Perez was of a different order entirely . Few of us know what flesh-eating disease looks like , much less how you go about recovering from it . <p> And the psychic toll would seem to be incomprehensible : to be out crabbing one day , and then to learn , just a short time later , that your arms and legs must be amputated if there is any hope of surviving ? <p> I learned about Angel Perez on July 8 , 2018 , just a few days after he arrived at Cooper University Hospital , when word of his medical crisis made its way to the NJ Advance Media newsroom . I interviewed Dilena by telephone the next day , and she spoke of both the randomness of her father 's plight and his faith and his determination in the face of the unimaginable . <p> " He 's been praising God nonstop , " Dilena told me during that first call . " He 's just happy to have a second chance . " <p> Angel 's daily physical therapy routine included exercises such as hitting and catching balloons to teach him how to use what remains of his limbs . <p> I reported on the story throughout the summer of 2018 , as Angel 's condition worsened , and news of his plight made national and international headlines . When he somehow survived the ordeal , the media attention naturally faded . But a new set of challenges and agonies presented themselves . <p> Would faith and family be enough to carry Angel through the long recovery ahead ? <p> How do you rebuild a life without limbs , especially if you 're a man whose livelihood once depended on your hands ? <p> Dilena invited me to meet her father in late September last year , just after he had been discharged and moved to a rehabilitation facility in Vineland . Back in July , she sent me a photo of Angel , which we published with our stories . In that photo , he 's wearing a black baseball cap crooked and low on his head and holding out a crabbing net to the camera , with several crabs at the bottom . Although the net obscures his face , you can see his grin , perhaps prideful , perhaps mischievous , maybe both . <p> But now , as I stepped into his room , I saw a different man entirely . A frail-looking Angel sat in a wheelchair , wearing black athletic shorts and a teal T-shirt . His arms were amputated below the elbow . His right leg was amputated almost entirely up to his kneecap . His left leg was amputated about 4 or 5 inches lower . <p> Despite his faith and the considerable progress he has made , Angel battles despair , thinking about all he has lost . <p> His face was pale and gaunt , his torso a mere shell . His daughter said he lost 40 pounds while in the hospital before the amputations . Thick , raised scar tissue branched in different directions on the nub of his left arm like the roots of a tree . <p> Dilena was there that day , and so was Angel 's older sister , Aida Perez . She was one of four sisters , but the one who always looked after him when they were kids . Together , we watched as he went about his daily physical therapy routine , exercises designed to teach him to use what remained of his limbs . That day , he was learning to roll over on a bed , posting his nubs on the surface and then rotating his torso . He ping-ponged a balloon back and forth with one therapist , hitting it with the right nub , then with the left . She then had him clamp the balloon with both nubs to get used to grabbing objects without his hands . <p> As a pair of physical therapists hoisted her father from his wheelchair and onto the bed , Dilena began to cry . Aida stood by the wall , looking on quietly . The therapists stretched Angel 's muscles , pushing down on his kneecaps . Just below the right nub was a thick line from the surgical incision that had been made about a month earlier . You could see the fading marks from the sutures that kept the folded skin flaps sewn together , not unlike the seams of a baseball . <p> Angel struggles to operate a TV remote control , one of the everyday challenges he faces after the amputation of his four limbs . <p> Angel signaled that he was fine and not in pain . He 'd been having trouble speaking since arriving at the rehab facility . When he did , he was barely audible . <p> The physical therapists secured him through his armpits and shifted his body toward the head of the bed , positioning him upright for the next exercise . To me , Angel looked utterly helpless . The therapists then showed him how to put on and take off his T-shirt -- one therapist recommended he bite down on the neckline to keep it steady as he slipped his limbs in and out of the sleeves . <p> Yet despite the obvious anguish of the session , Angel seemed hopeful , almost unaffected as I asked him questions . <p> He was adamant he had never felt fear . <p> " The doctors had more fear than I had , " he mumbled . <p> Everything had been in God 's hands , he said .
