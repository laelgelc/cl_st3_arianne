<p> Scientists played sounds of a healthy reef to attract fish to dead corals in a process known as ' acoustic enrichment ' . The process can be used to increase fish population that will subsequently help restore degraded regions of a reef . <p> The international team researchers conducted their study in the Great Barrier Reef of Australia . Various parts of the reef have fallen victim to coral bleaching as a side effect of rising sea temperatures due to climate change . Compared to the healthy coral reefs these parts are dead silent . <p> A senior author of the study published in Nature Communications , Prof. Steve Simpson from the University of Exeter explained : " Healthy coral reefs are remarkably noisy places -- the crackle of snapping shrimp and the whoops and grunts of fish combine to form a dazzling biological soundscape . Juvenile fish home in on these sounds when they 're looking for a place to settle . " <p> To attract the young fish to dead reefs researchers placed loudspeakers that would emulate the ' soundscape ' of a healthy reef . Twice as many fish with a 50% increase in diversity of species was observed in the degraded regions with loudspeakers . <p> The researchers said the return of fish is only meant to support the restoration of the reef , as each species of fish has different functions that help maintain the health of the ecosystem . <p> In a separate project , researchers are using ' floating nurseries ' and ' LarvaBoat ' to help spread and cultivate coral babies in the degraded sections of the Great Barrier Reef . <p> The team behind the Coral Larval Restoration Project collected millions of coral eggs and sperm released during the annual mass spawning event . The coral larvae from the spawn were then grown in floating nurseries and delivered to specific areas of degraded corals by LarvalBots and LarvalBoats.
