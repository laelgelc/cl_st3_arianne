<p> CLEVELAND , Ohio -- During a recent trip to our local Heinen 's grocery store , my two young daughters pointed out a set of new signs that had sprouted up in the parking lot . They featured a smiling planet Earth , and in a speech balloon , the phrase " Remember your reusable bags . " <p> It was a cute , friendly nudge in the direction of adapting to Cuyahoga County 's forthcoming ban on single-use plastic bags , which was enacted in May and is set to take effect in most cities Jan. 1 . And my girls spent a few minutes rattling off the reasons they have learned in school and elsewhere why plastics are a scourge on the environment . They reflected on photos they 've seen of animals tangled in bags and disturbing images of the Great Pacific Garbage Patch , a vortex of marine debris , mostly plastics , estimated at roughly twice the size of the United States . <p> They were pleased when I told them about the bag ban . They and many of their peers understand , if only on a basic level , how plastic poisons our waterways , suffocates wildlife and poses an existential threat for humans -- from the carbon footprint of its factory production to the microplastic lurking in many water supplies throughout the world . So , if elementary students can grasp the dire importance of policy changes like bans on plastic bags , why are so many of the grown-ups around them complaining about the inconvenience such laws create ? <p> Cleveland City Council recently passed legislation opting out of the ban in the city for at least six months to consider the concerns of some grocers and to perhaps adopt the city 's own approach to reducing plastic pollution . The ban , which could keep more than 300 million bags out of landfills each year , is so important , it 's worth doing right . So , if grocers say they need a few extra months to figure out how they will work within the new guidelines , then so be it , I suppose . <p> That said , in all the research I 've done on the controversy of single-use bag bans , I have yet to find the objection that trumps the prescient environmental concerns that even kids appreciate . Instead , it seems many counterpoints can be traced back to the nearsightedness of consumers who resist the notion of change . I 'm seeing man-on-the-street interviews with shoppers , saying they simply prefer the convenience of plastic and would rather not have to think about toting along reusable sacks . <p> " I always forget them at home , so I rely on the plastic bags , " one shopper frankly told a News Channel 5 reporter last month . Another griped that paper bags are n't as durable , entirely ignoring the option of keeping reusable bags handy . <p> These are the myopic viewpoints that lay the groundwork for retailers ' arguments that banning plastic bags would make them less competitive . And because the financial wellbeing of retailers is a priority of state lawmakers , they are now seriously considering legislation that would pre-empt Ohio cities from passing bans on plastic bags altogether . <p> But to all those arguments , elevating the importance of profitability and consumer convenience over the health of the planet , I respond with just one word : Aldi . <p> I 'm sure I 'll get emails from readers , who will find something they hate about the company , which is headquartered in Germany and operates more than 1,800 grocery stores in 35 states in the U.S. But Aldi has gone to laudable lengths to limit its impact on the environment . Its stores have never offered single-use plastic bags , a policy Aldi credits with keeping more than 15 billion bags out of landfills . And earlier this year , Aldi pledged to make 100 percent of its packaging , including plastic containers , reusable , recyclable or compostable by 2025 and to reduce packaging material by at least 15 percent across all products by then . <p> Despite some other grocers ' concerns that eliminating plastic bags would hurt business , Aldi 's stance on bags is one of many ways the company keeps its prices low . As a result , every family I know , whether living in the city or suburbs , shops there . And guess what . Those families always figure out a way to get their groceries home without plastic bags . <p> I know the counterarguments here . That paper bags , while biodegradable , require cutting down and processing trees , which boosts greenhouse gas emissions . That jurisdictions that have banned single-use plastic bags have seen an uptick in the use of heavier duty garbage bags instead , because many consumers also used the shopping bags as trash can liners or to pick up dog poop . And that a cloth bag must be reused more than 100 times before it can be deemed better for climate change than using a flimsy plastic bag once . <p> Indeed , every alternative comes with a catch . <p> But one need only Google those oceanic garbage patches to know we must start somewhere -- and now -- on the path toward reducing non-biodegradable trash or we are doomed to drown in plastic . A sweeping change in policy , such as what Cuyahoga County is attempting , could be the most effective way to get consumers to think differently about the choices they make . <p> And one good choice begets another . One can find compostable trashcan liners and biodegradable dog poop bags on the market . ( While we 're at it , we can buy food in bulk , rather than in smaller packages , avoid bottled water , pack our kids ' snacks in reusable containers instead of baggies , and of course , recycle everything we can . ) <p> We can adapt . In fact , adaptation is our lifeblood . And society managed to survive before there were plastic bags .
