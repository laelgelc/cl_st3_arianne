<p> According to a new report from the state Legislative Analyst 's Office , local governments in California are moving far too slowly to stop the worst damage from the sea-level rise that will come with climate change . <p> " If current global greenhouse gas emission trends continue , between $8 billion and $10 billion of existing property in California is likely to be underwater by 2050 , with an additional $6 billion to $10 billion at risk during high tide , " wrote the office in its report . <p> The actions local governments need to be taking right now are dramatic . <p> The report implies that California needs to start building 100,000 additional housing units annually in coastal cities -- and that new housing would need to be adapted to the potential risks of sea-level rise . Considering that California builds only 100,000 to 140,000 new units annually now , this would be an enormous political fight . <p> The report also recommends a more serious level of regional cooperation than is typical for California . <h> Related Stories <p> One example is the Oakland International Airport . The airport is owned by the Port of Oakland , but when it faces severe flooding -- as it 's expected to in the coming years -- it will affect residents and businesses across the Bay Area . <p> The Port of Oakland is already planning ahead -- construction is expected to begin next year on a dike to protect the main commercial runway -- but keeping the airport open and operational during years of storm surge will require coordination among a number of counties and agencies . <p> None of this will be easy , which is one of the reasons that local governments have n't yet done more . But the alternatives will be even harder .
