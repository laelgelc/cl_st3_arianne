<p> A top Democratic appropriator says the year-end spending deal marks a major change in climate policy . <p> Rep. Marcy Kaptur , the chair of the House panel that funds the Energy Department , is championing a big uptick in federal research and development for clean energy . <p> " We have the green real deal , " Kaptur told National Journal , alluding to the controversial Green New Deal resolution pushed by environmental hawks . <p> " We have to invent the future , whether it is grid modernization or new energy sources that are renewable and nonpolluting , " she said . " We have our work cut out for us , but I think this bill really moves us heavily in that direction . " <p> The nearly $1.4-trillion-dollar spending agreement , divided into two behemoth packages , bumps up renewable-energy and energy-efficiency funding by $411 million , a $2.5 billion increase over the White House budget request . The legislation also provides $425 million to the Advanced Research Projects Agency-Energy program , which the Trump administration has aimed to zero out . <p> Over recent years , lawmakers have reached across the aisle to deliver incrementally more funding for those programs , which help to promote wind , solar , and energy storage , as well as advanced nuclear and other emerging technologies . <p> But environmental groups are n't sold on the deal that lawmakers struck . In fact , some of them are livid . And they 're training their focus directly on Democrats . <p> Those green groups say the broader package that 's riding alongside the spending bills , which includes tax legislation that Democrats tried but failed to turn into a major clean-energy incentive , falls woefully short in enacting policies to confront climate change . <p> " House Democrats were given a mandate to fight hard and secure policy victories on climate action , clean energy , and our children 's future , " Sierra Club Executive Director Michael Brune tweeted . " They had an opportunity this week . They failed to deliver . " <p> Other environmental groups , like the influential Natural Resources Defense Council , did n't even weigh in on the renewable-spending increases in the appropriations bills . And environmentalists and public-health advocates have also blasted a deal struck in recent days to overhaul North American trade , as well as the annual defense-policy bill for language on a pervasive family of chemicals called PFAS . <p> But the environmental groups are taking particular aim at the tax-extender package , which hitched a ride on the minibus that included the DOE clean-energy funding . <p> " The Trump administration and some in Congress appear willing to ignore the very real promise of these innovative technologies , " said John Bowman , the top government-affairs staff member at the NRDC . " Lawmakers must continue fighting for a tax code that protects our climate . " The bills are likely to head to President Trump 's desk after passing the Senate on Thursday . <p> In mid-November , Democrats floated a tax package that would extend for years dozens of breaks , including the production tax credit for wind and the solar-investment tax credit . <p> The draft bill also expanded the investment credit to energy storage , which renewable advocates say is essential to rapidly increase solar deployment , and raised the threshold for the phase-out of the main electric-vehicle credit . <p> The deal struck earlier this week , however , dropped virtually all those demands , instead extending the wind credit for one year and biodiesel credits retroactively for five years , meaning the credit will be in place through 2022 . <p> " What happened with regard to the effort to move us into the 21st-century grid that we know we need ? " Greg Wetstone , president of the American Council on Renewable Energy , said in an interview . " What concerns us about this package is the overall lack of ambition here , the absence of a sense of urgency that was a factor here . We had an opportunity to make really important gains . " <p> Democrats on the tax-writing Ways and Means Committee said the White House and Republicans forced their hand . <p> " The White House would n't work with us on any of the green stuff we had , and we were having trouble with the Senate anyway , " Rep. Earl Blumenauer said . " It 's beyond frustrating , but I 'm hopeful that there 's going to be some people in the Senate that are going to feel differently . And I 'm hopeful that we 'll be able to mobilize some people in these industries . A lot of them are red-state issues . " <p> A congressional aide with knowledge of the negotiations , who spoke on background because of the secrecy of the talks , backed up that assessment . <p> " On Monday night , the White House cut the renewable provisions out of the package , " the aide said . " Of course , Democrats care deeply about these issues , but at that moment could not force the government to shut down over them . " <p> The solar industry , which has carved out big footprints in states like Texas and Georgia , as well as blue states like California , still comprises only roughly 1.5 percent of the total U.S. energy portfolio , according to 2018 data . <p> " After a marathon political showdown , President Trump 's opposition to green subsidies has won the day . Credit is something you earn , not give away , " Tom Pyle , president of the American Energy Alliance , said in a statement . " President Trump deserves credit for putting billions back into taxpayers ' pockets and the renewable-energy special interests ought to be terrified at this outcome . "
