<h> Global warming is robbing world 's oceans of oxygen <p> The amount of oxygen in the world 's oceans decreased by 2 percent between 1960 and 2010 and is expected to go down by a further 3-4 percent by the year 2100 as a result of global warming , something that will have an effect on habitats and the coastal economies that depend on them . <p> A Starfish crawls along a Kelp trunk next to a Sea Urchin in the Indian Ocean , Cape Town , South Africa , 06 October 2019 . ( EPA-EFE/NIC BOTHMA / MANILA BULLETIN ) <p> This situation was outlined in a report by the International Union for Conservation of Nature ( IUCN ) -- " Ocean deoxygenation : Everyone 's problem " -- presented Saturday at the United Nations climate change summit in Madrid . <p> According to the study , this loss of oxygen is closely linked to the warming of the planet and the acidification of the oceans caused by an increase in carbon dioxide ( CO2 ) as a result of greenhouse gas ( GHG ) emissions and the so-called fertilization of the oceans . <p> Most of the excess heat retained by the Earth is absorbed by the oceans , which inhibits the diffusion of oxygen from the surface to the depths , and the increase in nutrients that arrive via rivers leads to the proliferation of algae and an increase in the demand for oxygen , said Dan Laffoley of the IUCN Marine Science and Conservation program . <p> The study identified over 900 coastal areas and semi-enclosed seas around the world that are subject to the effects of eutrophication -- the excessive enrichment of the water with nutrients or organic matter . <p> More than 700 of these areas have hypoxia problems ( a lack of oxygen ) compared to 45 of them back in the 1960s . <p> And the volume of water that has been completely depleted of oxygen has quadrupled , according to the report . <p> The researchers point out that eutrophication-induced hypoxia can be reversed if certain measures are taken , but hypoxia caused by global warming is more difficult to combat . <p> They are therefore calling for a " drastic effort " to reduce greenhouse gas emissions in order to reduce the rate of oxygen reduction in the oceans , a new global problem that remains relatively unknown , according to professor Lisa Levin from the Scripps Institution of Oceanography . <p> Beyond the damage caused by overfishing , pollution , habitat destruction and plastic , there is no environmental variable of such ecological importance to marine ecosystems that has changed so drastically in such a short period of time owing to human activities as that of dissolved oxygen , said John Baxter , an expert in Protected Areas of the IUCN . <p> According to Baxter , although the causes are known , no attention is being paid to the consequences that this will have in the long term for human health , the economy and society . <p> It will entail the loss of biodiversity , changes in the distribution of species , the displacement or reduction of fishery resources as well as changes in biogeochemical cycles . <p> The report highlights oceans as a source of oxygen for the atmosphere and the Baltic Sea and the Black Sea as the largest semi-closed marine ecosystems whose oxygen content is low . <p> But the repercussions of deoxygenation are not limited to closed or semi-enclosed seas -- areas with limited oxygen content have also expanded in much of the Atlantic in the last 50-100 years , even in connected seas like the Mediterranean . <p> And besides affecting coastal areas , the lack of oxygen is also seen at medium depths between 300-1,000 meters -- the richest in biodiversity - , as in the Atlantic basin , which has witnessed a significant reduction in the last 60 years . <p> The report pointed to the need to reduce waste from agriculture , industry , wastewater and to avoid other sources of stress for the world 's oceans such as pollution and overfishing . <p> For Levin , there are solutions but there needs to be greater ambition when it comes to reducing greenhouse gas emissions -- an objective that is in the hands of governments .
