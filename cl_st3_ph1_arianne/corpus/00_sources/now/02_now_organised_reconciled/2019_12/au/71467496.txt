<p> Outbreaks of dengue fever in Australia can be predicted with surprising accuracy by measuring rainfall and temperatures in Bali the previous month , new research has found . <p> The research from the Queensland University of Technology 's school of public health and social work has found a strong link between weather patterns causing a rise in dengue cases in Bali and a corresponding jump in cases reported here . <p> Lead researcher Wenbiao Hu said it was clear Australian tourists were contracting the disease while in the popular holiday destination and bringing it back home . <p> An Aedes aegypti mosquito which is capable of transmitting dengue fever and other tropical diseases . <p> " Dengue in Bali is significantly associated with imported dengue in Queensland , New South Wales , Northern Territory , Tasmania , Victoria and Western Australia at a lag of one month , " Professor Hu said . <p> Advertisement <p> " This study quantified the relationship between Bali and dengue in Australia , and gives new insight into developing a dengue early warning system in Australia . " <p> Professor Hu said given the conditions in Bali correlated so closely with Australian outbreaks , authorities could theoretically develop a four-month window to respond to local clusters of the tropical disease . <p> " The mechanisms behind this lead-time include a few weeks between suitable climate and increased mosquito activity and local dengue transmission in Bali , and the incubation time for infections in Australian travellers as well as time to diagnosis in Australia , " he said . <p> Dengue fever is contracted through mosquito bites and causes symptoms similar to a bad flu , however extreme cases can cause serious fever , bleeding and extreme lethargy , and in very serious cases can be fatal . <p> There is no vaccine against dengue and much of the research into the disease has focused on trying to control its spread . <p> The disease is found locally in far north Queensland , where it is spread by a species of mosquito called Aedes aegypti , however it had not spread further south because conditions are not right for the mosquitoes . <p> Loading <p> In particular the research paper highlights a cluster of dengue cases in the Brisbane area in 2012-2013 , however there was no wider outbreak because Aedes aegypti are not found as far south as Brisbane . <p> But Professor Wu said as the effects of climate change are felt , there is a strong possibility Aedes aegypti will start to be seen further south , meaning isolated outbreaks from Bali tourists returning home would become more dangerous . <p> " At the moment in Brisbane for example we do n't have dengue here , but in the future we do n't know , " he said . <p> " If aegypti gets here then you would see the disease rise in prevalence ; at the moment we have relatively high levels of the disease but we 're lucky that the mosquito is n't here yet . " <p> " The life cycle of dengue viruses and the growth and survival of dengue mosquitoes is inextricably linked with climate . " <p> It comes after Rockhampton saw an outbreak of dengue fever earlier this year , the first confirmed case for nearly three decades , prompting a " full outbreak response " from Queensland health authorities .
