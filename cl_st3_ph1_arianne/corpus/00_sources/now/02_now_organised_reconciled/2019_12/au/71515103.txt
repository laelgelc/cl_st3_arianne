<h> The five biggest issues facing Australian football as it enters a new decade <h> Unite the Tribes <p> Saying Australian football is fractured is akin to saying it 's a tad warm right now . <p> The final years of football 's decade were defined by infighting and venomous point-scoring among its bitterly divided stakeholders ; with the eventual , hard-won reforms resulting in a total upheaval of the way the sport is governed . <p> Even when parties were seemingly acting for the good of the game , a closer examination would all too often reveal that their actions were born not of altruism but , instead , through a brief convergence of an ulterior motive and greater good . <p> Yet hope springs eternal . <p> The A-League and W-League are now independent , all bar one -- chairman Chris Nikou -- of the FFA board is relatively fresh and the FFA has a new chief executive in James Johnson . <p> A potential clean(ish) slate . <p> The Board of Football Federation Australia today confirmed the appointment of respected international football executive James Johnson as its new Chief Executive Officer.https : //t.co/Fo9FmZak5l <p> Ultimately , as a relatively small player in a global market , some challenges Australian football can not overcome . <p> However unlikely , a united game -- where stakeholders unite to pull together for a better future -- can still accomplish great things . <h> An independent A-League and W-League <p> Long agitated for by its members , Australia 's professional leagues will complete their divorce from the FFA in the coming years in a move hoped to bring about a roaring 20s . <p> While there remain some contractual complications to iron out , the clubs are now tasked with -- after talking the talk for so long -- walking the walk and reinvigorating Australia 's domestic competitions . <p> Flagging TV ratings and attendances will need to be addressed , as will a rapidly evolving landscape in which streaming and non-traditional broadcasters are flexing their muscles and sponsorship is evolving . <p> Philosophical decisions about the direction of the competitions will also need to be wrestled with . They include : <p> Is the salary cap long for this world ? <p> What type of football do we want to play ? <p> When is the next round of expansion ? <p> Is the W-League a partner or a competitor of the NWSL ? <p> Is the A-League a developmental league ? <p> What is our role in Asia ? <p> Perhaps most important , is the question of a national second division and promotion and relegation . <p> A thriving , multi-tiered footballing pyramid with promotion and relegation is the best path for Australia to go down to create better footballers and , by extension , a better product . <p> But commercial realities must also be taken into account to ensure that expansion of the footballing ecosystem does no harm on a macro level . <p> Second divisions and promotion/relegation remain frustratingly divisive issues in Australian football and it 's important that its proponents get it right . <p> An implementation in a haphazard manner that collapses in on itself will damage the concept enough to ensure it is n't a serious prospect for generations . <h> The 2022 FIFA World Cup <p> Qualifying for the 2006 World Cup gave football its biggest jolt of mainstream recognition ever -- The Golden Generation creating a wave of momentum that took the game to new heights . <p> World Cup qualification has since become a key plank in football 's ongoing strategy , as it banks upon the sizeable reputational boost brought by the world 's biggest sporting event once every four years . <p> In a nation where A-League games very rarely attract more than 50,000 viewers , the Socceroos World Cup clash with France in 2018 attracted 3.4 million eyeballs to SBS . <p> Fortunately -- regardless of how misguided an idea it is -- the expansion of the World Cup to 48 teams in 2026 means that Australia 's path is soon to become simpler . <p> Yet , that still leaves Qatar 2022 . <p> At a time when the game is struggling for domestic relevance among the wider sporting public , Australian football ca n't afford to miss qualification for the first time in almost 20 years . <h> The 2023 Women 's World Cup <p> If people like the Socceroos , they love the Matildas . <p> After 136,000 fans tuned into their opening match of the 2015 World Cup , 570,000 watched their opener against Italy this year and , despite crashing out in the round of 16 , the Matildas remain Australia 's most-loved sporting team . <p> There is a palpable sense of momentum around the women 's game -- and there would be no better way to capitalise on it than by bringing the 2023 Women 's World Cup to Australia and New Zealand . <p> While issues of skullduggery in the bidding process are a concern -- it is a FIFA tournament after all -- the potential legacy benefits to bringing the tournament Down Under are clear . <p> Lasting improvements to facilities , engagement and goodwill can continue to drive participation in the sport by young girls -- an increasingly important battlefield for codes . <p> Hosting the tournament would also provide a significant boost to the popularity of the W-League and bolster its journey towards much needed stand-alone commercial viability . <h> Being a global citizen <p> Despite Australian football 's talent for navel-gazing being without equal , it has an important part to play as a global citizen . <p> Ongoing climate change presents an intrinsic threat to the summer-staged A-League and W-League 's viability and football 's role as the most popular sport on the planet instils with it a moral obligation to use its voice in the drive for action .
