<h> Bodhi Farm and other countercultural communities live like the 1970s never ended <p> It is just after sunset and someone has blown the conch shell to summon residents to the weekly Sunday community dinner . <p> Painted by moonlight , silhouetted figures begin to appear in the door frames of houses and strolling up the gravel road . <p> Soon we 're sharing a meal -- vegetarian -- with around 20 others in a large , hand-built , hexagonal building that serves as the community centre . <p> Later , as dishes are dutifully washed and dried , a guitar and mandolin are picked up and folk songs begin to reverberate around the room . <p> Voices break into familiar harmonies as the words of Neil Young and Joni Mitchell resonate around the hall . <p> You 'd be forgiven for thinking this was a scene from the 1970s , save for the lyrics being read from a smartphone screen held by Michael Pawson , whose son and grandchildren are joining in the singalong . <p> How the world has changed . <p> This is Bodhi Farm , an intentional community in northern NSW . And if it seems straight out of the 1970s , that 's because it is . <p> Bodhi Farm residents gather each Sunday for dinner in the community centre . Photo : Heather Faulkner <h> The 1970s meets 2019 <p> The community started in 1977 with aspirations to share land in what long-term resident and community archivist Gai Longmuir terms " voluntary simplicity " -- a " thoughtful and reflective way to live collectively " in harmony with the natural environment . <p> It was also founded on an adoption of the five Buddhist precepts : Do no harm , do not steal , do not lie , do not misuse sex and do not consume alcohol or drugs . <p> The residents sometimes joke that they were " farmers of enlightenment " . <p> Communities like this were once dismissively labelled as " hippy communes " , yet we are currently facing twin challenges of environmental destruction and unaffordable housing . <p> Bodhi Farm is not typical of Australia 's ' 70s countercultural communities in its longevity ( or its spiritual foundations ) . <p> But neither is it the only one to flourish and endure . <p> Gai Longmuir says the farm is a " thoughtful and reflective way to live collectively " . Photo : Supplied <p> While utopian ' 70s communities were widely regarded as failures , they 've gone on to inform and influence the design of modern eco-villages , the tiny house movement , urban intentional communities and myriad minor movements in between . <p> Now , in 2019 , Bodhi Farm residents have a handsome communal building where meals and meetings are regularly hosted . <p> Decisions are made through a communal consensus process . <p> The community produces its own power via a solar and micro-hydro system and is proudly off the grid . <p> It has its own spring-fed water supply . <p> The houses have been mostly hand-built with recycled materials , usually over a long period of time , and are set in a beautiful regenerated forest . <p> And they tend to have cost hundreds , rather than hundreds of thousands , to build . <p> Multiple generations live together , and the kids have free run of a beautiful rainforest " backyard " . <p> A 1980 photo of one of the original homes at Bodhi Farm in northern NSW . Photo : Col James <p> The house has since been painted and has gained a few improvements , including solar panels . Photo : Heather Faulkner <p> The environmental costs of conventional urban housing construction are also becoming clearer . <p> And contemporary eco-villages can sometimes be no less expensive than their urban counterparts . <p> So , Bodhi Farm 's eco-village-type qualities have a lot of appeal . <p> Gai 's house , viewed from the back . Photo : Supplied <h> Early days were ' not quite legal ' <p> But it was hard work for the community to get to this position . <p> For one , what the young dreamers at Bodhi were doing in 1977 was not quite legal . <p> Neighbouring Tuntable Falls intentional community residents recall that their application for multiple occupancy ( or MO , as it is colloquially termed ) hinged on a working toilet . <p> Their innovative installation of a composting toilet swayed a weary government inspector in their favour . <p> The land itself was , as Gai describes it , " ravaged " . It was logged and had at some point been used to grow bananas . <p> Loo with a view : a composting toilet at Bodhi Farm . Photo : Heather Faulkner <p> Where rainforest now stands , was once a barren hill , full of " bladey grass " . <p> " It was very marginal land , " says Gai . " It was cheap . " <p> While experiments in communal living blossomed in 1970s Australia , they were by no means mainstream . <p> Often dismissed as drop-out hippies , communities like Bodhi Farm found their desires to live a different kind of life were not always understood or tolerated by locals . <p> " Have they told you about the baby stealing , " quipped one Bodhi original resident , referring to one of the many rumours that were circulated about the community . <p> Many locals thought their new and sometimes naked neighbours were dirty and uneducated . It was far from the truth . <p> University educated and widely travelled , they may not have been trained architects or gardeners , but they brought with them philosophies of community -- many had returned from experiences in India -- that were foreign to rural Australians , and they were determined for their buildings to enable the kind of society they wanted to create . <p> " When I first went travelling as a 21 year-old , " says Gai , " I vividly remember flying over what was then Portuguese Timor and looking down at what would have been the approaches to Dili . " <p> The simple thatched houses amid the bougainvillaea , replete with children running among the structures , resonated with her . <p> " I had a visceral sense of it being a pattern I really liked -- the pattern of village and community , " she says . <p> Community garden caretaker Michael Pawson . Photo : Supplied <p> " I had done a long Buddhist meditation retreat , and that made me look for a spiritual community -- not just an ordinary lefty community " , adds Michael , who joined Bodhi Farm in 1978 and is the current resident caretaker of the community garden ( and a doting grandfather ) . <p> With no electricity and no real houses , the community started in tents , trailers , a converted bus , and then built more-robust dwellings when amenities and supplies were available -- hence the recycled materials . <p> Michael 's home resembles a castle . <p> " I wanted to have an interesting house because I wanted to have an interesting life , " says Michael . <p> What started out as a lean-to in 1978 ( " We just put up four poles on a floor and a roof and did n't really have walls , " he says ) was expanded when children began arriving . <p> But when a large tree branch took out a corner of the house during roofing repairs , Michael spotted water damage . <p> " Timber 's too tasty to termites , " he says . And so , he decided to build out of cement . <p> Based on a design he sketched , the building began to take shape . <p> " Brick by brick , barrow by barrow , " was the way to go , says Michael , who built the house himself as he could afford materials . <p> " I did n't really have plans , " he admits , " I just sort of launched into it and problem-solved as I went along . " <p> Building stopped when Michael suffered a ruptured aneurysm . <p> By that time , the place needed lifting and restumping -- a task Michael was no longer able to do himself . <p> But ingenuity seems to be Michael 's forte ; He simply excavated underneath the structure and restumped it that way . <p> " I did n't quite finish it , " says Michael of the house . Though he hopes to get back to it in the future , he is realistic about it : " The arthritis is kicking in now " . <p> The threat of building inspections weighed heavily on residents ' minds . <p> Not much was built to code in the early days and they relied on the goodwill of council inspectors to continue . <p> Most of the early residents did n't have jobs . They pooled their money to buy materials for the community . <p> Cars were shared and maintained by a self-taught mechanic . <p> " We were quite young , " says Gai of the sparseness of Bodhi 's early days . <p> " I think we were very undemanding of what life might serve us , and grateful for what we got . " <p> It 's a sentiment that , with 21st century hindsight , " almost makes me want to cry " , she says . <p> The largest boulder in this photo fell from the cliff above last year , damaging a column of this Bodhi Farm house . Photo : Heather Faulkner <h> It takes a village ... <p> Then came the children . The challenges of a growing community were exhausting . <p> Though the socio-economic conditions at the time made it possible to establish the settlements -- through allowing some residents to remain on the dole -- raising families was incredibly difficult . <p> A commitment to extended forms of kinship often helped . <p> The adage that " it takes a village to raise a child " was almost literally applied as kids roamed the community , looked after by whichever adult was present . <p> People also began to go outside to find work to support their growing families , and facilities such as electricity and water . <p> " In our increasingly fragmented society , the bonding and continuity that we have been able to create for ourselves , the sense of place and mutual protection , is , for me , like a deep anchor to life , " she says . <p> The challenges facing Bodhi Farm today are a far cry from those faced in ' 77 . Photo : Heather Faulkner <h> The impact of age <p> The challenges facing Bodhi Farm today are a far cry from those faced in ' 77 . <p> They have safe , comfortable homes ( " Do n't forget to unlock the door on your way out " is the motto here ) . <p> And they even have wi-fi ( how else to share the lyrics for a song written nearly 50 years ago ? ) . <p> Bush trails have been tamed into level roads . It is quaint , it is comfortable and yet , its future is uncertain . <p> The original founders -- those who have stayed on -- are ageing . This is no Shangri-La . <p> At the monthly community meeting we attended , attracting new people was a top item on their talking board . <p> The cost to join the community is well-below current market trends , though repairs and maintenance will be expected to be footed by new community members . <p> But it 's not an easy entry into the community . There is a two-year trying-out period to settle into Bodhi Farm . <p> They are careful about who they welcome as a permanent resident . <p> Someone will be needed to maintain the electricity grid , powered by the sun and creek flows . <p> The system still needs maintenance , and a brilliant mind to upgrade its performance as technologies change . <p> Climate change is on their minds as well . <p> Shifting weather patterns are making the once-abundant creek-water supply more tenuous . No rain means no water . <p> There are other challenges , such as ongoing needs for carpentry , mechanics and child care that the community must solve together . <p> But they know from more than 40 years of living experience at Bodhi Farm , that change is inevitable , but not new .
