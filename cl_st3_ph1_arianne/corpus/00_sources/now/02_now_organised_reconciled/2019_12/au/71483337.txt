<h> The good , bad and ugly of corporate Australia in 2019 <p> Sometimes the numbers do n't tell the whole story . The ASX 200 might have risen 19 per cent since the start of the year , but there would be few chief executives who will look back on 2019 with any great fondness . <p> A sluggish economy , political uncertainty caused by the federal election and persistent crises in energy and climate change weighed heavily on the entire economy . <p> It 's been another very hard year for corporate Australia . David Rowe <p> And as usual there was no shortage of companies that shot themselves in the foot . <p> Here 's the good , bad and ugly of corporate Australia in 2019 . <h> The good <h> Related Quotes <p> Advertisement <p> Dulux says sayonara with massive multiple <p> As corporate dramas go , the listed life of paint giant Dulux Group was about as exciting as watching its product dry . That was until April , when Japanese giant Nippon Paint paid the highest earnings multiple ever seen in the paint sector to buy Dulux in a $4.2 billion deal . The price made Dulux one of the top three performing ASX 200 investments of the past decade , and ensured chief executive Pat Houlihan will have his portrait done in Brunswick Green and hung on the walls of plenty of fund managers . <p> Little Mac leaves BHP in rude health <p> BHP chairman Ken MacKenzie ( Big Mac ) has plenty to thank outgoing chief executive Andrew Mackenzie ( Little Mac ) for : a slimmed-down asset portfolio , an investor base well fed on fat dividends , and the sort of baton-change with incoming boss Mike Henry that most boards can only dream about . Mackenzie is a fascinating figure . He 's a deep thinker on big issues such as trade , science and geopolitics who 's also proven fabulously focused on turning BHP into a cash flow-generating machine . <p> Tech unicorns deliver global success <p> The world might have turned a little sour on tech companies towards the end of the year -- who knew that investors might actually value something as boring as earnings -- but Australian start-ups continue to do impressive things . Employee survey company Culture Amp became our latest unicorn in September with a $US82 million funding round valuing it at $1.04 billion , while design platform Canva was valued at a staggering $4.7 billion in October . <p> Lendlease Googles a brighter future <p> Construction and engineering group Lendlease has had a torrid few years , weighed down by impairments and write-downs stemming from its engineering and services unit , which is now on the sale block . But in July the group delivered the sort of urbanisation project that underscores what CEO Steve McCann has been trying to do with the group -- a $21 billion , decade-plus deal to help transform Google 's landholdings in California into mixed-use communities . These are the big city-shaping projects that Lendlease is increasingly focused on , and that 's helped investors - who have pushed the stock up 63 per cent in the last 12 months - forget about those engineering woes . <p> Advertisement <p> JB Hi-Fi defies the gloom <p> Retail is an unforgiving industry at the best of times , but right now it 's a sector where only the best can shine . Richard Murray , chief executive of JB Hi-Fi , is certainly that . Having spent most of the year as the target of short sellers , Murray 's ability to keep JB 's margins largely intact in the face of intense competition , a spluttering economy and changing consumer trends saw the company 's shares trade at record levels late in the year . <h> The bad <p> NAB 's royal commission roasting <p> Former NAB chairman Ken Henry would have known he was unlikely to receive plaudits in the banking royal commission 's final report after his controversial appearance in the witness box in November 2018 . But he and former chief executive Andrew Thorburn would never have thought they would be so brutally singled out by Commissioner Kenneth Hayne , who said they had left him unconvinced " that NAB is willing to accept the necessary responsibility for deciding , for itself , what is the right thing to do " . The pair stepped down three days later , although the eventual appointments of Phil Chronican as chairman and Ross McEwan as chief executive reassured investors . <p> Latitude fails to get altitude <p> Perhaps the mystery of what killed the Latitude Financial float will one day be answered in a business school case study . Was it the $20-odd million worth of shares to be paid to chief executive Ahmed Fahour on completion ? The hefty $4 billion price tag put on the company ? The fact it was floated by a private equity firm ? Worries about the state of the economy and household indebtedness ? The answer lies somewhere in between , but what 's really fascinating is what might happen next . A third float attempt looks unlikely , but will a private bidder be brave enough to buy a business twice rejected by ASX investors ? <p> AMP pours on more pain <p> Advertisement <p> It 's hard to argue too much with the rescue plan put forward by AMP chief Francesco De Ferrari in August . He 's aiming to drive growth from AMP Capital , the remaining jewel in the AMP crown , and , to a lesser extent , AMP 's bank . He 's got rid of AMP 's life insurance business and finally attacked some of the group 's legacy problem in wealth , such as agreements with advisers that guaranteed rich deals when they left the industry . He 's also committed to finding smart digital models to deliver advice , although this plan is vaguer . But it ca n't be forgotten that the future is very uncertain for investors , who end the year holding a stock worth 21 per cent less than in January , and with dividend payments suspended indefinitely . <p> TPG stares into an abyss <p> The Federal Court fight between the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission and TPG Telecom over the regulator 's decision to block TPG 's merger with Vodafone Hutchison Australia was great theatre , mainly because of the appearance of TPG 's reclusive billionaire founder , David Teoh . Two clear lessons flowed from the court case . First , Teoh is a maverick in the true sense of the word , and his business empire has been built on instinct rather than careful planning . Second , if this merger does n't go ahead , TPG is in huge trouble . The trial exposed its now abandoned plan to build a fourth mobile network as weaker than a mobile signal in a meat locker , while its enterprise division is hardly going to stem the losses from the NBN 's march into fixed-line broadband . TPG 's fate is in the hands of Justice John Middleton . <p> Investment banking 's big dogs lose bite <p> No one is going to cry too much for Australia 's investment bankers , but it 's hard to deny this has been an incredible year of change . First , in April , UBS star Guy Fowler announced his retirement , followed in July by the bank 's legendary country head , Matthew Grounds . This came days before Deutsche Bank announced the end of its equities trading business in Australia , while in September Credit Suisse farewelled John Knox . The exists occurred as the investment bank sector faces crushing pressure on fees , increasing compliance burdens , high levels of competition and , in Australia , the shift by the ever-growing super funds industry to bring everything in-house . It 's hard to see any of these trends reversing . <h> The ugly <p> Regulators hold Crown Resort 's fate <p> Just follow the share price to see the roller-coaster Crown Resorts investors have been on . The stock opened the year just below $12 , and then in April spiked to about $14 after it was revealed US casino giant Wynn was mulling a takeover . Those talks evaporated in a day , but in late May , major shareholder James Packer announced the sale of a 19.99 per cent stake in the business to Hong Kong casino group Melco International -- this time at $13 a share . <p> Advertisement <p> But by the middle of August the stock had fallen to $11.15 after major allegations about links between criminal elements and the junket operators who bring high rollers to Crown . The company strenuously denied the claims , but faces a public inquiry by the NSW Independent Liquor and Gaming Authority that will examine both the suitability of Melco boss Lawrence Ho to own shares and sit on Crown 's board , and the allegations about Crown 's operations . The stock ends the year around $12.22 , but next year looks like being equally dramatic . <p> Westpac 's accountability failure <p> It 's remarkable to think that the spectacular departures of NAB 's Ken Henry and Andrew Thorburn could be topped in less than 11 months . But that 's exactly what happened when Westpac 's outgoing chairman Lindsay Maxsted and former chief executive Brian Hartzer fell on their swords after the bank became embroiled in an ugly scandal over claims that it allowed paedophiles to send money to countries considered to be at high risk for child exploitation . The facts of the allegations were bad enough , but what really cost Hartzer and Maxsted were the gaps in accountability ; Westpac 's board and senior leaders still do n't know how regulatory warnings on the payments were n't acted on . <p> Boral 's foggy windows <p> Westpac is n't the only company that ends the year with a major investigation under way . Late on December 5 , Boral chief executive Mike Kane revealed that accounting irregularities had emerged in the group 's North American window business around inventory levels and raw materials . The scandal delayed detection for some 14 months , apparently because so many different people were in on it . But coming after four profit downgrades in 18 months at Boral , it has raised big questions about the company 's direction . <p> Costa 's operations are , of course , suffering from a brutal drought . But it must shoulder at least some of the blame for intense competition in most of its key markets due to oversupply . Chief executive Harry Debney expects conditions to improve as the weather hopefully improves and smaller , weaker players are forced out of the market . But after watching the stock plummet 64 per cent in 2019 , investors will be watching closely for more signs that the fruits of its recovery could wither on the vine . <p> James Thomson is a Chanticleer columnist at The Australian Financial Review based in Melbourne .
