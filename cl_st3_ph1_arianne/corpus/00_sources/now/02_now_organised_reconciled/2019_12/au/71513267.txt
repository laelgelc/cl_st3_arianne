<h> More <h> A Nobel laureate 's guide to climate-proofing your investments <p> Nobel laureate Robert Engle has some advice for investors who are concerned about climate change wiping out the value of their assets , but are wary of anaemic returns on " ethical " funds : read the news . <p> Or , more precisely , train analytics algorithms on one trillion newspaper articles and social media posts , develop indices for public alarm about climate change , and use them to form hedge portfolios . <p> NYU professor Robert Engle won the Nobel Prize in 2003 for his work on volatility . Michele Mossop <p> The idea is to identify which stocks rose and fell when there was " bad news " about the climate , and develop a mimicking portfolio of long and short positions in response . <p> The bet is that news is a better proxy for investor alarm about climate change , which contributes to the volatility that drives price changes , than environmental measures such as rising temperatures . <p> Professor Engle told the Australian Finance and Banking Conference that a sample of 100 portfolios hedged against climate risk showed only a handful with excess returns above the benchmark index over a five-year period . <p> But in the past year , over a third of surveyed portfolios had positive returns over their benchmarks . <p> " There is evidence that climate is getting worse , and consequently we would expect that to show up as a positive alpha in these hedge portfolios , " he said . <p> But whereas alternative portfolios hedged against climate risk by taking " industry bets " -- long green energy , and short fossil fuels -- Professor Engle and his co-authors ' approach identified specific opportunities within and across all sectors . <p> The resulting portfolios were more diversified and , at least in one instance , performed " substantially better " than funds based on exchange-traded funds . <p> ' ' There are a lot of investment products that are ESG-based ... But it 's not quite clear whether it 's doing anything , or whether it 's doing what you want it to , ' ' Professor Engle said . <p> Advertisement <p> They also contained some surprising long positions -- for example , in tobacco products , paper products , and textile mill products -- that meant capital diverted from carbon-intensive companies was not necessarily going to those working on climate mitigation . <p> Professor Engle acknowledged , while discussing a different hedge portfolio , that a portfolio that was " well-positioned to withstand climate change " was not necessarily " what you think of as an environmental portfolio " . <h> Revenue-neutral tax <p> The economist and New York University professor , who was awarded the Nobel Prize in 2003 for his work on volatility , supports a revenue-neutral carbon price , which balances out the increased tax intake with cuts and concessions in other , ideally regressive and inefficient , taxes . <p> His arguments in favour of pricing carbon are based on free market principles , tempered by concerns about free-rider problems and unpriced externalities that prevail in the absence of a pricing intervention . <p> " The market would be able to give us the best possible solution to this problem ... The trouble with regulation and subsidies is you have to foresee what is going to be the winning solution , " Professor Engle said . <p> The Nobel laureate acknowledged that the physical risks of climate change may precede the transition risks that would arise from government intervention . <p> Advertisement <p> But he concluded that the lessons of finance may have something to teach policymakers and investors about decision-making in the absence of full information . <p> " If you think about it as a risk management problem , uncertainty does n't mean do nothing . It means having a plan that 's appropriate for how you see the risks , " Professor Engle said . <p> " When you 're driving on a foggy day , or a smoky day , and you see a light flashing in the distance , you do n't know what it is , you hit the brakes . You do n't go full steam ahead . "
