<p> A significant marker of rising global greenhouse gas emissions has been passed , with a key monitoring site on Tasmania 's north-west tip recording atmospheric carbon-dioxide exceeding 400 parts per million for the first time . <p> As it turned out , " the unfortunate milestone " was reached on Tuesday May 10 at 8am , local time , said Paul Krummel , who heads the CSIRO team analysing data from the most important site in the southern hemisphere . ( See chart below . ) <p> Cape Grim , one of the world 's most important monitoring stations for gauging changes in the **25;1491;TOOLONG <p> Atmospheric readings from Cape Grim , along with two stations in Hawaii and Alaska , are closely watched as they date back decades and closely track a range of pollutants from ozone-depleting chemicals to the various greenhouse gases resulting from burning fossil fuels and clearing forests . <p> Mr Krummel said that while mostly symbolic , the 400-ppm reading " highlights the problem of rising emissions , which are increasing more rapidly than they used to be " . <p> A report out earlier this year from the World Meteorological Organization noted atmospheric readings of CO2 at the Mauna Loa site in Hawaii rose 3.05 ppm in 2015 alone -- the biggest increase in the 56 years of research . <p> The recent surge in CO2 levels was not unexpected because of the giant El Nino event now breaking up in the Pacific . In El Nino years , global temperatures get a kick higher and droughts tend to be worse . As a result , vegetation take out less CO2 from the atmosphere . <p> The Climate Change Commission recommends a type of emissions trading scheme that would push up prices.Credit:Michele Mossop <p> CSIRO 's Mr Krummel said the El Nino influence was evident in Cape Grim 's reading with barely a dip in the past year as might otherwise be expected in spring . <p> " This year , it 's just plateaued and now it 's taken off again , " he said , adding the site was " probably one of the last places on earth " to remain below 400 ppm . <p> Melting moments : A polar bear off the coast of Svalbard , Norway.Credit:Steven Kazlowski <p> Sites in the northern hemisphere exceeded 400 ppm from 2012 onwards .
