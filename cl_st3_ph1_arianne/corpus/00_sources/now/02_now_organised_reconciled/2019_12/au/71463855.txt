<h> More <h> Business braces for onset of climate lawfare <p> Kenneth Hayne 's warning to directors over climate risk wo n't change the inevitability of more climate lawfare internationally and domestically . The impact on business overwhelms the views of the Morrison government . <p> Kenneth Hayne 's warning to company directors about the need to respond to climate-change issues and record those responses in their financial reports is a statement of the legally obvious . <p> A range of regulators , both domestic and international , have already made the urgency of addressing this new responsibility clear to companies . <p> What is not at all clear is whether the form and extent of those responses will be considered adequate by any number of interested parties . Nor are arguments over relative costs and the interpretation of best interests clarified . And that inevitably means this issue will be repeatedly tested by courts and lawyers over years . <p> No matter how many boxes that boards tick , no matter what statements of intent they issue , no matter what actual changes they make , it will no longer be enough to ward off the prospect of legal action . That 's because Australian companies will be carried along on the same wave of international climate lawfare -- at least in Western countries -- against business for not doing enough to combat climate change . <p> And as Hayne 's own royal commission demonstrated , even if directors are now more cognisant of the need for a board to be seen to address this issue , it wo n't stop efforts to hold them to account for supposed past failures or to accuse them of not acting quickly enough . <h> Related Quotes <p> Advertisement <p> The rise of class actions in Australia forms just one flank in what is becoming a global assault . <p> In that sense , potential liability for failing to address climate change is just another of the amorphous risks now facing directors and the obligation to act in the best interests of the company . <p> Yet the passion surrounding climate change , including the determination of environmental activists to enforce radical change across industry , pushes this into another level . AFR <p> The Morrison government will fulminate about being in the " sensible centre " and declare -- as the Prime Minister did again on Tuesday -- that Australia always meets its commitments and its targets for emissions reduction . Such political logic may work for quiet Australians despite the increasing collective community angst about the need for everyone somehow to do more . Business logic is more complicated still . <p> It 's one reason why boards like BHP 's ignored Canberra 's irritated reaction to talk about reducing the Scope 3 emissions produced by its customers as part of its own assessment of the company 's best interests . Other prominent resources companies do n't agree but all now stress their commitment to reducing emissions and increasing energy efficiency . <p> Yet the legal challenges will keep coming . That is despite the uncertainty of the results and the slippery concept of assigning individual corporate culpability for climate change . <p> One industry super fund , REST , for example , is being sued by a member claiming the fund breached its fiduciary duties by failing to adequately consider climate change risks in its investments . The trial is due to start next year . It may not get that far , or REST may win the case . <p> But the threat of legal action or investor reaction is still enough to deter many companies from bothering with potential costs and potent criticisms . Witness the decision of Australian banks to retreat from funding coal projects . <p> In Europe , Royal Dutch Shell , for example , is facing a legal suit from not-for-profit groups and thousands of individuals for allegedly pursuing a business model that undermines the Paris agreement . <p> In the US , the New York Attorney-General took ExxonMobil to court over allegations of securities fraud by hiding the true extent of its financial exposure to laws targeting emissions . <p> According to the suit , the company provided " false and misleading assurances " to shareholders that it had properly factored in the cost of increasing climate regulation and that its vast reserves of oil and gas were n't at risk of becoming stranded assets . This is just one of hundreds of climate change lawsuits filed against companies in the US but one of the first to go to trial . A judgment is expected shortly . <p> That global legal minefield puts Hayne 's views about Australian businesses into perspective . He commends the continuing work of the international Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures for showing all directors what they should do . <p> Advertisement <p> No doubt Morrison would be more likely to consider this body an example of the " negative globalism " promulgated by an " unaccountable internationalist bureaucracy " that he condemned in his speech to the United Nations in September . <p> But international climate legal action is increasingly forcing rapid adaptation in the approach of major companies as well as overwhelming the policies of national governments . <p> Short of the government magically altering Australian corporations law to somehow pre-empt legal and investor challenges , Australian companies are unlikely to be exempt from coming legal battles over climate change action . <p> Global companies , particularly resources companies , are already subject to much larger commercial forces than Canberra -- including the growing skittishness of international investors . <p> The government 's frustration is obvious .
