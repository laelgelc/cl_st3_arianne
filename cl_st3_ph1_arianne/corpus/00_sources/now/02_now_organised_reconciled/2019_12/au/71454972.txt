<h> ' I 'm a sceptic by nature ' : Lunch with Tim Entwisle <p> Those not familiar with Professor Tim Entwisle , director and chief executive of Melbourne 's Royal Botanic Gardens and one of Australia 's leading botanists , might imagine his expertise might have a singular focus . <p> But Entwisle is quite the Renaissance man . <p> Radio listeners might know him as the one-time host of ABC Radio National 's Talking Plants , or discussing broader topics on RN 's Blueprint for Living program , or for his entertaining blog . But you might also have read one of his articles in The Skeptic magazine , or been in the mosh pit with him at a gig at The Tote in Collingwood . <p> We 're at Hellenic Republic in Kew , near Entwisle 's home , and for a Friday it 's conspicuously empty . ( I 'm disappointed he does n't live in a groundskeeper house inside the Gardens , and later impressed to learn that when he worked at Kew Gardens in England , he did -- " in a Georgian cottage from the 1730s " ) . <p> Entwisle knows about the problems with the George Calombaris-owned restaurant , where he and his wife Lynda were occasional visitors . <p> Advertisement <p> Tim Entwisle is something of a Renaissance man.Credit:Eddie Jim <p> " We stopped coming when there was ... uncertainty about whether the staff were being paid properly , " he says , tactfully , " but presumably the staff are now being paid . I thought we should support them . " <p> We do n't broach the issue with the cheerful waiter who talks us through the menu . Entwisle is keen on the grain salad , which he 's been trying to re-create at home , but decided on a sprouting lentil salad . <p> " Well this has ruined the whole plan , " he says , ordering the lentil salad anyway , and the seared tuna . <p> " I should confess -- I 'm a small-time cook , " he says . " My wife does the very unfair balance of the cooking . But I like it on weekends . " <p> The tuna at Hellenic Republic.Credit:Eddie Jim <p> I settle on the lunch special of roast chicken with lemon and roast potatoes , and a Greek wine , while Entwisle -- wisely -- orders a King Valley pinot grigio . <p> A respected scientist , Entwisle , who has been director of the Gardens in the city and Cranbourne since 2013 , after stints at Kew Gardens and the Sydney Botanic Gardens , is also an honorary Fellow at Melbourne University 's School of Botany and a world expert in freshwater algae . <p> He even has an alga named after him . Discovered by one of Entwisle 's former University lecturers , Entwisleia bella grows only on submerged rocks in an estuary of Tasmania 's Derwent River . It is so rare there are less than a dozen known specimens . <p> " It 's also very pretty , " Entwisle says , showing me a picture on his phone . " When you see it dried up on the beach , or slimy in water it 's not , but once you put it under a microscope , you see an entirely different thing . " <p> Not merely a new species , the Entwisleia is a new genus , " not related to anything else on Earth , " Entwisle enthuses , adding that it 's like discovering the first primate . <p> The lemon-roasted chicken lunch special.Credit:Eddie Jim <p> It 's not just marine plant life that sparks ( genuinely infectious ) enthusiasm in Entwisle -- he loves all manner of plant life . <p> He 's the patron of the Cactus and Succulent Society Australia , even though he 's no " expert " . <p> " The fact you can take a tiny piece and pop it into a pot and grow something ... I like that sense of surprise , " he says . In my university days there was talk of students sneaking into the Gardens to sample the peyote , known for its psychedelic properties . But Entwisle says they were " mostly grown behind glass " . <p> " I 've grown a peyote at home , " he says . ( No , he has n't tried it . ) <p> He does n't have a big garden at home -- he grows veggies in pots -- and has a few indoor plants . <p> He sees the recent spike in indoor plants as a good thing , attracting younger people to the joy of plants . Plant fashions come and go , he says , when we talk about the sudden ubiquity of the fiddle-leaf fig . <p> " I think there 's a risk when you 're a botanist that you become a plant elitist -- you start to think some plants are better than others , you start to look down on a geranium . " <p> But they grow in dust ! Surely a good thing in our climate ? " I 'm coming around to them now , " he concedes . <p> The only plants he will diss are those with variegated leaves , but says " there are n't really plants that I hate " . <p> There 's been an increase in nature writing and literature about trees too , but he 's not convinced by some , particularly those that discuss the way trees " communicate " . " I love that people are looking for new ways to look at plants , but I 'm also a sceptic by nature , " he says . <p> He 's reviewed such books for The Skeptic magazine , pointing out he does n't think we need to anthropomorphise plants . " Plants are fascinating and the way they communicate is fascinating , but I think the mistake we make is trying to turn them into us , " he says . <p> " Plants are living on this planet in an entirely different way than the way we do , " he says . " They do n't talk , they do n't have a brain , and they interact with the world in all kinds of odd and interesting ways -- that , to me , is the interesting thing . They produce sugar from the sun ! " <p> This is the kind of work " on the edge " of gardening that Entwisle enjoys , and he 's applying this approach to the job ; as a lover of Melbourne 's arts scene , he 's been integrating the Gardens into the city 's cultural landscape . <p> In 2018 , the Gardens hosted Fire Gardens , a display of firelight and kinetic sculptures , as part of the Melbourne Festival , and next winter the just-announced Lightscape will take over 1.5kms in the gardens with luminous walkways , floating lights and a field of light . <p> Other recent events include poetry reading at the Writers Festival , wellness experiences and dozens of regular events that run year round . <p> It 's obviously working : last week the Botanic Gardens won the Major Tourist Attraction category at the Victorian Tourism Awards . <p> Receipt for lunch at Hellenic Republic . <p> He 's also transformed the century-old commemorative tree planting ceremony , once an honour extended largely to governors-general and visiting royalty , into a much hipper affair . <p> " In the past we 've had the gardens as a beautiful landscape , we do great science , we have this rich collection of plants , " he says , " but we have n't connected with the cultural life of Melbourne and I think , like the museum , like the gallery , we have a role to play . " <p> He hopes that role will be further enhanced by the plans for the new nature and science precinct , as part of the Gardens ' 20-year master plan , which includes the restoration of the 1934 Herbarium building , the creation of a new underground Herbarium , and the Melbourne Observatory . <p> " We have the arts precinct going down St Kilda Road , sport and entertainment across the river , so we see sort of a triangle -- our precinct will be in the middle . " <p> Loading <p> By the time we 've finished a fun , lengthy lunch , our conversation has been expansive , from horticultural myths ( spraying water on leaves during the day does n't burn them , he says , although he 'd not heard the one about pampas grass in British front yards being code that the residents are swingers : " I know it 's noxious though " ) , people shagging in the Botanic Gardens ( " We do n't encourage it , but we do n't kick them out " ) , literal tree-hugging ( " We do encourage that ! " ) , noisy pub and garage rock ( " I like to see new local bands , particularly from the Geelong-based Anti Fade stable , but other favourites at the moment are The Peep Tempel and Ty Segall ) , and climate change . <p> This is acutely felt in the Gardens , where some plants , like the rhododendrons , have been moved in order for them to thrive , and where there is a plan to plant more drought-tolerant species . <p> Trying to convey where Melbourne 's climate will be in 2070 or 2090 , Entwisle says it will be similar to Dubbo . With respect to the regional city , that sounds grim . " If you want somewhere more exotic , " says Entwisle , " like Algiers . " <p> As a scientist , does he despair at climate change denial ? <p> " I despair at people who are vocal deniers and try to change other people . I understand though , when people find it hard to accept ; it 's a big thing , " he says . " A lot of people are denying because it 's just impossible to comprehend . They 're the people we should work on . " <p> In some ways , he says , the biggest impact of climate change will be on humans and animals . <p> " The planet will continue on , it 's really our own interests we need to protect , " he says . " So if you want to be selfish about it , we should look after the planet . "
