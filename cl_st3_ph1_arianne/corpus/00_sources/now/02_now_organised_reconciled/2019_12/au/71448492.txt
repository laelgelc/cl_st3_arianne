According to the report , " Sydney and Melbourne could experience unprecedented 50C summer days by the end of the century . " <p> Some of the world 's brightest minds are working on solutions . They use words like " climate emergency " and tell us we need " urgent action " to minimise the toll on our planet and human lives . <p> But what does that look like ? And who can make it happen ? <h> First ... what 's the goal we need to achieve ? And how quickly ? <p> The general consensus among climate scientists is that we must limit the global temperature rise to 1.5 ? C. To achieve that , the world has to be at net-zero carbon emissions by 2050 . <p> But crucially , it 's cumulative emissions that count . In other words , if countries leave decarbonisation until the last minute , it will be too late . <p> As Thomas Nicholas of the University of York explained via The Conversation , " Even if we could snap our fingers on December 31 , 2049 , and replace all fossil fuel plants , the world would have already emitted twice as much carbon as the budget allows . Sound climate policy involves cutting emissions as soon as possible , and any further delay makes the task even harder . " <h> So what are some of the solutions to climate change ? <p> The following are some of the most common recommendations made by climate researchers and scientists if we want to achieve the 2050 goal . <h> End use of fossil fuels . <p> The burning of fossil fuels for energy and electricity is the largest contributor to greenhouse gas emissions . <p> Coal is the biggest villain here . It makes up 42 per cent of global emissions from fossil fuels , according to the Global Carbon Project . This is due to several factors , from clearing forests for farmland to raising livestock that produce methane . <p> Alessandro Demaio , a fellow in global health at the University of Copenhagen , recommends a " planetary health diet " that involves reducing meat intake in Western countries by half . His plan also involves halting the spread of agricultural land use , focusing investment on nutritious , sustainable food ( eg. drought-resistant crops ) and radically reducing food waste . <h> Governments must overhaul their economies . <p> All of the above requires governments to transform their economies and make significant financial commitments to decarbonisation . <p> This would mean investment in the renewable energy sector , including jobs and infrastructure , as well as consulting with workers to ensure a " just transition " from existing energy industries , like coal mining . That might involve job guarantees and universal basic services such as free public transport , for example . <p> Governments would also be expected to cut the billions they spend on subsidising the cost of the fossil fuel industry -- that is , injecting cash to keep the price of coal/electricity low . According to the International Monetary Fund , globally , US$5.2 trillion was spent on fossil fuel subsidies in 2017 . Our government forked out $29bn , which is equal to $1,198 for every Australian . <p> The IMF found that because of these subsidies , in 2015 , the price paid for coal was typically less than half of its true cost , and that if it had been priced appropriately , global carbon emissions would have been reduced by 28 per cent . 28 per cent ! <h> But what can you do ? <p> Reducing your household energy consumption and waste , purchasing local , sustainably produced products and food , and flying less are all great ways to help reduce your impact on the planet . <p> But the reality is that change on the scale and at the pace we need will be up to governments and business . <p> As an individual , you 're not entirely powerless in that . <p> Engaged communities are a crucial part of " urgent action " on climate change . After all , it 's unlikely governments will make any of the above happen without significant pressure from their citizens . <p> Educate yourself ( The Conversation , for example , has lots of easy-to-read articles by climate researchers and scientists ) .
