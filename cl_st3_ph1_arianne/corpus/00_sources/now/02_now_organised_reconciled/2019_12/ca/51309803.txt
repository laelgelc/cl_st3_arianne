<p> Members of a generation share a sense of history , a storyline on which they can place the present day . <p> Today 's stereotypical Boomer oldsters , for example , recall their generation 's victories over Communism , racism , sexism and homophobia , and imagine those oppressions are safely buried in the past . They see a modern world ordered by the rights and freedoms they fought to enshrine in law , from civil rights in America to Charter rights in Canada . They see this order threatened , however , by the doctrinaire overreach of the radicalized young , who have been ideologically trained not only to recognize old injustices , but to invent new ones , to lionize victims , and to parse the language of justice ever more finely until it vanishes into the dust of microaggressions , privilege , and pronouns that start with x . <p> This , at least , is Douglas Murray 's rough diagnosis in The Madness of Crowds , one of a recent spate of big idea books that sketch the contours of the generational culture wars , including also Meghan Daum 's The Problem With Everything and Robert Boyers ' The Tyranny of Virtue . <p> " Our public life is now dense with people desperate to man the barricades long after the revolution is over , " as Murray puts it in his description of how " legitimate human rights campaigns " in the last century such as feminism and racial equality went " through the crash barrier " and started demanding to be not just equal , but " better . " <p> " In each case a demonstration of virtue demands an overstating of the problem , which then causes an amplification of the problem , " Murray writes . <p> Got that , kids ? Everything is fine . <p> British author , journalist and political commentator Douglas Murray attends a photocall during the annual Edinburgh International Book Festival at Charlotte Square Gardens on August 13 , 2017 in Edinburgh , Scotland.Roberto Ricciuti/Getty Images <p> Describing generations is a bit like telling horoscopes . It is not science . It is impressionistic , subjective and context-dependent , like reading fortune cookies . But it can be weirdly convincing if you let it . Generations are more than just demographic cohorts . They are like signs of the Zodiac , each with a mood and a destiny that can only be fully divined if you suspend disbelief beyond all reason . When you do , though , familiar storylines fall into place , not only for history but for the future . <p> Gen Z youngsters see something different as they become adults in the Boomers ' retirement years . Stereotypical college kids today see high-principled laws that are skewed in practice against marginalized people . They see socioeconomic systems that perpetuate the same historical injustices their elders imagine have evaporated like a bad smell . They encounter resistance and condescension to their smallest efforts toward social justice , especially in the main Boomer blind spots of transgender and Indigenous rights . Their critical thinking is mocked as flighty indulgence , their newly coined words held up as evidence of cultish conformity in contrast to the Boomers ' plain spoken common sense and clear-eyed distaste for political correctness . Even the term " social justice " has been stolen and used to patronize these naive , brainwashed little " warriors . " <p> Ideologically , the generation gap has not seemed this wide since the 1960s , nearly a human lifespan ago , when the Boomers came of age with psychedelic fury and overthrew the stifling conformity of the retiring Greatest Generation , and not without a fight . <p> This dynamic is creating confusion and turbulence in diverse areas of the culture , from Hollywood to professional hockey , from campus politics to climate activism . Today , at the end of the twenty teens , the future is not what it used to be . <p> Signs are everywhere you look over the year just passed . Greta Thunberg , the teenage climate activist has become an improbable hero and an even less probable villain , and possibly the most generationally divisive character since U.S. President Donald Trump , who indulged in comically self-absorbed outrage when Time magazine named her Person of the Year . <p> Swedish climate activist Greta Thunberg is pictured after disembarking from the catamaran La Vagabonde at the Santo Amaro docks in Lisbon , on December 3 , 2019 . CARLOS COSTA/AFP via Getty Images <p> At the other end of the age/gender/political spectrum there is Don Cherry , as timelessly familiar to Canadian Boomers as Mansbridge and Robertson , but curious to the young , like an old couch that is somehow still in the living room after a renovation . <p> Cherry 's firing and the cancellation of Coach 's Corner marked an important moment because it was one of the unusual instances of online outrage actually having a real world effect . His career was marked by frequent outrageous bigotry against French and European hockey players , but it finally collapsed because of a rant about immigrants he imagines not wearing the poppy . This was just Cherry being himself . It did not make a lot of sense unless you saw it through a generational lens . Then you realized you were looking at a racist dinosaur in a suit made of curtains , yammering through the intermission about some culture war talking point that has nothing to do with hockey . Then it became clear it was long past time for some new furniture . <p> Something is changing , in hockey as with the rest of the world . It was evident in the stories that came out after Mike Babcock was fired as coach of the Toronto Maple Leafs , especially from Chris Chelios , a defenceman who played for Babcock in Detroit . He had horror stories of vindictive spite , of the sort that would get you banned from beer league , let alone house league . He described Babcock " verbally assaulting " Johan Franzen on the bench , deliberately scratching Mike Modano at the end of his final season in 2011 to prevent him reaching 1,500 career games , and not letting Jason Spezza play a season opener this year against his former team , the Ottawa Senators . <p> It is one thing to mock a weak-kneed social justice warrior for saying words are violence and authority figures are too mean . It is different when it is Chelios , a three-time Stanley Cup champion . <p> This shift in the power of victimhood and outrage can be baffling to older observers . <p> Babcock is a rock-jawed leader from central casting , the cultural archetype of a boss , an alpha male who gets results and whose strategic use of cruelty might once have been excused , in the long run , on utilitarian grounds . It might even have been excused earlier this year , before the Maple Leafs embarked on a losing streak . <p> Former Toronto Maple Leaf Head Coach Mike Babcock scrums at Leaf year end availability in Toronto on Thursday April 25 , 2019 . Craig Robertson/Toronto Sun/Postmedia Network <p> But that line of thinking is lately showing its age . Young people have seen the power of speaking up and calling out . On their own they might seem like snowflakes . As a generation they can be a blizzard . <p> The flip side is evidenced in episodes like the recent Pocahontas scandal at Toronto 's Ryerson University , in which the student union president presided over a Social Justice Week workshop called Generations of Resistance , where an international student described as " Indigenous on her own land " sang " Colors of the Wind " from the Disney movie Pocahontas as a way to describe her own " personal resilience . " <p> Another student politician who is Indigenous said the song " triggered " her and could have been " traumatic " to others because it reminded her of another song in the movie , called " Savages . " As reported by The Eyeopener newspaper , the student union president pledged to vet musical performances at social justice workshops in future to prevent such cultural appropriation . <p> Taking critical thinking this far has bolstered the common view among Boomers that young people have been trained to seek out ever more subtle and complicated injustices , especially about representation and identity , and to invent them where they fail . <p> The result , typically , is a dead-end conversation in which hollow outrage is mansplained away , and no one learns anything except that the other side is deluded about the state of the world . They are not even thinking and arguing in the same way . <p> The young fetishize personal experience , which has left them vulnerable to ideological hucksters shopping diverse sob stories in the campus grievance industry and on the delusion amplifier of Twitter . But the old fetishize logic and supposedly unquestionable and timeless facts , which has left them vulnerable to deception on the post-truth internet . <p> Emotions set them apart too . If the young are drowning in performative empathy , the old are parched . This was illustrated in Nova Scotia recently , after a young alumna of St. Francis Xavier University took offence on Facebook that the new Brian Mulroney Institute of Government features a quote from the former prime minister 's father : " The only way out of a paper mill town is through a university door . " <p> " As a social worker in training , I need to speak out against oppression and discrimination , " Meaghan Marie Landry wrote in defence of the residents of mill towns , including herself , who should not be regarded as " impoverished and embarassing . " <p> Now , of course , this was widely mocked . Getting out of mill towns and fishing villages is what maritime life was all about in Mulroney 's childhood . It still is . That is literally Sonny 's Dream . But it was not just the young person who complained who got mocked . The university itself was harangued for apologizing . To the young , it looked like a school had humbly listened to criticism and apologized for inadvertently causing minor offence . To the old , it looked like an uppity scold on Facebook bullied the school 's president into submission and debasement , when he should have fought back . <p> In the National Post , columnist John Robson evoked imagery from Greek mythology and the French Revolution to suggest this Facebook post was like the story of Cronus ( the Roman god Saturn ) devouring his children for fear they were destined to overthrow him , as his sixth child Zeus eventually did . His point was that , in the famous words of the 18th century French anti-Revolutionary journalist Jacques Mallet du Pan , " Like Saturn , the Revolution devours its children . " Everything and everyone has become offensive in the " brave new ' how dare you ' world we have created , " Robson wrote . <p> In the cultural orbit of America , there has been a cataclysm once every lifetime , almost like clockwork <p> Compelling as the imagery was , Robson gave the game away by using the pronoun " we , " because of course sanctimonious indignation is not an invention of the young . People have been wondering how people dare forever . The English comedian Catherine Tate , for example , does a sketch character called Derek who is an older single man who lives with his mother and has a speaking manner that is gayer than Christmas . So when young people assume he is gay and treat him respectfully as such , his tagline , " How very dare you ! " is a caricature of a self-deluded Boomer whose crusty , outdated sense of place in the world has been challenged by a new morality of kindness and acceptance . <p> One thing you notice in these books about generational contrast is the frequency of insanity metaphors . There is plenty of religious and cultish language . Occasionally military images are called up for service . But it is the claims of insanity that stand out . <p> " How did our societies become so insane ? " lamented Eric Kaufmann , author of Whiteshift : Populism , Immigration and the Future of White Majorities in his Financial Times review of Murray 's book . <p> Kaufmann is a Canadian Gen Xer in his late 40s , but that is a question that would sound perfectly normal coming from the average Gen Z teenager of today , as he looks around at climate change , Brexit , Trumpism , and all the rest , and wonders how it got this way and what in hell the future might look like . <p> This sort of generational astrology has lately caught on , in part because it seems to be working better than ever . Traditional astrology has been packaged into memes and gifs as a Millennial trend of breezy self-care , so it is already current in popular culture . Intellectuals are at it too , both young and old , traditionalist and revolutionary . The theory of the Fourth Turning , a cyclical and quasi-mystical view of generation history that suggests we are now in the boundary years between the collapse of an old order and the rise of a new one , has taken on a sulphurous whiff of the dark arts through its endorsement by Steve Bannon , former Trump chief of staff and alt-right thought leader who is now rallying nationalist political groups in Europe . <p> Roughly , that theory observes that , in the cultural orbit of America , there has been a cataclysm once every lifetime , almost like clockwork . The American Revolution , Civil War , the Great Depression and World War II , and the 2008 financial collapse are all separated by about 80 or 90 years . All have led to the collapse of an old order and the rise of a new -- colony to republic , slavery to freedom , fascist threat to global hegemony . These periods are what the demographic economist Neil Howe calls Fourth Turnings , the final acts in four-part generational dramas in which the future seems uncertain , contingent , up for grabs . Things are changing and everyone knows it . This is what gives a Fourth Turning era its uneasy mood . The old order is dying and the new order is not yet born . <p> Things feel more apocalyptic today than they ever did for the Boomers , even with the threat of nuclear annihilation in their childhood , which they matured out of when they won the Cold War . The European security compact is falling apart , encouraged by a renegade American president . Centrist politics have weakened . The fringes are closing in . <p> It seems that way at least . Beliefs about the future are uncertain , not entirely rational . They resist argument . They involve fear and longing . They emerge from a shared story about the past . <p> So what we have in all this modern talk about how the young and old each regard the other as delusional lunatics are competing stories about how the past will determine the future , and what might be done to encourage or prevent that . These new books that sketch the battlefields of the culture war between old and young are not predictions , in the sense of rational arguments about what is likely to happen .
