<h> Social Sharing <h> Our MPs behave in ways that would n't be tolerated in a Grade 8 classroom <p> Conservative Leader Andrew Scheer , left , Liberal Leader Justin Trudeau and NDP Leader Jagmeet Singh , seen speaking after the results of the October election were announced . Critics have said this election brought out some of the worst in our political parties . ( Jeff McIntosh/The Canadian Press ; Paul Chiasson/The Canadian Press ; Ben Nelms/CBC ) <p> This column is an opinion by Tamara Miller . She holds a PhD with a specialization in Canadian history , and has had a 20-year policy career with the federal government . For more information about CBC 's Opinion section , please see the FAQ . <p> The federal cabinet has been chosen , rookie MPs have had their orientation , and the House of Commons comes back into session today . It 's time for our elected officials to get down to business , but recent experience has shown that modern governing looks more like a combative sport than a reasoned approach to making decisions in support of the country and its citizens . <p> On a visit to the Parliament Buildings in Ottawa in the fall with a middle school class , I was mortified to watch the example our elected officials set on the House floor during Question Period . Though I understand the tradition of heckling , the level of disrespect was an embarrassment as shouts and jeers , some of them personal , routinely drowned out the recognized speaker . <p> And it was n't reserved to one party . Members across parties were intent on shouting over each other , name calling and launching sarcastic retorts . <p> After our visit , the Grade 8 students spoke not about the issues that were being discussed , nor about the parliamentary process . What drew their attention was how adults yelled each other down , instead of engaging in reasoned debate . <p> It did n't take the kids long to notice that the political leadership in this country acted in a way that would never have been tolerated in their own classrooms . <p> Prime Minister Justin Trudeau responds to a question during Question Period in the House of Commons on June 19 in Ottawa . ( Adrian Wyld/Canadian Press ) <p> Their observations were consistent with a 2016 Samara Canada study . It found that 69 per cent of MPs surveyed believed heckling was a problem in the House of Commons -- and yet 72 per cent of MPs admitted to doing it . <p> The same survey noted that 20 per cent of respondents , especially those who are female , reported that heckling affected their job performance in the House . <p> That report was released two full years before the kids ' visit , but based on our experience , nothing has really changed . <p> It seems we have come to a place in our politics when there is more civility in the average middle school classroom than in the House of Commons . <p> Look , I get it . Barbs and jabs do stand out better than policy announcements , and what 's more , they work . Throwing out slights does get attention on the news . <p> But the problems facing this country require our leaders to put aside childish political posturing and get to work . <p> The climate change crisis is upon us . Across the globe , there has been a rising tide of nationalism spurred by weakening economies and the largest mass migration of people since the Second World War . And the next generation is predicted to be the first in history that will be worse off than their parents . <p> These are big , complex problems and we need equally big , complex solutions . And we are n't likely to get those kinds of solutions from our elected government , given the level of acrimony and divisiveness on display in Question Period on the House of Commons floor . <p> Refusing to listen to the person across the aisle because they wear a different political stripe is not just impolite ; it is foolish , and a betrayal of the principles of public service . <p> As the recent election kicked off , I sent an identical letter to each of the four main national party leaders asking the following questions : <p> If elected to the House of Commons , are you open to listening to ideas and working with all MPs , regardless of political affiliation ? <p> Will you commit to improving the level of civility in the democratic process and the House of Commons ? <p> The answer from all , except for Green Party leader Elizabeth May , was a resounding silence . A member of Ms. May 's campaign team responded to my query directing me to the pages in the Green Party Platform on Good Governance , but did not expressly respond to my questions . <p> From the tone of the party leaders during the campaign , it was clear civility and respect were not on their radar . <p> Politics in Canada seems to be well on its way down the same toxically divided partisan path as our neighbours to the south . <p> Cindy McCain , the wife of the late U.S. Senator John McCain , recently noted : " We have little hope of overcoming our common problems , defending our common interests and advancing our common ideals if our public debates imitate the angriest Twitter feeds . " <p> Now that the Canadian federal election is over , the time has come for our politicians to take a lesson from the middle-school kids -- grow-up . <p> Start working towards finding solutions to the bigger problems we are facing , rather than competing to see who can yell the loudest or throw the best insult inside , and outside , the House of Commons . <p> This column is part of CBC 's Opinion section .
