<p> Grizzlies have long roamed the four mainland Inuvialuit communities , including Inuvik , but there are more of them and they 're appearing for the first time around the two more northerly communities of Sachs Harbour and Ulukhaktok , he said . <p> Amos , 42 , grew up in Sachs Harbour and says the ecosystem has changed significantly over his lifetime . While the seasonal freeze used to begin in August , this year it occurred toward the end of September or early October -- an increasingly common occurrence , he said . <p> " There is a much longer melting and growing season . Areas that were tundra or barren for the most part are now grasslands and have other types of vegetation . Willow , for instance , are starting to establish themselves and grow higher or taller , " he said . <p> Doug Clark , a University of Saskatchewan associate professor in the school of environment and sustainability , is working with members of the community to document the bears . <p> During a layover in Calgary on his way back from the territory , Clark said in a phone interview that he has installed four remote cameras in areas where locals say they 've spotted the massive animals , and distributed eight more cameras to local hunters and trappers to install . <p> The movement of the grizzlies in the North is significant because it 's part of a wide scale expansion , he said . <p> " That 's not the only part of Canada where grizzlies are expanding their range , " he said . <p> Grizzly bears have lost significant habitat to human settlement across North America and continue to struggle in some regions . But they have been expanding their range northward for several years , he said . One area seeing more grizzlies is the west coast of Hudson Bay , including Wapusk National Park near Churchill , Man. , best known for its roaming polar bears . <p> With no southerly source population , it shows that grizzlies are n't just moving north , they 're moving east and south as well . <p> " Something pretty big is going on and we do n't know why , " Clark said . <p> The most obvious question -- why now and why not earlier ? -- suggests climate change is playing a role alongside other changes like resource development . <p> " They 're very much looking like one of the early winners in the climate change sweepstakes . But what that means in the long term , we do n't really know , " Clark said . <p> The bears are not the only species expanding their range and the High Arctic is n't the only place with a changing climate . <p> Some of the biggest changes are happening in ocean environments and coastal areas , said Brian Starzomski , director of environmental studies at the University of Victoria . Melting glaciers are cooling the climate in northeastern North America , while an unusual warming event known as " The Blob " has brought some tropical species like the pufferfish to British Columbia 's waters . <p> Inland , wildfires are burning bigger , hotter and over wide areas . Mountains are also a hot spot for range changes , as species typically move both pole-ward and toward higher altitudes . <p> Species that can move easily -- birds and insects -- are expected to fare better than those that are stagnant , Starzomski said . <p> Of particular concern are species like the subalpine larch , a tree that lives at or near the tops of mountains in B.C. and Alberta . It can live for 1,000 years but it also takes almost 100 to 200 years to reproduce . <p> But blaming it all on climate change is too simple , he said . Humans have done a " great job " of introducing invasive species to new ecosystems through global trade , polluting the atmosphere and making land use decisions that destroy habitats or sever migration routes , he said . <p> " There 's a lot of pressure on nature at the moment . We talk a lot about climate change , but all of these things add up in the matter of human impact on the environment , " he said .
