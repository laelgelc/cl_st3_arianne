<p> About a year ago , Alberta 's United Conservative Party leader stood in front of 1,500 raucous supporters and called the federal carbon tax an act of " economic masochism " that he would fight with everything in his power if he were elected premier . <p> Jason Kenney did win the election last spring , and government lawyers are to argue today before Alberta 's Appeal Court that he should win his tax fight , too . <p> " Alberta says that this is an unwarranted and unprincipled intrusion into provincial jurisdiction , " says the province 's written argument . " It undermines the basic structure of our constitutional system . " <p> Two other provincial Appeal Courts have begged to differ . Ontario and Saskatchewan lost their challenges of the federal tax on carbon , although both judgments had dissenters . <p> Eric Adams , a University of Alberta law professor , said he does n't expect to hear any new arguments on Monday . <p> " It 's just a new panel of judges to bring these arguments in front of , " he said . <p> " The argument is that justice on a local level requires judges who live in a jurisdiction and understand its peoples and its concerns . Sometimes , constitutional arguments made in a particular place can have the flavour of that particular place . " <p> Adams said he believes Alberta is unlikely to win . But if there 's a dissenting judge , that could bolster the government 's argument before the Supreme Court of Canada , which has already scheduled a January date for the Ontario and Saskatchewan appeals . <p> " If they do n't win , they hope for a judgment from some judges that lends weight and credibility , and maybe a new perspective to add to the dissenting opinions that have already been rendered in Saskatchewan and Ontario , " said Adams . <p> Ottawa argues that the peace , order and good government clause of the Constitution gives it power to pass legislation on matters of national concern . Establishing minimum national standards on greenhouse gas emissions " is a matter of national concern that only Parliament can address , " it argues . <p> Alberta says using that clause is an unwarranted expansion of a federal power that in the past has been used sparingly . It says provinces already have the power to deal with emissions and should be left to do so . <p> " The federal government made a gamble here that this was a case that was worth opening up that previously neglected box , " Adams said . " They 've taken a bit of a risk here . " <p> Three out of five Saskatchewan appellate judges agreed with Ottawa , as did four out of five of their Ontario colleagues . Past judgments have recognized the environment as a matter of shared jurisdiction . <p> Either way , Adams cautions against putting too much weight on these cases . If current arguments for a national carbon tax are rejected , the federal Liberals have invested too much political capital not to try new ones , he suggested . <p> " If they lose the case , they 'll look at other ways to bring legislation that can deal with the climate crisis . No matter how this case goes , it wo n't be the end of parliamentary attempts to deal with climate change . " <p> Kenney ditched a consumer carbon tax that the previous NDP government had brought in soon after his party won the election . He has established a $30-a-tonne carbon tax on industrial emitters , replacing somewhat stronger measures introduced by the New Democrats . The Trudeau Liberals have approved that tax . <p> Arguments are scheduled for three days . As well as Alberta and Canada , the attorneys-general of Ontario , New Brunswick , Saskatchewan and British Columbia are to speak , as are eight First Nations , non-governmental groups and Crown corporations .
