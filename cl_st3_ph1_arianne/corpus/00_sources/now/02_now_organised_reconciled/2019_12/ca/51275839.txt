<p> Male squirrels that leave home turf for parts unknown live longer and have more offspring . <p> But female squirrels are better off if they stay put , say University of Alberta ecologists . <p> The conclusion is based on 30 years of data from a population of North American red squirrels in the Yukon . <p> " The benefits to living in a different population than you were born are sex-dependent , " says April Martinig , PhD student in the department of biological sciences and the study 's lead author . <p> " Males benefit from moving away , whereas females do not . We also found that the decision to move away or stay at home has an impact on offspring . " <p> Male squirrels that move away no longer need to compete with siblings for limited resources , including mates or food . In addition , female squirrels prefer to mate with newcomer males . <p> Female squirrels that leave the area in which they were born , however , lose out on the support of nearby family -- something that male squirrels do n't receive . <p> In some cases , only one squirrel is allowed to stay in the home , so any other siblings must go , moving further away . If those siblings are female , they lose out . <p> " Squirrels live in a world where there are only so many ' empty apartments ' to live in , " adds Martinig , conducting her PhD studies under the supervision of professor Stan Boutin . <p> " Movement is a crucial component of species persistence . Understanding how and why certain individuals move is key to protecting populations as climate change impacts their distribution on the landscape . " <p> The paper , " The new kid on the block : Immigrant males win big whereas females pay fitness cost after dispersal , " will be published in Ecology Letters on Dec. 13.
