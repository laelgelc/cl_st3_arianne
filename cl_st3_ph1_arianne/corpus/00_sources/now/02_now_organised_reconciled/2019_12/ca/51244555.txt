<h> Arctic research , climate change in spotlight at conference in Halifax <p> Jeff Taylor is shown on Ellesmere Island when he was doing graduate research on the northern ozone layer about 15 years ago . He 's now academic vice-president of applied research and innovation at the Nova Scotia Community College . - Contributed <p> HALIFAX , N.S. -- <p> The sun goes below the horizon on Ellesmere Island in Nunavut about mid-October and it does n't return to the sky until late February . <p> Jeff Taylor remembers experiencing Ellesmere 's first sunrise about 15 years ago as a University of Toronto graduate student . <p> " We would typically go at the time of year when the chemistry is interesting and that happens at sunrise , " said Taylor , who studied the chemical reactions in the stratosphere sparked by sunlight as part of researching ozone levels . <p> " When you show up , it would be completely black , very dark and cold of course , " recounted Taylor , who is now academic vice-president for applied research and innovation at the Nova Scotia Community College . <p> Former Arctic researcher Jeff Taylor is now academic vice-president of applied research and innovation at the Nova Scotia Community College . - Contributed <p> " We were all wearing parkas and survival gear . Minus 30 ( celsius ) was a common temperature at that time of year . . . . I was sponsored by the Canadian Space Agency and ( the team ) did some great work . " <p> About 10 years later his work took him to Alaska with the National Ecological Observatory Network in a project that produced the world 's first continental-scale climate observatory . <p> While most people in Atlantic Canada likely do n't give the Arctic much thought , what happens there definitely has an impact on our lives , Taylor said . <p> He referred to a report by Natural Resources Canada released last February that said melting Arctic snowpack and glacial ice could raise global mean sea levels by more than a half-metre by 2100 . <p> Arctic research has been in the spotlight this week in Halifax at the 15th annual ArcticNet Conference . <p> Billed as the largest Canadian gathering of scientists and researchers who investigate the impacts of climate change and modernization in the coastal Canadian Arctic , the conference began Monday and ends Thursday . <p> The potential impact of melting in the Arctic on Atlantic Canada is clear . The mayor of Annapolis Royal in Nova Scotia , Bill MacDonald , told a municipal conference in November that the most recent flood maps put his town entirely underwater by 2050 . <p> The ArcticNet conference will explore northern climate change in light of " everything from the way Inuit communities are impacted , the traditional way of life of indigenous people , the way biodiversity is lost , caribou migration is changing , " said Taylor , who wo n't get to this year 's conference but has attended its meetings in the past . <p> One of the highlights of the conference will happen Thursday with the presentation of the $100,000 Weston Family Prize for Lifetime Achievement in Northern Research . <p> " Even in the high Arctic , we 've started to see signs of landscape response to climate change . " <p> - Wayne Pollard , winner of the Weston Family Prize for Lifetime Achievement in Northern Research <p> In a recent interview , Pollard said he was " pleasantly surprised " by the award , for which he was nominated by his department at McGill . <p> Although he 's reaching the end of his 40-year career , he intends to put some of the money to work on his signature project of studying - and lobbying to preserve - Axel Heiberg Island . <p> " It actually has some of the greatest diversity of high Arctic landscapes , " Pollard said . <p> Geographer and Arctic expert Wayne Pollard will be presented the $100,000 Weston Family Prize for Lifetime Achievement in Northern Research at the ArcticNet conference in Halifax on Thursday . - Contributed <p> " There 's a number of unique geological features , it 's largely uninhabited , it 's in a very strategic position and I think that we would be very well placed if we were to try and set places aside in the Arctic , basically for science , particularly in light of climate change . It 's islands like that are going to be dramatically affected by climate warming in the Arctic . " <p> The Arctic is warming twice as fast as any other part of the world , he said . Glaciers , sea ice and permafrost will be strongly affected by any kind of increase in temperature . <p> " Glaciers are already receding , the sea ice throughout the Arctic is becoming less and less in terms of its year-round presence and basically the permafrost is starting to show signs of instability , depending on where you are in the Arctic of course , " he said . " But even in the high Arctic , we 've started to see signs of landscape response to climate change . " <p> As a specialist in permafrost and the behaviour of water in cold environments , Pollard has also gained recognition as a collaborator with the Canadian Space Agency and NASA in the search for life beyond Earth . <p> " From a planetary exploration perspective , water is essentially the one thing that we 're looking for on other planets like Mars or some of the moons of Jupiter and Saturn , " he said . " It 's the most common criterion for life . " <p> The life forms he expects to find might disappoint those with visions of ET in their heads . <p> " What we 're really thinking of is probably less advanced life forms , probably microbial lifeforms , " he said .
