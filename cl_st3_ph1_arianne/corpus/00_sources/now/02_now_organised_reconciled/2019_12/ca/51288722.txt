<h> Amid arctic sovereignty questions , Canadian Rangers still see few credible reports of unusual activity <p> A strange pinging sound in the mid-Arctic community of Igloolik . Reports of what appeared to be a vessel , moving beneath cold , crystalline waters . Suspected military aircraft in remote areas of Yukon . <p> These are among the reports of unusual activity logged in recent years by a Canadian military entity that fans out across remote and isolated parts of the arctic . The Canadian Rangers patrol the region in their emblematic crimson sweatshirts , armed with a rifle and any combination of GPS technology , high frequency radios and satellite phones . These are portions of Canada where the Armed Forces has no permanent presence . And in an era where arctic sovereignty of the powerful , their work is gaining attention . <p> The federal government committed in July to efforts for increasing the size , geographic footprint and effectiveness of the military entity . <p> " The Canadian Rangers are critical to effective military surveillance and play a key role in exercising Canada 's sovereignty in our Northern and Arctic regions , " the government assessed , as part of their response to recommendations on arctic sovereignty that were presented to the House of Commons in April . <p> The report identified several unanswered questions for Canada with regard to its high north , urging the feds to better understand China 's growing interest in the Arctic and Russia 's military intentions there . <p> And while it 's subtle , the Commanding Officer of 1 Canadian Ranger Patrol Group can see the way their work is changing . <p> " I think there is -- I would n't say concern -- a heightened awareness , perhaps . And definitely a more acute sense of paying attention to what 's happening , " L-Col Timothy Halfkenny , a former chief staff in operations for Joint Task Force North , told iPolitics in a telephone interview earlier this month . Although his is a low-level , tactical unit , he said his persistent presence in the north " raises the interest in my unit to more strategic proportions . " <p> Currently , some 1,400 of the 2,000 Canadian Rangers are considered active , patrolling 65 communities across Nunavut , Northwest Territories , Yukon and Atlin , British Columbia . Despite this wide swath of terrain , most of the reports of unusual activities that land on Halfkenny 's desk or reach his ear -- for low-flying aircraft , strange observations in the waterways , and any sight or sound that seemed unfamiliar and out of place -- come from Nunavut . <p> Scene from Iqaluit , Nunavut on August 12 , 2016. iPolitics/Kyle Duggan <p> " The western side ( of the arctic ) is still very ice clad , very dangerous when the ice breaks up , and we do n't see a whole lot of traffic in that area , " Halfkenny explained , noting that the Canadian military 's American counterparts also monitored the northern coast of Alaska ; some information from their work was shared with Canada at the national level . But despite growing discussion about arctic sovereignty on the Hill , the total number of reports received from the Rangers are still sparse -- around two or three per year , by Halfkenny 's count . <p> Furthermore , very few of those reports are found to be credible . As of early December , Halfkenny said he had only been flagged to one instance of unusual activity in all of 2019 -- a report of a water vehicle that was later determined to be unfounded . " That could have been a whale . It could have been anything , " he told iPolitics . " After analysis , it really proved to be nothing . " <p> Still , every report spurs a flurry of activity . If the report is of suspected military aircraft in remote areas , they send pictures or descriptions out for identification , cross referencing with CAF activity , allies ' activity , and civil agencies ' activities . ( Often , in Yukon , such sightings are military aircraft in transit to Alaska . ) The same process applies for unknown ground vehicles suspected to be military . <p> For the unknown " pinging noise " in Igloolik in particular , a sonar specialist was sent out to document first hand reports . " It turned out to be nothing , but in the end , we have to examine that , " Halfkenny said . <p> Among the recommendations presented to the House earlier this year on arctic sovereignty was a request that the government explore whether the Rangers could be given drones , in order to enhance their " domain awareness . " That suggestion was ultimately shot down by the government , at least for now . <p> " Drones are an interesting question , and I 've had an opportunity with a couple of committees that have come up to ask about that , " Halfkenny said . <p> The technology would have to be a careful discussion , by his assessment , given the remoteness of the Rangers ' work , the repair and maintenance required to incorporate drone use into their work , the initial training required , and , practically speaking , the ability of a drone to actually operate in the ice , fog and high winds that are part and parcel to arctic life . Halfkenny has -- as he described it -- " carefully " engaged , when asked , with senior military and departmental leadership on the subject of the military 's future in Canada 's far north . <p> Some senior military officials have cast doubt on the severity of perceived threats to the North from countries like Russia and China . Maj.-Gen . William F. Seymour , the deputy commander of the Canadian Joint Operations Command told iPolitics last fall that he felt it was " alarmist " to suggest that Canadian sovereignty was under threat . ( Earlier that day , though , he had noted to the House Standing Committee on Foreign Affairs and International Development that Russia had displayed a capability to launch missiles far enough that their military would n't necessarily have to expand into Canada 's north . ) <p> Beyond the threat of unknown foreign intentions looms the threat of climate change . According to a report commissioned by Environment and Climate Change Canada and released earlier this year , Northern Canada has been warming faster than the rest of the country , which is already warming on average at around double the global rate . ( Average temperatures in the North were found to have increased by an average of 2.3 Celsius per year since 1948 , with the most significant warming taking place in the winter months . ) <p> And as ice in the arctic melts , it 's adding ease to navigating certain northern waterways , as several academics and journalists have reported in recent years . <p> " There 's a common perception that there will be more traffic in the arctic as climate affects the region , " Halfkenny noted to iPolitics . Though he concedes that he 's seen " a little bit of that " over the last four years , particularly around shipping traffic , he says the phenomenon has n't reached the magnitude that had been expected or predicted " from a practical perspective . " <p> " I know a lot of people refer to ' militarization of the north ' , " he added . But he does n't believe the arctic Rangers are meant as a response to specific threat assessments . ( The military also has other entities in the North , running their Operations NANOOK and NEVUS , the latter on Ellesmere Island , maintaining their high-north post at CFS Alert , as well as several other missions , operations and partnerships with groups like the Canadian Coast Guard . ) <p> " The Rangers are n't designed as a conventional deterrence with respect to ... operations or active military engagement . They 're really , largely , a visible presence role , " Halfkenny concluded . " They report on unusual military activity , and that role really has n't changed . "
