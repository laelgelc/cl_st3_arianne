<h> A River in the Sky <h> By Ainslie Cruickshank -- Star Vancouver May 30 , 2019 <h> Above the waters of the North Pacific a ribbon of moist air some 800 kilometres wide and 5,000 kilometres long spins off from swirling winds . <p> To the naked eye this ribbon may be no more than a sea of wispy clouds , disguising an atmospheric river that carries 25 Mississippi Rivers worth of water vapour -- and it 's headed for B.C. 's south coast . When it collides with land , it will release tremendous rains that could last for a couple of days and trigger devastating landslides , floods , and fatal avalanches . <p> Sometimes known as a Pineapple Express , atmospheric rivers carry moisture from the tropics and subtropics toward the poles and they are fundamental to the Earth 's water cycle . <h> Related Story <p> ARs carry about 90 per cent of the water vapour that 's transported toward the North and South poles across the mid-latitudes -- in the Northern Hemisphere , that includes the area covering the Canadian provinces . <p> At their finest , they soak parched landscapes and replenish depleted water sources . In California , close to half of the state 's annual precipitation can come from atmospheric rivers . <p> Over the open ocean , ARs often " look pretty benign , " said Marty Ralph , a research meteorologist at UC San Diego and the original AR storm chaser . <p> Photo by Erik Jepsen/UC San Diego <p> " All of a sudden , we see some clouds up way in the distance ... and those clouds thicken up " <p> -- Marty Ralph , THE ORIGINAL AR STORM CHASER <p> Ralph started flying " missions of discovery " straight into the body of these so-called rivers in the sky in 1998 . <p> " We 're out over the AR where it just looks like low clouds ... and we look ahead to the northeast and all of a sudden , we see some clouds up way in the distance ... and those clouds thicken up , " he said . <p> As the AR hits land , it lifts and all that water vapour it 's carrying starts to condense into rain and snow . <p> Never miss the latest news from the Star . Over three days 380 mm , or 15 inches , of rain fell across the Russian River watershed . The land , already sodden from earlier storms , could n't handle any more moisture . <p> The river burst its banks inundating the Sonoma County town of Guerneville . Residents , who suddenly needed boats to get around , were stranded . When the floodwater finally receded , it left 2,000 muddied and sopping wet properties behind . <p> PHOTO BY Justin Sullivan/Getty Images <p> About 25 to 30 ARs hit B.C. 's coast each year , mostly during the rainy , winter season , said Ruping Mo , a senior research and development meteorologist with Environment and Climate Change Canada . <p> They have been blamed for damaging floods , devastating landslides , and linked to at least one deadly avalanche . <p> And in the future , there could be 50 per cent more ARs that make landfall in B.C. , said Duane Waliser , a senior scientist at NASA 's Jet Propulsion Laboratory . <p> Waliser and his co-authors published a study last spring that said climate change will make atmospheric rivers a lot bigger . Models indicate the typical AR in the 21st century will be about 25 per cent wider -- 855 km versus 700 km -- and 25 per cent longer -- 5,400 km versus 4,300 km -- than the ARs of the 20th century . <p> AR chaser Ralph and a team of researchers recently developed a five-point scale -- similar to the Saffir -- Simpson hurricane wind scale -- to categorize atmospheric rivers . <p> Weaker ARs , the ones and twos on the scale , are more likely to be of the beneficial , drought-quenching variety . It 's the fours -- like the one that hit Guerneville -- and the fives that can wreak havoc . <p> Advertisement <h> Landslides <p> In September 2015 , an atmospheric river slammed into B.C. in a fury . <p> Rain pelted the Pemberton , B.C. region , saturating the slopes and triggering mudslides and floods that washed out roads . <p> PHOTO BY B.C. " An extreme danger rating is a warning to " avoid all avalanche terrain . " <p> Advertisement <p> The danger rating is rarely elevated to extreme , said Grant Helgeson , a senior forecaster with Avalanche Canada . But ARs commonly push the danger rating to high , he said . When that happens , it means avalanches are very likely . <p> Even atmospheric rivers that do n't smash into B.C. 's coast directly can create dangerous conditions in the backcountry . <p> In January 2016 , 15 people were buried by a catastrophic avalanche in the Renshaw-area , about 20 kilometres east of McBride , B.C. <p> PHOTO BY DARRYL DYCK/THE CANADIAN PRESS <p> Five snowmobilers were killed . <p> In the days before the disaster , rain and snowfall had stressed the snowpack , an Avalanche Canada spokesperson told media at the time . That rain and snow was most likely the result of an AR that actually made landfall much farther south , in Oregon , said Benjamin Hatchett , an atmospheric scientist at the Desert Research Institute in Nevada , who analyzed the event for the Star . <p> After the AR hit land , strong winds carried that moisture north , over the mountains to interior B.C. <p> " It would have facilitated unstable snow conditions , " he said , likely contributing to the tragedy that cost five Alberta men their lives . <p> Pascal Haegeli , an expert in avalanche safety research based at B.C. 's Simon Fraser University , says precipitation from ARs can weaken a snowpack for the whole season . <p> Whether the water vapour carried by an AR falls as rain or snow , a big dump adds weight that can quickly weaken a snowpack , particularly when there is already a vulnerable lower layer . An earlier spell of warm , sunny weather or rain , followed by a return to freezing weather , can create the perfect crust for a heavy dump of snow to slide along . <p> Rain also adds heat , which can melt the bonds between individual grains of snow , which then start to lose strength . <p> When that " wet layer is buried in the next snowfall , that creates a weakness in the snowpack that often persists for months , " Haegeli said . <p> In the western United States , atmospheric rivers have already been linked to more than 100 avalanche deaths . <h> Related Story <p> A study , co-authored by Hatchett and published in 2017 in the Journal of Hydrometeorology , found 105 avalanches that killed 123 people in the western U.S. between 1998 and 2014 happened during or just after atmospheric rivers . That 's 31 per cent of the avalanche deaths in the region during those 16 years . <p> Fatal avalanches in the U.S. triggered by ARs <p> Benjamin Hatchett , an atmospheric scientist at the Desert Research Institute in Nevada has connected atmospheric rivers over a 16-year period to deadly avalanches in the U.S. In Canada , no one has yet mapped the connection between ARs and avalanches . <p> Source : Benjamin Hatchett , Desert Research Institute in Nevada <p> The situation may be more complicated in B.C. and merits further study , Hatchett said . ARs seem to hit B.C. less frequently than the U.S. coast , and those that do must contend with the coastal mountain ranges .
