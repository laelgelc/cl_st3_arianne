<p> The response to a changing climate is well advanced in the Pacific , providing lessons for the rest of us , writes Tom McKinlay . <p> As the thousands of School Strike 4 Climate marchers enveloped the Octagon stage this year , a busy figure went unobtrusively to work . <p> Hiliako Iaheto , musician and music teacher , had been prevailed upon to organise the public address system . <p> So the young climate leaders delivered their calls for action through the microphones he passed them . <p> " What do we want ? " they asked . " Climate justice , " came the booming response . <p> " When do we want it ? " they asked . " Now ! " <p> Then , some of the speakers talked about the Pacific and the places where " now " ca n't happen soon enough . <p> Iaheto says he was happy to help . <p> " I 'm really proud of them , " he says of the young people who led Dunedin 's School Strike 4 Climate effort . <p> " But I can also sense for them , I feel for them that they have been left with a responsibility that the generation before them , and before them , should have done something about ... What a weight . " <p> Iaheto has seen this dynamic before , in Tokelau , where he was born and raised . Where young people have led from the front on climate change . <p> " They are a lot more conscious over on the islands , " he says . <p> For several years now , Tokelauans have been active as part of the Climate Warriors group , young people drawn from islands across the Pacific who have taken their call for action to climate talks in Europe . <p> Dunedin 's school strikers have quite explicitly joined that cause , owning the fact they live in a coastal Pacific city where some have only dunes between them and a rising ocean . <p> Tokelau is a non self-governing territory of New Zealand , a collection of atolls and islets just south of the equator . It 's the paradise of popular imagination ; sand , reefs , palms and blue ocean . <p> But it 's also on the front line , as nowhere on its three main atolls is more than 5m above sea level . <p> Tokelauans ' response has been robust . It was the first nation in the world to go 100% solar for electricity , ditching its diesel generators , and is adding wind power to the mix . <p> They just need the rest of the world to catch up . <p> " Soon those atolls will no longer be the beautiful atolls they are , of sand and luscious green , island vibe , " Hiliako says . " It will be the islands of retaining walls , of concrete blocks . Already I see big slabs of concrete being built . <p> " That is a picture of survival . The culture , the people , they are trying to survive . " <p> Iaheto lives in Macandrew Bay , Dunedin these days , he came out to New Zealand on an educational scholarship in the early 1980s ; stayed , met his partner , had a son . <p> Taua Ritiata and his daughter Leila . Photo : Supplied <p> The last time he was back in Tokelau -- not an easy place to get to -- was 1999 . <p> " In 1999 I was there for about four weeks but the impact of the water , my gosh . I noticed the major shift of the sea level rising . " <p> Growing up there , the surrounding reef was bone dry at low tide , so that was a time to gather food , Iaheto says . <p> However , by 1999 the low tide still covered the reef , and high tides , king tides , they were on the march . <p> " When you go to the middle of the village , you just dig a metre and there 's water . <p> " So king tides or high tide , part of the village that we call the malae -- which is equivalent to the marae -- sometimes at king tide , water comes up on there . " <p> Iaheto does not like to think about the prospect of his people having to abandon their homes . <p> He observes that mass migration has not gone well for others , Europe providing the most recent example of that . <p> " What are people without land ? " he asks . <p> First the land goes , then the culture , then the language . <p> That dynamic is at the centre of Taua Ritiata 's research at the University of Otago . <p> The man from Kiribati is completing a master 's thesis through the National Centre for Peace and Conflict Studies on identity in the face of climate change . <p> It is something the people of his country -- it 's population about the same as Dunedin 's -- have been wrestling with now for a while as the big existential question arrives with the tide ; can they stay or must they go ? If they go , what then of the Kiribati sense of identity ? <p> Ritiata 's academic endeavours are part of one response to those questions . <p> Kiribati 's former president , Anote Tong , argued that as part of his country 's response to climate change the young people of the islands group should educate themselves . Then , if they must flee their island homes , they would at least be well-educated refugees . <p> " Because we do n't know the day , we do n't know the time , " Ritiata says . <p> During his presidency , Tong also bought land in Fiji , as a further backstop for his country . <p> But there is the prospect of conflict there too , Taua says . For all that the Government of Fiji has been supportive , it is another question whether that welcome extends down through society . <p> " There will be ordinary Fijians with connections to that land too . " <p> Tong 's presidency came to an end at the last election , and the new administration , led by Taneti Maamau is taking a different approach , Ritiata says . <p> Ritiata is certainly not giving up and plans to return to Kiribati to do what he can . <p> " I want to go back and contribute myself . It is very beautiful , where I come from . " <p> Among Ritiata 's motivations is his daughter , Leila . Walking but not yet speaking , the sun shines from her smile and it seems entirely likely the sparkle in her eyes mirrors the ocean surrounding her home . <p> " I hope that she will see some of the culture there when she goes back , " her father says . <p> Mino Cleverley too is studying in Dunedin while keeping an eye on the islands . <p> Mino Cleverley is looking at the potential for conflict as climate change intensifies . Photo : Peter McIntosh <p> The senior student 's PhD , also through the National Centre for Peace and Conflict Studies , is on climate change conflict ; what might happen as coastal dwellers are pushed inland . <p> His experience of the response to climate change in Samoa is of people accepting the challenge and moving to meet it . <p> There his wife 's family land has been affected by coastal erosion . <p> That comes with a particular threat for people of the Pacific , where family are commonly buried in front of homes , between the front door and the sea . <p> " When Pacific people see their ancestors floating away it is really heart-rending , " Cleverley says . <p> But the community is rallying and efforts around his wife 's land are focusing on salt-resistant plantings to hold the line , he says . <p> " I am looking at Samoa , I am looking at the coastal village situation . And looking at it with an indigenous perspective and looking at the resilience that is within the village . Because that is our anchor , the village . That is how we have survived for millennia in the Pacific because of the interconnectedness of our villages and families . That 's our history , there 's no social welfare , no central government to help us . We have had to do it within our own resources . " <p> There will be lessons there for everyone . <p> " A big determinant of quality of life is the sense of community , wherever you are and it is looking out for each other and I think it is universal . " <p> Cleverley 's observations are backed up by research by others at the University of Otago . That work has identified a range of strategies being employed at a village level , including to diversify food and water sources , remain geographically mobile and have more than one place to live , and develop mental and spiritual strength . <p> Back in Macandrew Bay , Iaheto says none of this will be easy for the coming generations , but that has to be kept in perspective . <p> " The difference between not easy and survival is the urgency of it , " he says . <p> He sees the same challenges here as high-tide lines in Macandrew Bay move higher . <p> Then there was 2015 , during the rainfall that flooded South Dunedin . <p> Macandrew Bay became a pond where it was hard to see where the coast road ended and the harbour began . <p> " We live in the biggest island in the Pacific , " Iaheto says . " For Aotearoa , it completes the triangle ; from Hawaii to Aotearoa over to Rapanui , Easter Island . So all the people who live here , be it pakeha , whatever , we are still islanders . " <p> We 're connected , he says . <p> " These young people they have embraced all our cultures , " he says of the young School Strike 4 Climate marchers . <p> " We can move from that , being able to embrace who we are as a nation . Equally , we can shift to accept responsibility for the survival of mankind , with this global issue . It can be done , it is achievable , it is a possibility . With will and hope it is possible . " <h> Call to board the vaka <p> Kelly Moneymaker is in Madrid with her film about Tokelau 's climate change efforts . Photo : Supplied <p> It 's time to get in the vaka and help with the paddling , Kelly Moneymaker says . <p> The message is for anyone with ears to hear , but the documentary maker is already broadcasting it to those in the best position to respond . <p> Moneymaker is in Madrid for the COP25 talks , the latest United Nations-sponsored climate change conference , where she is unveiling her documentary Vaka , about the remarkable efforts of the people of Tokelau to meet the challenge . <p> Moneymaker spent three weeks in the island nation earlier this year with a documentary crew from her Massey University film-making course and came away with a story about how a combination of indigenous knowledge and modern technology is mapping a way forward . <p> " What we found is the Tokelauan people , they have this long-term data from their ancestors because they pass down information from one generation to another and they know what their environment looked like before humans started to impact it . <p> " Also , even though they have all this long-term knowledge , they are embracing modern science , data collection and assistance from New Zealand to help them support their cultural wisdom . <p> " The combination of the two , I feel , could be the key to solving climate change issues . " <p> Tokelau 's unique " inati " system , in which the atolls ' resources are shared equally , provides the platform for the approach . <p> " It is always about sharing and taking care of each other . " <p> Moneymaker , of Samoan and Inuit heritage , says the inclusive culture allows them to access knowledge from whoever holds it , whether that 's from different groups within their own community or the wider world . <p> They come together , talk , share and decide what to do . " We call them solutionists . " <p> So far , that 's included a rapid transformation of their energy system , away from diesel to solar and wind , shrinking their already minuscule greenhouse gas footprint , building seawalls and renourishing their reefs , building raised garden beds and getting about in electric golf carts . <p> " They are not giving up , they are giving it everything they have . " <p> The associated message is that if they can do it , everyone can do it . <p> " What we are asking is for people to watch this documentary and learn from their resilience and their problem-solving skills and get in the vaka. "
