<p> Bernard Hickey is the Managing Editor of Newsroom Pro based in the Parliamentary Press Gallery . He is a director and shareholder of Newsroom NZ Ltd . He has previously worked for Interest.co.nz , Fairfax NZ , the Financial Times Group and Reuters . <p> New Zealanders feel relatively relaxed about taking on debt worth 300-400 percent of their own personal incomes , but get very nervous when their Government wants to take on debt worth more than 20 percent of the national income or GDP . <p> To help us navigate this split personality on debt , and explain how home buyers and banks think about debt , Two Cents Worth talked to a couple of mortgage and property valuation to explain the mechanics of home owners borrowing from banks . <p> We also looked at about how the nation , and the Government in particular , think about borrowing and debt , and how all that 's changing as interest rates fall and the bank regulator forces banks to hold more capital . <p> To understand the disconnect between how we think about personal and Government debt , we have to look at how the world of mortgages has changed , and is changing month by month in response to a bunch of things , including interest rates . We also look at how much capital banks have to keep aside whenever they lend through a mortgage . And we need to look at what the Reserve Bank has just done with those capital requirements , and at the rules the banks have about mortgages and how they 've changed for some good and bad reasons . Also , we look at what the Government is doing with its own rules about borrowing . <p> John Bolton is the founder of Auckland 's Squirrel Mortgages , which is a broker that helps borrowers find the best deal from a range of banks . He also used to run ANZ 's mortgage operation , so he 's a sort of gamekeeper turned poacher of the mortgage business . <p> Bolton says Kim and Jenee could borrow $850,000 and buy a house worth around $950,000 . <p> I was curious to see what you could get for just under $1m in Khandallah . This one caught my eye . <p> But is it really worth that ? And is the bank taking too big a risk if it lends to you . <p> To do that Two Cents Worth spoke to Carmen Vicelich from Valocity Global . Carmen is the CEO and founder of Valocity , which is an automated platform for banks and brokers to get valuations on properties before they decide to lend to the likes of Jenee and Kim . <p> Her systems estimated it was worth $920k to $970k , so the hoped-for valuation is just over that . <p> But if Kim and Jenee wanted to bid more , they have plenty of room to move if their interest servicing ability was the question . They have a notional $80k a year for interest costs , which would support debt of $2.3m if their mortgage rate was permanently at 3.5 percent . <p> But Bolton says banks wo n't allow that because they assume interest rates will rise to 7 percent at some point , and that owner-occupiers ca n't have interest-only loans . That helps keep mortgages as low-risk loans for the banks , which allows them to allocate only small amounts of capital . <p> Vicelich says the banks also have plenty of room , given they have lent $273 billion against property worth $1.17 trillion . That 's close to $1 trillion in equity . <p> So why is the Reserve Bank so worried ? <p> This week the bank announced its final decision on how much capital banks must hold , in particular , the big four banks . It said they would have to almost double the amount of their own capital they hold to back their loans . That 's an extra $20 billion . <p> Photo : RNZ/Claire Eastham-Farrelly <p> Reserve Bank Governor Adrian Orr says many forget that banks can collapse and that there have been expensive near misses . <p> " I 'll just mention something that people often forget . They are the near misses . I grew up in a forestry town . Deaths were very noticed . Near misses were never counted , " Orr says . <p> " This amount of capital allows people , the owners of those banks , to be a lot more focused on good outcomes over the long period and much more focused on avoiding collapses because they 've got a lot more of their own skin in the game , " he says . <p> " It 's the near misses , the bailouts , they can be very expensive but are never really picked up . So again , it 's not captured the essence of this or not picked up on what the costs can be to society . " <p> So why ca n't the Government borrow 400 percent of income ? <p> But what if it was the Government doing the borrowing to build houses , pipes , roads , railways , hospitals and schools and the other things we need to live in a house and earn money to pay the mortgage . <p> Next week Finance Minister Grant Robertson is expected to unveil what he says is a significant increase in borrowing to bring forward some infrastructure projects . <p> We do n't know how much , but we know it 's likely to nudge the Government 's net debt to GDP ratio from around 20 percent now to maybe as high as 25 percent . <p> That 's because Robertson has set a debt range of 15-25 percent , having achieved the 20 percent net debt target he set for Labour and the Greens in Opposition , and which was the same as National . <p> And just to give you a sense of how much political fear is around this 20 percent number . Here 's an exchange between Robertson and Opposition Finance Spokesman Paul Goldsmith in Parliament this week . <p> Goldsmith : " Does he intend to keep his Government 's solemn promise made to New Zealanders to keep net debt below 20 percent of GDP in 2021-22 ? " <p> ROBERTSON : " What I will commit to is our great record around keeping debt low : two Budgets with debt below 20 percent and , I might say , far below the 23 percent , or nearly 23 percent , that we inherited from the previous Government . " <p> So there we have it . They 're arguing over who has the lowest debt . National at 23 percent and Labour at 20 percent . <p> I checked with Treasury and the Government has assets worth $370 billion and net borrowings of about $60 billion . So that 's a Loan to Value ratio of 16 percent . <p> Why are the politicians and public so worried about an LVR of 16 percent and a DTI of 20 percent ? <p> There 's plenty of borrowing capacity . If the Government borrowed up to 50 percent of its income , which is the debt to income ratio of other AAA borrowers , then the Government could borrow $100 billion over the next ten to 20 years . And it can do it at 1.4 percent at the moment . <p> Infrastructure economist Brad Olsen from Infometrics agrees this two-faced approach to debt is holding back the country . <p> " That 's also because when a house is a household as borrowing that amount , they 're borrowing for one asset , whereas the government is borrowing for many , " Olsen says <p> " So I think it 's just a visibility problem of knowing where that money 's going . And probably a little bit of distrust in where that funding will go and where people want it into . But I suspect seeing every poll around the sun , being a political poll or an issues poll saying how important housing and even climate change are , if you invest in the things that will make a difference now and will make a difference over the future , both to the housing market , but also ensuring that we 're in a more resilient position as an economy our environment , I think people would be on board with it , " he says . <p> " That 's not the story that 's being told , though . All we 're hearing is more debt , more money . We do n't have more bad . And it 's not like that . We need to start investing because it 's not bad money . It 's smart money .
