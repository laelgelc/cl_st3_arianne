<p> A small , but growing , number of people like Paul Kennett are using discarded batteries to create their own powerwalls . <p> Rain thunders down on the corrugated roof of Paul Kennett 's Lower Hutt workshop , but inside the place glows with LED lights and digital battery displays . <p> Kennett , perhaps best known along with his brothers as a publisher of best-selling cycling and mountain bike guidebooks , has joined the ranks of a small but growing band of hobbyists making their own DIY powerwalls . <p> They tinker away in sheds like Kennett 's , soldering iron in hand , constructing battery packs to store energy from solar panels with the aim of cutting their power bill - and their carbon footprint . Then they take to YouTube to outline the process , a maker community keen to share their knowledge . <p> " A scene has sprung up , " says Kennett , one that feeds off our insatiable demand for battery-powered consumer electronics . The battery packs Kennett powers his workshop with , uses the discarded batteries from obsolete gadgets , in particular , the 18650 rechargeable lithium-ion battery , which features in hundreds of millions of laptop battery packs . <p> " They are a commodity item and it is reasonably easy to source them from IT recyclers , " says Kennett , who will be sharing his DIY powerwall tips at the Wellington Maker Faire being held at the TSB Arena on Sunday . <p> " You can put hundreds or thousands of them together to make a decent-sized battery pack . " <h> Second life for batteries <p> The cylinder-shaped brightly-coloured batteries - six of them form a typical laptop battery - are stripped from their case by recyclers . Then they are shipped to processing plants that break the batteries down into their constituent parts - cobalt and lithium salts , stainless steel , copper and aluminium , for re-use in battery making . <p> But Kennett intercepts some of them . He regularly rides his e-bike to a nearby e-waste recycler where he pays around $100 for battery cells that would have made up 50 laptop batteries . <p> " It is like opening presents , " he says . " You do n't know what you are going to get . " <p> Some of them are leaky or rusty , a sure sign they are no use . But of those he runs a voltage test on , he finds around 70 per cent are useful , which goes to show that our electronics are obsolete long before the battery dies . <p> Weeding out bad battery cells is crucial to the safety of the whole DIY enterprise . <p> " When they go bad , the lithium-ion batteries start to develop little fractures inside that are like micro short-circuits . If you develop enough of those then it will short and you 'll get a massive amount of current running through it , " says Kennett . <p> " That 's when things can go very bad . " <p> He has no official electrical qualifications or certification , which are n't required to work on these type of relatively low-power systems anyway . Kennett spent a year studying towards an electrical engineering degree in the early 1980s , before dropping out because he " wanted to play with microprocessors " . <p> He joined the Wellington company Lester Abbey and Associates , which made embedded computer systems for washing machines , fruit drying devices and alarm monitors . In 1984 , Kennett discovered mountain biking which kicked off a decades-long passion for exploring and writing about the stunning bike trails of New Zealand , a business that received a boost on the back of Prime Minister John Key 's scheme , kicked off in 2009 , to build a network of cycle trials the length of the country . <h> Cutting carbon <p> But alongside his biking grew a passion for energy efficiency . He bought his first home in 2004 and set to work adding a double ceiling and floor insulation , then a solar-powered hot water system . <p> He used a hair dryer to blow thousands of polystyrene beads into the outside walls of his house to make it even more energy efficient . Then his interest turned to cutting carbon . <p> " What would it take to reduce my personal carbon footprint to 40 per cent of my 1990 levels by the year 2020 ? " He asked himself . <p> Unable to afford a new electric car , a few years ago he instead looked for a cheap second hand Nissan Leaf , the world 's best-selling electric plug-in car . The plan was to find one with a knackered battery and build his own lithium-ion battery as a replacement . <p> That 's where the interest in all of those multi-coloured 18650 batteries came from . It was by no means a crazy idea . Even now , leading electric car maker Tesla uses the exact same batteries in its Tesla cars . Over 7,000 of the batteries are packed into the battery model that powers the Model S and Model X Tesla . <p> They have also been used in the Tesla Powerwall battery packs for home use , which Tesla has been selling in New Zealand for the last couple of years . Tesla has provided inspiration for Kennett 's own DIY powerwall efforts . <p> " There are brilliant little ideas we steal from them , " he says . <p> " For instance , Tesla has a fuse on every single battery cell . If any individual cell goes bad , it will blow the fuse and disconnect itself from the system . " <p> Kennett bought a 3D printer to make his own plastic battery housings and added the all-important power inverter and cabling . The cobbled-together system can store around 10kWh ( kilowatt hours ) of energy from the solar panels on his workshop roof . That 's less than the 13.5 kWh capacity of the Powerwall 2 , Tesla 's newest battery pack , which is rated to cover the daily energy needs of a mid-sized home . <p> But it is also vastly cheaper . Kennett estimates he 's spent up to $1,000 on the Powerwall in addition to the outlay on solar panels . A Tesla Powerwall 2 sells for $14,800 including the supporting hardware . Installation can cost $1,600 - $4,500 more . <h> Cheap but time-consuming <p> Then there is the cost of solar panels to factor in . It is a big outlay that offers a return on investment only over the long term , though solar installers have pay as you go plans to take the sting out of writing a big cheque up front . <p> But Kennett is n't advocating DIY powerwalls as a cheaper alternative for the family home . <p> " It is cheap , but it 's an absurdly time-consuming process . I 've enough curiosity and ingenuity to make it work . " <p> While his powerwall is n't connected to mains power , there 's also still a significant safety risk involved . <p> " These batteries are designed to vent rather than explode , but it is possible for them to self-ignite . The key to safety is identifying bad cells . You 've got to do lots of testing . " <p> Who has the time or inclination for that ? Kennett , a Tesla fan , recommends the Powerwall or equivalent from vendors such as LG , for those who want hassle-free battery power to store solar energy produced during the day for use overnight . <p> " Elon Musk 's had a rough year , but Tesla have been putting their minds to it longer than anyone else . They 've come up with some really good solutions to tricky problems . " <p> His plan is to add more battery cells to his own configuration - he has an old computer rack he plans to fill with batteries , so that he can charge his Nissan Leaf in addition to powering his workshop . As for the house , he thinks that will be a stretch for his DIY set-up . <p> " I 've done the numbers on going off-grid and it does n't really make financial sense if you are actually connected to the electricity network , " he says . <p> " But it 's a no br-iner for a bach off the grid . " <p> As for the viability of solar power , he says the price of the panels , electronics and batteries is coming down all the time . <p> " My first 200-watt panel cost $750 . The last panel of the same size cost $250 , " he says . <p> " It 's just getting more and more affordable . " <h> Pedal power <p> His colleagues in the powerwall maker community are also working on standardised powerwall configurations that would make it easier for the more adventurous to put together their own systems . <p> The supply of unwanted batteries certainly is n't likely to disappear anytime soon . Despite the move to thinner pouch batteries in laptops and tablet computers , which are harder to convert to Kennett 's purposes , billions of 18650 batteries continue to be produced every year for a range of devices by the likes of Panasonic , which produces batteries for the Tesla . <p> Mobile phone batteries are no good - by the time we discard them , the batteries tend to be fried . <p> But a new source of batteries is on Kennett 's radar and his cycling connections are helping him get his hands on them . <p> " I 've got good relationships with bike shops around Wellington . They give me dead e-bike batteries , which I rip apart into the individual cells and do the same testing process , " he says . <p> Ultimately , says Kennett , the DIY powerwall project is one little effort to reduce power consumption , which will need to be amplified on a global scale to reduce the worst impacts of climate change . <p> " There are all these batteries being wasted . I 'm just interested in putting them to good use . "
