You can choose any of our great value subscriptions for your renewal . <h> 2019 chemistry news roundup <p> How will you remember 2019 ? As the International Year of the Periodic Table ? As the year that the Nobel committee finally recognised the work of Goodenough , Whittingham and Yoshino on lithium-ion batteries ? Perhaps the year of Brexit or presidential impeachment enquiries ? Or maybe , for you , 2019 will be remembered for the small things , the smiles and the heartbreaks shared between colleagues , friends and family far from the world 's stage . <p> The year was framed by celebration , with the IYPT giving agency to people and organisations worldwide to promote Mendeleev 's greatest achievement and with it the history and spectacle of chemistry . Throughout 2019 , we saw a global community come together to share their passion and knowledge of chemistry , punctuated by the long anticipated awarding of the Nobel prize in chemistry to the lithium pioneers . <p> The same community also bore the hardships of funding gaps , department closures and job losses as the world economy staggered between political and societal upheavals . We also came to terms with the worsening mental wellbeing of students , and the gaps in opportunity and recognition for different groups of people working in science . <p> And yet a new year brings with it a chance to improve , learn and challenge ourselves afresh and , perhaps , put our hard-won resilience to the test again . 2020 is the year that you could change the course of history . And if that does n't work out for you , you can at least enjoy the robot Olympics . For now , here 's our pick of some of the top stories in 2019 . <h> Bringing out the best <p> The celebration of 150 years of the periodic table has given the public the opportunity to brush up on their school chemistry and revisit the most iconic symbol of the subject . The chemistry community has been in fine fettle for this celebratory year , indulging both its passion for the subject and a sense of humour about its place in the world , as well an urge to educate everyone about why chemistry is so interesting and why it matters to everyone 's lives . This year has seen the world 's smallest and largest periodic tables built , several of the world 's oldest ones uncovered and many more creative and edible versions made . Coupled with events and conferences it 's really helped to bring the topic alive for a new audience , teaching them about the history of the table and why this simple , yet powerful , idea has been so important for chemistry . <p> A personal highlight for Chemistry World this year was our collaboration with Michele Ceriotti and his colleagues at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology -- joining together two of this year 's big trends by using a machine-learning algorithm to map the elements based purely on their properties . As Ceriotti was keen to convey -- our experiment is about showing the potential of machine learning , but also where its limitations lie to address some of the hype . Because there 's plenty of that around . <p> To cap it all , we 've had over two hundred people take part in a re-recording of Tom Lehrer 's elements song , including a new verse with 16 superheavy elements that had n't been synthesised when Lehrer first wrote the song 60 years ago . I do n't know if he 's heard it , but I think he 'd approve . <h> Dawn of the digital chemistry era <p> The march of the machines ' drumbeat steadily intensifies . We 've followed the progress of artificial intelligence and machine learning in chemistry for some time now and this year the field has taken some notable steps . <p> In pharma R&amp;D , AI is joining the disparate dots of vast clinical trial databases and structure -- activity relationships to spot new targets and drugs , or new targets for old or failed drugs . And we 've seen a raft of machine-learning algorithms that shortcut computationally costly calculations such as DFT to make highly accurate predictions for molecules and materials in a fraction of the time . <p> Some of the most exciting applications of AI are happening in synthesis design . The idea of a synthetic synthesis expert has come a long way since E J Corey 's early attempts to codify chemistry in a computer , largely thanks to advances in computer science and the exponential effect of Moore 's Law on microprocessor technology . These electronic experts capitalise on the huge amounts of information that exists in the literature ( and in chemists ) -- deriving their chemical knowledge either directly from documents or databases , or with a helping hand in the form of curated datasets constructed by humans . <p> One of the earliest successful entrants was Bartosz Grzybowski 's Chematica , which we first covered back in 2014 . Since then it has been bought by Merck KGaA in 2017 who commercialised it as Synthia , and rival commercial synthesis tools are growing in number . Earlier this year , Grzybowski showed how Chematica can evade patented routes to a drug molecule , adding IP protection to the list of sectors facing disruption from AI . Of course , these systems still have limits -- complex natural product syntheses is still a challenge , for example . Although Grzybowski promises we are now close to even that milestone . <p> These algorithmic assistants can extrapolate as well as interpolate , making intelligent connections between and beyond the data they 've been given . So they are capable of making genuinely new discoveries . Lee Cronin 's chemputer pairs synthesis planning with robotic automation to rapidly test and inform its predicted pathways , performing autonomous reaction research that hopes to take the legwork out of lab work in reaction discovery . <p> Coverage of these reports often prompts debate among the chemistry community about the changing role of chemistry and chemists , sometimes provoking defensive responses about the impossibility of replacing humans and about the ineffable , inimitable creative capacity of the human brain . Those responses might be right , but the most edifying discussions generally conclude that chemistry is about to get much more interesting as chemist and computer collaborate together . <h> Chemistry solutions to global warming problems <p> The evidence is clear : sea levels are rising , landscapes are changing and extreme weather events are becoming more common . Strategies to mitigate climate change must be ambitious and governments must stop sitting on their hands to stop global warming from running out of control . <p> Championing renewable energy will only get us so far . The intermittent nature of renewable energy means developing sustainable ways to store and transport energy is more pertinent than ever before . Batteries are playing an important role in weaning society off fossil fuels so many were satisfied that the 2019 Nobel prize in chemistry honoured three scientists who were integral to our rechargeable world . <p> Chemists are also increasingly seeing carbon dioxide as a valuable raw material . This year we have brought you research on ways to turn carbon dioxide into speciality chemicals , liquid fuels , edible protein and sugars . Whilst 2019 saw people unite across generations and borders to participate in protests demanding action on climate change , chemisty has again underlined how central it will be to a world that runs on renewable feedstocks and consumes less energy .
