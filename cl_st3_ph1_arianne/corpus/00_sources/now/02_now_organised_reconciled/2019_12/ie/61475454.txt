<h> What Australian books should young people read ? <p> Alfred Tatum , a US education professor specialising in literacy for African American boys , coined the term " bookprint " . He said it 's something we all have -- a list of books that have impacted how we see ourselves and the world . <p> What we read matters . Reading shapes the way we see the world , increases our understanding of others , and helps us imagine different narratives for ourselves . <p> School holidays are an ideal time for teenagers to expand their reading repertoire and pay attention to the bookprint they are creating ( and is being created for them ) . It is important for young people to read literature that reflects their own life and also expands their experiences of the world . <p> In this spirit , we suggest five Australian texts that connect with diverse teenagers ' experiences and interests . This is not a quintessential list , but one designed to enhance any young person 's bookprint , at different ages and stages . These texts are by Australian writers , and written in the past five years . <h> 1 . Terra Nullius by Clare G Coleman <p> Recommended for ages 16+ <p> " Terra Nullius " would be my first pick for a book to teach in an Australian Lit course . It 's fascinating , strange , gripping , and a remarkable piece of experimental writing . It 's be fun to design a course starting from there ! https : //t.co/Zv1NY5bxbo <p> In Terra Nullius , Clare G Coleman offers older teenagers the opportunity to immerse themselves in an imaginative response to colonisation . <p> Coleman 's creative representation holds both history and potential futures in tension , and an unexpected plot twist engages and provokes the imagination . <p> This book is an example of both climate fiction and speculative fiction . Climate fiction enables older teenagers to think about the implications of climate change from diverse perspectives , while speculative fiction encourages readers to use their imaginations to consider different futures or pasts . <h> The Tribe by Michael Mohammed Ahmad <p> Recommended for ages 13+ <p> New in , The Tribe by Michael Mohammed Ahmad . It 's rich with tradition , direct &amp; honest from an important new voice . **26;4502;TOOLONG <p> The Tribe , by Arab-Australian writer , editor , teacher and community arts worker Michael Mohammed Ahmad , is short but powerful . It focuses on the experiences of Bani , an Arab-Australian boy , his family and their wider community . <p> The Tribe was Ahmed 's first work of fiction . It insightfully considers issues of identity , family , community , loyalty and love . <p> The text makes clear both the struggle and beauty at the heart of one immigrant family 's experiences of being Australian . The book is richly descriptive , and the reader is carried into the home that is the centre of Bani 's world . <h> 3 . The Yield by Tara June Winch <p> Recommended for ages 16+ <p> Ellen van Neerven on ' The Yield ' by Tara June Winch ( @PenguinBooksAus ) for our August issue . <p> ' Albert 's story-in-dictionary form shows us not only how to read Wiradjuri but also how to feel and speak and taste it ; it decolonises the throat and tongue . ' https : //t.co/NZ0NyCfEbH <p> The question of language , lost and found , begins Tara June Winch 's latest novel . The Yield traces the history of a family in Massacre Plains on the banks of the Murrumby River . <p> After her grandfather 's death , August Gondiwindi returns to her family 's land . The story of her return is interspersed with the dictionary written by her grandfather before his death . <p> These complementary narratives reveal the complexity of place , voice , language and family in the Australian context and force a consideration of what has been lost . <p> While moments of violence and dispossession are central to the story , there is also tenderness and beauty in this novel by one of Australia 's most exciting authors . <h> 4 . Growing Up African in Australia , edited by Maxine Beneba Clarke <p> Recommended for ages 15+ <p> I 'm excited that Growing Up African in Australia is out next month . I have a story in there , alongside Faustina Agolley , Khalid Warsame , Tariro Mavondo and several other authors . It 's edited by Maxine Beneba Clarke .... https : //t.co/RF9I4tSkf1 <p> Like the others in the Growing Up series , this compilation acts as an intersection at which we can reflect on the similarities and differences of young people growing up across the country . <p> One section of the anthology , named " The Body " , is particularly noteworthy , with a range of authors describing their experiences grappling with body difference and self-esteem . <p> For some young readers this will provide a moment of " I thought it was just me " . It might help others to see cultural beauty standards -- and the challenges of growing up within them -- through another 's point of view . <h> 5 . Meet Me at the Intersection , edited by Rebecca Lim and Ambelin Kwaymullina <p> Like the Growing Up series , this new collection offers much-needed new voices and perspectives for young adult readers . <p> Conceived as a collection of stories about marginalised people , told by people from these marginalised groups , Meet Me at the Intersection presents stories , poems , and memoirs from First Nations writers , writers living with a disability , LGBTIQA+ writers and writers from diverse cultural backgrounds . <p> Including a range of established and emerging writers , this collection reminds us identities are complex , created at the intersections of race , disability and sexuality , and that we are collectively a richer nation if all voices can be heard . <p> Historically underrepresented people including Aboriginal writers , writers of colour , migrant writers , queers writers and writers living with disability are particularly underrepresented . <p> Yet , we know it is of paramount importance Australian teenagers are able to locate their literary imaginations locally as well as globally , and that reading texts by and about diverse Australians will change the ways all young people see themselves and their communities . <p> For a longer list of Australian texts both historical and contemporary , see the Reading Australia website . This resource is designed for teachers , but is also a great starting place for parents and teenagers .
