<p> Continued destruction of its tropical forests -- measured in tens of thousands of square kilometres per year -- could transform much of the Amazon into dry savannah , with massive impacts on climate and biodiversity worldwide , experts warn . <p> Covering more than five million square kilometres ( two million square miles ) , the Amazon 's dense tropical canopy store a huge amount of carbon , roughly equivalent to 10 times the amount humanity discharges into the atmosphere every year . <p> About 20 percent of the Amazon rainforest has disappeared over the last half-century . <p> Vast tracts of tree cover have been cleared , mostly for the production of lumber , soy , palm oil , biofuels or beef . <p> -- Carbon sink -- <p> When a tree is cut , its stored carbon will leak into the atmosphere gradually ; when it is burned , the CO2 escapes all at once . Either way , it contributes to global warming . <p> At the same time , global forests -- and especially the tropics -- soak up 25 to 30 percent of the carbon dioxide that humanity spews into the atmosphere . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ these CO2 " sinks " , Earth 's surface temperature would already be hotter , and the risk of runaway global warming that much higher . <p> Of the approximately 160,000 km2 of tropical tree cover lost worldwide in 2017 , 35 percent was in the Amazon , and more than a quarter in Brazil , according to the World Resources Institute ( WRI ) , an environmental policy think tank . <p> " The world 's tropical forests are now in the emergency room , " Frances Seymour , a distinguished senior fellow at the WRI , told AFP earlier this year . <p> " The health of the planet is at stake . With every hectare lost , we are that much closer to the scary scenario of runaway climate change .
