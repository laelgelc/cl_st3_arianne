<p> By Ifeoluwa Adenike Alade , PhD candidate , University of Ibadan , Nigeria <p> Food and nutrition insecurity is a chronic problem for Africa . Despite decades of humanitarian and development efforts , one in five Africans remain undernourished and a third of Africa 's children are stunted . <p> One little-known crop that is essential to food security on the continent is amaranth . This leafy green vegetable is one of the most consumed in Africa and accounts for up to 25 per cent of daily protein intake in some countries , making it essential to their health and wellbeing . <p> What 's more , it is a plant that is quick to grow , thanks to its highly efficient photosynthesis process , and which thrives on degraded land even in hot and dry weather , making it an increasingly important crop to mitigate the impact of climate change on food production . <p> But one threat that holds back amaranth from making an even greater contribution to Africa 's food security is the amaranthus leaf webber , a species of moth that is commonly found in the tropics . However , long-term pesticide use comes with challenges , including the development of resistance among leaf webbers . <p> So , what if farmers had another tool to stop this pest ? <p> This question motivated me to search for new crop protection techniques that could be affordable , effective , and sustainable , and it turns out that nature may have already come up with a solution . <p> Many plants have evolved ways to deter pests through releasing biochemicals that repel harmful insects .
