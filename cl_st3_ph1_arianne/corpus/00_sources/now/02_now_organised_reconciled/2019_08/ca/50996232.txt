<p> As the days of summer burn out one by one , the much-dreaded return to autumn is on its way . <p> That means two things many Canadians dread -- the coming of a new school year , and a federal election . <p> With the sun occasionally shining and an election around the corner , politicians are beginning to show their faces more and more in communities around Newfoundland and Labrador . <p> In anticipation of the campaign , CBC News spoke with retired Memorial University political science professor Stephen Tomblin about his predictions and asked his opinions on the biggest issues this time around . <h> Provinces fighting for themselves <p> Throughout the conversation , one thing came up continuously : Premiers are out for their people and their regions , even when it means sticking it to another province ( or the rest of the country ) in the process . <p> " The agenda is about what does Quebec want ? What does Newfoundland and Labrador want ? Not what is good for Canada , " he said . <p> Canada 's premiers listen as Saskatchewan Premier Scott Moe addresses them during a meeting @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Press ) <p> During the Council of the Federation meetings this month in Saskatchewan , premiers all made pitches for what they want . Newfoundland and Labrador Premier Dwight Ball made it clear he wants national pharmacare and an energy corridor through New Brunswick . Alberta and Saskatchewan were out for pipelines . <p> But Tomblin said nobody was out for unity , and that 's a problem in a vast country of 37 million people . <h> Anger and division <p> When asked if he thought it could contribute to the same kind of politics we 've seen in the United States , Tomblin said it 's already here . <p> " There 's an underlying frustration , I mean , not only in Canada but I think in Europe and North America generally , that people feel disconnected , " he said . " That 's why there 's so much anger , so much volatility , and so much unpredictability in terms of how this is going to unfold .
