The study included 166 surveys of abundance across 1,676 locations around the world , with some data going as far back as 1928 . The majority of the locations surveyed had already been experiencing high rates of human activity prior to the execution of the surveys . <p> Originally conceptualised in 2017 , the objective of the study was to evaluate how sharply insect populations are falling or rising . <h> How did fears of an insect apocalypse spring up ? <p> Concerns over an insect apocalypse were first sparked in February 2019 , following the publishing of the first worldwide scientific review of insect species that found that dwindling populations could yield a " catastrophic collapse of nature 's ecosystems . " <p> Another report that focussed on sites in the UK appeared to corroborate the findings of the February study , stating that approximately 40 per cent of the 1 million known species of insects could be staring at extinction , also noting that 23 bee and wasp species had already been wiped off the planet over the last century . <p> The latest study though , suggests that the situation may be far more nuanced than earlier believed . <h> What does the latest study reveal ? <p> Using data slanted towards Europe and North America , it found that populations of insect species including dragonflies , beetles , and mayflies that spend a large part of their lives in freshwater , are actually increasing at a rate of 11 per cent every decade . The populations of their land-dwelling counterparts though were falling at a rate of 0.9 per cent per year , or 9 per cent per decade . <p> The study 's authors stated that while insects are unlikely to disappear any time soon , there was cause for concern since the data meant that , in the absence of preventive measures and regulation , the populations of land-dwelling insects could fall by a quarter over the next three decades . They also noted that it is highly unlikely that there was one single overarching reason for the decline , primarily due to the sparse and varied nature of the data used in the study . <p> Roel van Klink , an entomoligst at the German Center for Integrative Biodiversity Research in Leipzig , and one of the co-authors of the study listed multiple causes including the increased use of pesticides , human encroachment into insect habitats , rising rates of urbanisation , climate change , and light population , all potentially contributing to the decline in varying degrees . <p> The authors also stressed that much more data is required from different sites before a more robust understanding of insect population trajectories could materialise . Only two datasets were used from the continent of Africa , while data from Australia was also limited . No surveys from India were used in the study . It is also worth noting that data from North America formed an outsized share of all data used .
