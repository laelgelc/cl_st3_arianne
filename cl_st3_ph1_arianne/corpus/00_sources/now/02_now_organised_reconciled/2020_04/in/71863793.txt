<h> A day in dystopia <p> Authors list their preferred sci-fi texts , which might as well reflect the uncertain state of the world we are living in right now <p> Life as we know it has turned seriously dystopic in the last few weeks . As the definition of ' normal ' mutates drastically , this might be a good opportunity to delve into tales of other lives and other universes . Science fiction , after all , is said to mirror our own world through warped glasses . Here are recommendations from some of the best in the business . <p> Children of Time by Adrian TchaikovskyThis is a spellbinding work of imagination that stayed with me long after I first read it . The earth is dying , and humankind sends out terraforming ships to prepare other planets for its arrival . And then , years later , ships bearing humans arrive on one of these planets -- to find that matters have gone horribly wrong , and instead of the glorious civilisation that was supposed to receive them , what awaits them on their new home is beyond their worst nightmares . <p> I do n't want to give away too much about the book , but the sheer intelligence with which it is written and imagined is a standout even in a world full of dazzling books . There 's a second in the series out now , Children of Ruin , which I ca n't wait to devour . <p> Invisible Planets by various authors , translated and edited by Ken LiuKen Liu is one of my favourite authors in his own right , both of short stories in science fiction and epic novels in fantasy , but he 's also known around the world for being a phenomenal translator of Chinese science fiction . He 's introduced several of their genre classics to the English-speaking world . Invisible Planets is a great collection of various renowned Chinese speculative fiction authors , and a good introduction to the field , which is gaining importance globally for cleverly using imaginary worlds to talk about present-day issues and avoid censorship . A great place to meet authors like Cixin Liu , Hao Jingfang , Chen Qiufan and Xia Jia , fall in love with their ideas and find more of their work to read . I 'm currently reading Broken Stars , another Ken Liu translated short stories collection . <p> ***** <p> The Competent Authority by Shovon ChowdhuryMumbai has been destroyed . Delhi is under reconstruction . West Bengal is now a protectorate of China , and the Southern states are an oasis of peace ' no one has visited in a long time ' . India 's fictional futures appear bleak -- environmental disasters and global climate change , **26;32196;TOOLONG riots , gender violence clashes , state antipathy , religious extremism , economic inequalities , and an acute shortage of resources horrifies , yet forces one to rethink our today . Samuel R Delany asserts that science fiction is a narrative convention that employs the " future " to present distortions of the " present " . The Competent Authority not only extrapolates a fictional near-future , but also satirises the present . I recommend this rib-tickling , rollercoaster of a narrative -- India 's answer to Douglas Adams ( though with a few drops of shorsher tel ) . You wo n't be able to comprehend whether to laugh at the absolute brilliance of the satire or cry in the face of what stares back at you from the pages of this novel . <p> The Gollancz Book of South Asian Science FictionThe dystopian virus had infected science fiction much before Covid-19 arrived on the scene . While this Gollancz anthology of 28 stories -- and a foreword and introduction -- does n't cover the whole of South Asia , and not all stories in it are science fiction , it does adopt a dulce et utile philosophy that instructs and entertains . It connects the dots between our pasts , presents , and futures , between our history , myth , folklore , and science , and functions within an interconnected matrix of technology , culture , politics , and society . Edited by Tarun Saint , it features originals from stalwarts of Indian science fiction such as Manjula Padmanabhan , Vandana Singh , Anil Menon and Priya Sarukkai Chabria , and translations of greats such as Rahul Sankrityayan , Adrish Bardhan , Harishankar Parsai , apart from rising voices of speculative fiction from ' partitioned three ' . The paranoid anticipation of a looming dystopia can be the most efficient portal to a utopia -- and nothing prepares us better for this than science fiction . <p> ****** <p> The Clockwork Orange by Anthony BurgessThe novel works at two levels . First , the book is written in a kind of future-slang , which keeps the reader constantly uneasy about what 's being said . Second , it 's about very basic values , the kind that anyone would instantly respond to . Initially , we 're so sure of how we feel about the young thug through whose eyes and mind we experience the hateful crimes he commits . We loathe him . We want him to be crushed , to be erased from memory . Then the second part begins and , once again through this worthless brute 's eyes , we find ourselves sympathising with him . It 's an extraordinary reversal . Visceral , morally disturbing , profound . This is a book that causes deep , long term shifts in the reader 's perceptions of reality . I read it in my late teens and it still moves me , just to think of it . <p> The Sirian Experiments by Doris Lessing <p> This is the third of five books . The first , Shikasta , is presented as a series of reports by inter-galactic " agents " . They 're struggling to describe the calamity that has befallen the planet they call Shikasta -- which we call Earth . Of the five books , Experiments is the one that stopped my heart . In it , Lessing reveals the enormous scale of her narrative , showing us our world and its many cultures from the perspective of a god-like being , a Sirian . Any number of SF authors has attempted similar feats but Lessing 's version feels terrifyingly authentic . These books are NOT easy reading . They challenge one 's core beliefs about reality and morality , truth and history . They present a vision of beauty that is almost unbearably sweet .
