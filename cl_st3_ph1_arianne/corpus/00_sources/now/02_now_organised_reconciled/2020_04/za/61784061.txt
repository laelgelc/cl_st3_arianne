<h> Does coronavirus reveal a ' World Without Us ' ? <h> Los Angeles ( tca/dpa ) -- Skies have cleared over the smoggiest urban centres , wildlife runs free in parks , streets and plazas , flowers rise from the cracks of formerly well-trodden sidewalks and birdsong replaces the more motorised score of modern daily life ... <p> With much of the world 's population on COVID-19 lockdown , nature has never seemed more gloriously present -- sublimely indifferent to human anxieties , eager to run riot over all the places in which it was once controlled , to return to the spaces from which it was expelled . <p> Meanwhile , in his Massachusetts home , environmental journalist Alan Weisman is getting many calls from people in many nations who all want to know the same thing : <h> Are we beginning to see the world without us ? <p> " The World Without Us " is Weisman 's 2007 book , which imagines how the Earth would adjust should humans simply vanish one day . How long before the cities would return to meadows , the suburbs surrender to forests ? How long before the bridges fall and the monuments crumble -- before animals repopulated , some from the brink of extinction , and all trace of human progress vanished ? <h> WIN a R 2,000 Woolworths Voucher <p> Subscribe to our Free Daily All4Women Newsletter to enter <p> Not , in many cases , as long as you 'd think . Which is why so many people want to know if the explosion of bears in Yosemite , the miraculously clear skies and the cacophony of birdsong everywhere mean that his thought experiment is coming true . <p> " I have been inundated by people sending me pictures of swans in the Venetian canals , or wild turkeys in the streets , " he told me . " A journalist who lives in Gurugram , which is No. 1 on the list of the world 's most polluted cities , just told me she is seeing blue skies . I even got an email from a nuclear physicist in Chernobyl , who I interviewed for the book , saying that whole towns look like Chernobyl now , silent and full of plant growth . " <h> In the last two weeks , Weisman had done interviews with outlets in India , Denmark , Argentina , Spain , Portugal , Iran . <p> " People are suspended between terror and wonder , " he said . " They 're terrified that this is all so fragile , but they also realise there are things we have been missing -- the birdsong everyone is noticing , the beautiful skies -- and that those things are important . " <p> For the record , Weisman does not think that COVID-19 will bring about the kind of scenarios he imagined in " The World Without Us , " but he does see parallels , in both reality and metaphor . One section of the book that many people have mentioned to him lately is a scene in which Jerry Del Tufo , a structural engineer who manages the bridges that link Staten Island to the mainland , explains how , without human vigilance , nature could vanquish even a model of modern design like the Bayonne Bridge . <p> " Every connection is vulnerable , " Weisman writes , and though he is describing a bridge , he is also describing the world with us . <p> " A lot of people realise that we are in this situation that looks so powerful , our economy , our society , " he said , " and now this one little infection comes out of one animal in one marketplace in one part of Wuhan and it can explode across the world that fast . It 's not even that lethal and yet look how it has brought us to our knees . " <h> Peace <p> At the same time , he said , " people talk to me about how it 's so peaceful and the streets look so nice when they 're not clogged . ... People are just moving back and forth between feeling really scared and feeling oddly exhilarated . " <p> Weisman , who has spent much of his life writing about the complicated and crucial relationship between our growing population and our increasingly battered environment , hopes that when humans emerge from our coronavirus quarantine , we do so with a greater appreciation for nature . And not just because of heart-lifting birdsong or the awesome beauty of Yosemite without lines of traffic . <p> Natural spaces , he said , provide barriers between humans and the microbes and diseases that exist in the wild . " National parks are so important because without them , and without all the forests and the jungles , we will be coming into more contact with viruses like COVID-19 . " <h> Likewise , if we continue to push species to extinction , " well , the viruses have to go somewhere . " <p> Indeed , Weisman followed " The World Without Us " with " Countdown : Our Last Best Hope for a Future on Earth ? " -- a 2013 L.A. Times book prize winner that examines the perils of global population growth and the many ways it pushes us toward the chilling scenario of " The World Without Us . " <p> One of those ways is , obviously , a pandemic . Like many , Weisman hopes that this coronavirus will serve as a wake-up call to prepare for the possibility of a more lethal virus in the future . <p> More important , however , he hopes that the speed with which COVID-19 literally spanned the globe proves once and for all that we are all part of the same ecosystem and it is our personal and collective responsibility to protect it . <h> " We are living in the middle of a metaphor , " he said <p> " Anything that can happen anywhere can have an impact on us . We are 7.9 billion people so tightly bound by our travel and trade that a small , relatively weak virus can reach all of us in a matter of months . So it does n't matter if the smokestacks are in L.A. or Beijing or Tehran . The atmosphere does not care where it started . <p> " But information can travel just as fast as the coronavirus , and maybe now people will understand . A virus is invisible but deadly ; climate change is invisible and even deadlier . " <p> When COVID-19 hit , Weisman was at work on a book about climate change ; he should be in the midst of a very ambitious itinerary of international research trips that he will reschedule as soon as it is safe . <p> " I 'm 73 , " he said , " so why am I doing all this traveling ? Because my species is facing a crisis . Because if we get past two degrees ( increase in global temperature ) , the younger generations may not even get a chance at a full life cycle even without a pandemic . " <p> The book , he says , will look at reasonable expectations for the future , the obstacles that need to be overcome and the people who continue to work for a future in which everyone realises the lesson of the coronavirus : That there is no us and them , there is only us.
