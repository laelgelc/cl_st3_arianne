<h> Veterinarian was as ' rare and exotic ' as her patients <h> By Jennie Churchill and Sandra Steele <p> April 1 , 2020 -- 9.46am <p> Dr Michele Margaret Cotton : January 6 , 1948 - January 13 , 2020 <p> A veterinary scientist alternately described as a Renaissance woman , Dr Michele Cotton used her left and right brain ( often it seemed , simultaneously ) , joyously racing through life amassing , and using with impact , skills and knowledge across multiple fields of interest . <p> Collecting degrees throughout her career -- bachelor of veterinary science , bachelor of science ( vet ) in clinical biochemistry , masters in veterinary public health management and diploma of international animal health -- this endlessly curious scientist with a deep and infectious love of learning had a huge impact on the Australian and international veterinary communities and allied professions over 45 years . Her footprints can be followed from rural NSW to Sydney , Seattle , the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia , Jordan and Zimbabwe , and back to Sydney . <p> Michele with Oryx in Jordan . <p> Michele graduated in 1973 from the University of Sydney at a time when female vets were few , and began her career in country practice at Bathurst , close to her home town of Oberon . Her colleagues remember clients " enjoying her immensely " as she unhesitatingly tackled tough , physically demanding cases . Recognising her passion for clinical pathology , they supported her establishment of one of the few comprehensive diagnostic laboratories in private veterinary practice at the time . <p> Advertisement <p> Returning to the veterinary faculty at the University of Sydney , research in clinical pathology and teaching microbiology , parasitology and necropsy techniques followed , before marriage to the physician and endocrinologist Dr Roman Judzewitsch intervened . Travel and work in exotic , often challenging destinations became the norm . <p> Michele Cotton with daughter Sofia and a chimpanzee at home in Saudi Arabia . <p> Saudi Arabia , via Seattle and a research role at Washington University , became home from 1982 for almost 20 years . Cotton never lost her love for the Middle East . She returned often , later working from 2012 to 2014 for AusAID , through Australian Volunteers International , as the first veterinary advisor to the Oryx Conservation Project in Jordan 's Aqaba and Wadi Rum . <p> As a sideline , she advised local vets working with the goats , sheep , horses and camels of the Bedouin . Fearless , charming and good at her job , the Bedouin welcomed her into their homes and tents , often telling Dr Cotton she would make a good wife . <p> Michele Cotton with a camel in Jordan . <p> Her considerable intellect was matched by her resourcefulness and capacity to improvise . She just got on with addressing the need when confronted with animal health and welfare issues . While Roman worked at the King Faisal Specialist Hospital in Riyadh , Michele -- young , female professional -- forged her own multi-faceted veterinary career within a strict Middle Eastern culture . <p> Her public roles in Saudi Arabia crossed many species , from providing clinical pathology and microbiological services to the Riyadh Equestrian Centre , consulting to Masstock Dairies ( Middle Eastern milk company Almarai ) from 1982 to 1984 during a worldwide Escherichia coli mastitis outbreak , to providing veterinary services to the Riyadh airport detector dog program . <p> Dr Michele Cotton with a raptor used for falconry and a client in Saudi . <p> The opportunity to become consultant veterinarian to Riyadh Municipality Zoo from 1985 to 1992 ignited a passion for wildlife conservation -- Dr Cotton did n't just talk about conservation , she lived it . While holding responsibility for the health of hundreds of zoo species , she worked with the staff of London Zoo as they reintroduced Arabian wildlife species back into Saudi reserves . <p> Michele and Roman 's three children , Andrei , Sofia and Peter , shared their Riyadh home with a passing parade of exotic animals and Saudi royalty visiting her not-so-public veterinary practice . Veterinary medications were sourced through her legendary international network , the kitchen became the pathology lab with diagnostic stains boiled up on the stove , and the cats of local sheiks and princesses were desexed on the kitchen table ( after she single-handedly anaesthetised these often-wild felines in string carry bags ) . <p> Roman 's diagnosis with mesothelioma in 1999 saw the return of the family to Australia from their beloved Middle East . Widowed in April 2000 , Michele returned to Sydney University , where she became director of the Postgraduate Foundation in Veterinary Science ( now the Centre for Veterinary Education ) , introducing innovative online continuing education courses for vets and vet nurses . In 2006 she was awarded the prestigious Belle Bruce Reid Medal as an outstanding female veterinary science graduate . <p> Michele Cotton with a baby camel and a Bedouin client in Aqaba , Jordan . <p> Perhaps what most endeared Dr Cotton to her profession was her generous and selfless sharing of experience and knowledge . She was renowned for engendering an enthusiasm for learning . While holding the role of director , she continued to lecture part-time on the parasitology of wildlife , fish and birds . Her students were captivated : in the words of one , the " rare and exotic " Michele was " wondrous to behold for a young female veterinary student trying to work out how I might manage balancing a career I was passionate about and a family once graduated " . <p> At a time when women were n't strongly represented in the profession , Dr Cotton mentored many female veterinarians in particular who chose to pursue non-clinical or unusual veterinary roles such as veterinary public health , One Health , epidemiology and wildlife medicine . <p> In 2015 Michele co-founded Global Veterinary Solutions , an Australian consultancy that seeks to promote non-traditional veterinary roles . She wrote in a March 2016 blog : " We have to agree that being a veterinarian is often more about people and communities than it is about individual animal health ... Veterinarians are perfectly placed to bridge the human/animal interface , especially now when there is so much more emphasis being placed on emerging diseases " . How prescient , she would not have been surprised about the emergence of COVID-19 . <p> Alongside her professional roles , Dr Cotton was a board member of the Australian Veterinary Association ( AVA ) and committee member of the AVA Veterinary Conservation Biologists and Veterinarians in Public Health . She was twice president of the Linnean Society of NSW and council member of both the Friends of the Royal Botanic Gardens and the University of Sydney Near Eastern Archaeological Foundation . <p> Elected to the council of the Royal Agricultural Society of NSW in 2003 , she served on numerous committees as an honorary councillor for a further 16 years and played a key role in developing the show society 's biosecurity guidelines . She never missed a Royal Easter Show , volunteering 12-hour days , working with the show 's veterinarians and delighting in the opportunity to interact with exhibitors and their animals . <p> Michele Cotton despaired at the lack of action on climate change , extinction and poaching . <p> Dr Cotton was both a scientist and an artist , an accomplished wildlife and botanic photographer and artist with a deep knowledge of botany and love of plants . An enduring image is of a somewhat short , inveterate traveller in a pith helmet , weighed down with binoculars , several camera bodies and lenses , a zoom lens that was almost as tall as she was , sketch pads and GPS . <p> She despaired at the uncertain , frightening future of the planet -- the lack of action on climate change , species extinction , and poaching in Africa and Asia . Her particular love of pangolins and rhinos saw Africa become another conservation focus . She travelled there regularly , becoming a dedicated supporter of the Zimbabwe-based SAVE African Rhino Foundation . A rhino called Michele now honours her legacy . <p> Michele first showed signs of motor neurone disease in April 2019 on a trip to the Broome Bird Observatory , where she 'd joined a group counting shorebirds as they departed on their arduous annual migration to Siberia . Her decline was rapid and relentless , but her humour , intense intellectual curiosity , love of nature and compassion for animals and humans stayed to the end . <p> Michele Cotton is survived by her children and partners Andrei and Kelli , Sofia and Nathan , and Peter , and grandchildren Roman and Elena .
