A wave of sewage -- enough to fill almost 30 Olympic-sized swimming pools -- washed into the river when London was deluged by a storm that broke a 127-year record for one-day rainfall . <p> Diverting trash from its rapidly filling landfill , recycling , encouraging cycling , even electric vehicle charging stations -- all have become environmental priorities in London , yet still the pollution of the river from such overflows persists . <p> Eight times last year , 11 in 2018 and 17 the year before that . <p> It averages out to about one a month . <p> Environmentalists have called out London about its filthy habit before . <h> Advertisement <p> Story continues below <p> This advertisement has not loaded yet , but your article continues below . <h> Article content continued <p> In more than one report , Ontario 's environmental czar similarly rapped cities like London for the same problem , one that erupted 766 times in 2018 -- the latest year for which full figures are available -- in the province 's 44 municipalities that , like London , still operate the so-called combined sewer systems at the heart of the issue . <p> Why does it keep happening ? <p> How can a city that says it 's doing its best to fix the problem allow more than 68 million litres of sewage -- nearly 12 times all its overflows last year -- to run into the Thames in a single day , as happened in the Jan. 11 storm ? <p> The why has long been known . A fraction of the city 's sewers , about 15 km out of roughly 2,800 km , throwbacks to an earlier time , are a key culprit . " <p> London has a nearly $285-million plan on the books to fix the problem , one that will take the better part of 20 years to complete , replacing old sewer systems and upgrading plants that handle wastewater .
