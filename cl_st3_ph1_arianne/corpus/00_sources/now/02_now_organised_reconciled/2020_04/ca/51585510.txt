We 've saved the whales , more or less . Now , scientists say we have a chance to save the rest of the life in the oceans , just by expanding what 's already happening around the globe.THE CANADIAN PRESS/AP Photo/Reed Saxon <p> Now , scientists say , we have a chance to save the rest of the life in oceans by expanding what 's already happening around the globe . <p> " A lot of us have chronicled ocean depletion over the years , " said Boris Worm , a marine ecologist at Dalhousie University in Halifax and a co-author of a paper published Wednesday in Nature . <p> " A lot of us , recently , have seen signs of hope . Not to say that the world is getting better wholesale . But there are now hundreds and hundreds of examples that when we do something , the ocean displays remarkable resilience . " <p> Worm and his 14 international co-writers take care to list the formidable challenges that the world 's oceans face . <p> At least one-third of fish stocks are over-harvested . A similar fraction of crucial marine habitat has been lost . <p> The seas continue to get warmer , more acidic , more oxygen-deprived and more polluted . Up to 12 million tonnes of plastic is added every year . <p> But the paper concludes local and regional conservation efforts are adding up . <p> Commercial fishing pressure is starting to decline . Controls on fertilizer and sewage dumping instituted years ago are paying off . <p> In 2000 , less than one per cent of the Earth 's oceans had some form of legal protection . Now the figure is almost eight per cent -- double that in Canada . <p> Nearly half of 124 marine mammal species are increasing , some significantly . The proportion of fish stocks that are fished sustainably increased to 68 per cent in 2012 from 60 per cent in 2000 . <p> Species such as humpback whales have been restored to their historic baseline . <p> Particularly heartening is the rebirth of what Worm calls " marine infrastructure " -- habitats such as mangrove swamps , kelp forests and seagrass beds that host much undersea life as well as provide important flood protection for coastal communities . <p> " Even habitat loss is less prevalent than it was before . " <p> Worm said hundreds of examples worldwide prove that when governments , industry and communities decide to improve the oceans , they 're successful . <p> " We see local efforts that lead to local improvements . We have global examples as well , species that are crossing international boundaries , that were close to extinction , that have rebounded -- in some cases , manyfold . " <p> Elephant seals , for example , are a thousand times more abundant than they once were . <p> Worm and his co-authors conclude that despite all the justified concern over the seas , their ecosystems could be substantially rebuilt by 2050 . That 's if -- and it 's a big if -- governments and societies take the willingness they 've already shown to address problems and focus it on a big issue : climate change . <p> " We need to do a bunch of things we 've been doing all along . But we also have to really deal with climate change . " <p> It would cost the world up to about $28 billion a year to protect enough ocean to have an impact on half of it , the paper concludes . It says the economic return in fisheries and other industries such as ecotourism would be about 10 dollars for every one invested . <p> " This is absolutely doable , " Worm said . <p> " What we really hope is that this provides some good news and more than a glimmer of hope at a time when there 's a lot of despair about facing our global challenges . <p> " There 's reason to be concerned . But there 's literally hundreds and hundreds of examples of ocean recovery . Let 's scale up what we have done locally to a global level . "
