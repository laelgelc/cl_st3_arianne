Go DW ! <p> Mark Kurlansky , the New York author who penned a marvelous book on cod years ago , has written a new and exhaustive history of salmon . His well-illustrated book fills nearly 500 pages and is called Salmon : A Fish , the Earth and the History of Their Common Fate . <p> But , as this review will explain , it should really should be called The Fish Killers ; The River Assassins ; or The Forest Haters . For salmon really have only one mortal predator : humans who behave and think like industrial Europeans . This powerful tribe , which is now doing much to change climate and reduce diversity , worships technological progress and has no respect for the sacred . <p> Right off the top , Kurlansky argues that " if the salmon does not survive , there is little hope for the survival of the planet . " The great fish chronicler Roderick Haig Brown tirelessly issued the same warning decades ago . The reason is simple . Salmon make a reliable barometer of global health because they live their lives in both fresh and ocean waters . They genetically appreciate and enrich biological connections we can not see . If they fail , humans wo n't be far behind them . <p> Salmon also do something heroic . During their final river journey to their birthplaces , they starve themselves . Even while changing shape and colour , they do not eat . <p> That sacrifice leaves nutrition in the river for their newborns . When the adults die after reproducing , they gift their remaining nutrients , harvested from years in the ocean , as food for the forest and its wild creatures . A salmon 's death guarantees greater life . <p> Coho salmon eggs . The black dots are the eyes of the embryos . From Salmon : A Fish , the Earth and the History of Their Common Fate by Mark Kurlansky . Photograph by Eiko Jones . <p> There can be no rain forest without salmon-filled rivers , and no salmon without river-filled forests . The movement of salmon represents one of the world 's great natural flows of energy . <p> Salmon are global elders , too . They became a distinct species about 100 million years ago . Fossilized bones as old as 50 million years have been found in B.C. lakebeds . At one time , notes Kurlansky , there was even a sabretooth salmon more than six feet long . <p> The largest salmon on Earth is this publication 's cherished namesake : the tyee -- also named king or chinook or spring salmon . ( In Campbell River and other parts of B.C. , anglers reserve the name tyee for chinook or spring salmon more than 30 pounds . ) <p> The Chinook Nation on the great Columbia River once traded this fine fish far and wide . The tyee trade even begat a special trade lingo , Chinook Jargon . Within this pared down vocabulary , tyee meant a chief or person of high standing . At one time the chinook boasted the greatest range of any salmon species in the Pacific and could be found in waters from California to Alaska . <p> Due to the cumulative impacts of logging , mining , hatcheries , and damming on rivers , it is now the rarest . <p> Yet the energy and determination of salmon have survived volcanoes , mountain uplifts , floods , centuries of drought , dramatic lava eruptions , and the movements of glacial ice . The Celts believed that a salmon gained more knowledge with each leap , and they were right about that . Kurlansky , who writes elegantly , likes salmon because they have " an unstoppable ability to adapt . " <p> Incredibly , " The difference in DNA between one person and another is far less than the variation between two salmon of the same species from different rivers , " writes the author . Thirty different stocks of sockeye swim the Fraser River and each possess distinct behaviours and spawning places . In many ways salmon shout out the importance of biological diversity . <p> Chum fry in the Campbell River , BC . From Salmon : A Fish , the Earth and the History of Their Common Fate by Mark Kurlansky . Photograph by Eiko Jones . <p> In the past , salmon adapted to changing climates by straying . Not all salmon return to their birthplace with the energy of patriots . A few wander off and move into a new river and give birth to a new clan or tribe . These days , a lot of salmon are straying into Arctic rivers . As temperatures warm and oceans acidify , there is nowhere else to stray after that . <h> Blasting Was n't Enough . Salmon Again Will Be Blocked at Big Bar <p> Before the industrial revolution ( and that 's just 300 years ago ) the Northern Hemisphere boasted hundreds of narrow , fast-moving rivers with forested banks . Salmon occupied them all . Before there was a Paris or a London , salmon swam in the Thames , the Seine , the Duro , the Elbe , the Vistula , the River Dee . <p> Coal-fired factories changed all that with their pollution , sewage and wool washings . Then came a dam-building craze to foster more progress . Scotland was one of the first places to sacrifice fish for electricity . As Kurlansky puts it , " England became a nation of well-lighted homes and dead rivers . " <p> European colonists exported this industrial mindset to the New World and soon the Connecticut , the Merrimack , the Penosbscot rivers were raped and looted of their riches . The colonists often eliminated the protectors of salmon too , such as the Beothuk , who even buried their dead with cured fish because they could not imagine an afterlife without salmon . <p> Next came the lumber barons whose brutal work destroyed salmon nesting places and entire watersheds . Then came " green dams , blue skies " religion that snuffed out salmon runs throughout the Pacific Northwest . Kurlansky has not written a happy book . <p> Yet his history , illustrated with luminous photos by the likes of Ian McAllister , Eiko Jones and Tavish Campbell , is full of insightful revelations . Lewis and Clark , for example , only recognized that they had crossed the Continental Divide when they started to find salmon in rivers flowing to the Pacific . <p> The Nez Perce War led by Chief Joseph in places now named Oregon , Idaho , Wyoming and Montana was fundamentally about protecting salmon fishing rights . White colonizers wanted to turn the Nez Perce into farmers and move them onto the poorest land . Chief Joseph , God bless him , resisted . <p> A bear lines up a coho salmon in the Great Bear Rainforest on British Columbia 's central coast . From Salmon : A Fish , the Earth and the History of Their Common Fate by Mark Kurlansky . Photograph by Ian McAllister . <p> Kurlansky also has a few things to say about hatcheries , the great technological fix for declining stocks created by wholesale river destruction . At the time making more fish sounded like a good idea -- " salmon without rivers " -- but it has n't worked . <p> Hatchery fish just are n't as fit as wild stocks . Hatcheries , however , do serve a political convenience : they give the impression we can battle the depletion of salmon stocks without doing the hard work of river restoration . They are , as fish biologist Jim Lichatowich writes , failed " technological substitutes for place . " <p> At the same time human-caused climate change has entered this confusing picture by warming waters . As Kurlansky notes , salmon navigate by reading ocean clues including temperature and salinity during various stages of their life-cycle . <p> Yet climate change is changing both . Our warming climate is already changing the time salmon spend in rivers and at sea . Their growth rates are shrinking , too . <p> Charles Darwin laid out the specific problem now facing salmon and traditional cultures that respected this life-giving fish in his treatise on evolution , On the Origin of the Species . <p> The smaller a population shrinks , the more likely it is to fail and go kaput . In simple terms , diminished populations of salmon in Pacific Northwest rivers invite the spectacle of extinction . <p> The situation is worse in the Atlantic where but 1.5 million Atlantic salmon remain out of an original population that probably exceeded a billion . Because of interbreeding with escaped farmed fish , these survivors bare little genetic resemblance to their hardy ancestors . <p> Given such facts humans really do n't have much time left to make amends or save themselves for that matter . Kurlansky presents a familiar and wide-ranging list of actions needed to save salmon from oblivion . Most Tyee readers will be familiar with its contents . <p> We must stop cutting down trees in critical watersheds . We must end agricultural pollution in salmon watersheds . We need to reverse climate change . We need to dismantle many dams and stop building more on critical salmon river ways . We need to reduce irrigation . We need to keep cattle and suburban sprawl out of salmon country . We need to stop diminishing the genetic diversity of wild salmon with hatchery fish . We need to stop the spread of lice and deadly diseases from industrial salmon factories . " And after we have accomplished all this , the salmon will be saved . " <p> Here I quibble with Kurlasnky 's analysis . None of these changes will make a difference without a change in cultural thinking . <p> Pink salmon swimming up Lawson Creek to spawn under the northern lights , while Juneau , Alaska is lit up in the background . From Salmon : A Fish , the Earth and the History of Their Common Fate by Mark Kurlansky . Photograph by Christopher Miller . <h> The Deadly Gauntlet Fraser Salmon Must Travel to Come Home <p> Pacific Coast First Nations and Japan 's Ainu regarded salmon as a gift that merited great respect . Moreover , First Nations , unlike their European counterparts , intentionally restricted the use of fishing technology , a very rare behavior in human societies . Over thousands of years , the First Nations learned from their mistakes ( and they made many ) and then they listened to the fish and the river . As a result , beliefs in the sacred presence of salmon combined with taboos against waste prevented over-exploitation . <p> It is noteworthy that Alexander Mackenzie , the first European to accomplish the east to west crossing of America , could n't get over how many rules First Nations had to not offend salmon . But the rules respected the complexity of life . Unless First Nations are allowed to reassert their control over salmon , the fish that still defines British Columbia will probably continue its slide to extinction . Only a people attached to place can care for fish that return home to die . <p> So that means dismantling the government management agencies overseeing the extinction of the salmon . <p> All in all , Kurlansky has written a book that will fill anyone living near a Pacific flowing river with equal parts wonder and rage . <h> Facts matter .
