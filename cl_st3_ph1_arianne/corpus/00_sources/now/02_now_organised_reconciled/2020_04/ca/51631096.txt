<h> Sven Spengemann <p> Sven Spengemann was born in Berlin , Germany and immigrated to Canada with his family at age 15 . He earned his undergraduate degree at the University of Toronto in 1990 . After spending time in the financial services industry , Sven returned to university to pursue his LL.B at Osgoode Hall Law School ( ' 98 ) . He articled with McCarthy T ? trault in Toronto and received his call to the Bar of Ontario in June 2000 . Having left Osgoode with a strong interest in international law , Sven went on to graduate studies at the College of Europe in Bruges , Belgium ( LL.M ' 99 ) and Harvard Law School ( LL.M ' 01 , waived ) . He completed his S.J.D. dissertation ( Harvard , ' 06 ) under the direction of Anne-Marie Slaughter . <p> On April 1 , John Baird visited Baghdad and , having procured the obligatory Green Zone photo , left with the ' announceable ' that he has become the first Canadian foreign minister to have done so in 37 years . <p> Canadians might be led to believe that this was an extraordinary achievement and that their government has embarked on a path to renewed engagement with this important country . They would be wrong on both counts . <p> The Harper administration 's policy of progressive international disengagement is now so obvious that even those among us who tend to follow foreign policy with one eye open now know the formula : Administrative cost-cutting at home and abroad , white-washing and green-washing as required , strategic overtures to stakeholders in industry and trade . Examples include our withdrawal from the Kyoto Protocol and , most recently , the Convention to Combat Desertification . The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change might be next . <p> Baird 's visit to Iraq is just one piece of this larger puzzle . Let 's call it " globe-washing " -- the creation of a fleeting perception that Canada , even in the face of increasing international derision , somehow remains a player on the world stage , this time in the politically complex Middle East . <p> On March 16 , members of the Kurdish community and their friends around the world commemorated the 25th anniversary of the Halabja tragedy , the day on which Saddam Hussein , then dictator of Iraq , launched a devastating chemical attack on the civilian population of this small Kurdish town . At least 5,000 people , including many children , died a horrible death in that attack . Scores more perished between 1986 and 1989 during the dictator 's genocidal Al Anfar campaign . <p> Halabja stands as a stark , distressing symbol of the ongoing persecution of ethnic , cultural or religious differences . The Kurds , a proud and fiercely independent nation , tend not consider themselves to be a " minority , " neither in Iraq nor in the neighbouring countries of Syria , Turkey and Iran . But many other peoples , trapped in the same cycle of religious or ethnic violence and forced displacement , have begun or continue to defend their causes before the international community under the banner of minority rights : Coptic Christians in Egypt ; Turkoman , Sabeans , Mandeans , Christians , Yazidis and Sunni Muslims in Iraq ; Christians and the Ahmadiyya ( whose faith is explicitly ' criminalized ' ) in Pakistan ; and the Christian populations in war-torn Syria . <p> With the recent fall and removal of several long-standing , oppressive strongmen in the Middle East and North Africa ( the MENA zone ) , the world has witnessed a re-assertion of religious and cultural autonomy by minority groups . At the same time , multi-party elections in several countries have brought to the fore new political majorities that are influenced by , if not explicitly linked to , religious parties . A Shiite majority rules in Iraq , and the Sunni Muslim Brotherhood has won a convincing majority at the ballot boxes in Egypt 's recent presidential elections , although there is significant discord over the country 's nascent constitutional process . <p> In early November , we begin to wear the poppy , a solemn symbol to honour those Canadian men and women who fell in the cause of freedom , in the First and Second World Wars and the conflicts that followed , including Afghanistan . On November 11th , on the eleventh hour , we remember them with a minute of silence .
