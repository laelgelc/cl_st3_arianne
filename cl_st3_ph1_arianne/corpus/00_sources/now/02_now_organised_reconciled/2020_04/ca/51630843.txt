<h> Paul Boothe <p> Paul Boothe served as associate deputy minister of Industry and deputy minister of Environment . He is a Fellow of the Institute for Competitiveness and Prosperity and a member of the Ecofiscal Commission . <p> Having served with the Honourable Jim Prentice as a deputy minister in two departments , I have many fond memories of our times together . But one story stands out in particular because it illustrates his dedication to Canada -- his ability to rise above day-to-day politics and take a long view . <p> One of the things you learn early on as a deputy minister in Ottawa is how to suffer in silence . <p> The work of government departments is an easy target for opposition politicians , pundits , lobbyists and , yes , even auditors working with the benefit of hindsight . As a public servant you ca n't respond : You soak it up and move on to deal with the next issue . <p> That 's why , now that I 'm a business professor , it gives me particular pleasure to share my views on the recent report from the Office of the Auditor General ( OAG ) on " support to the automotive sector " . ( To stay on familiar ground , I 'll apply the same standards I would to any research paper submitted by my undergraduate business students . ) <p> The Canada Revenue Agency is under a cloud . While the average Canadian might not feel much sympathy for tax collectors , we need them . The CRA collects taxes for both federal and provincial governments . Without its work , many of the public services we rely on , like hospitals and schools , would cease to function . It 's literally a mission-critical institution for Canada 's economy and society . <p> Our tax system works on the basis of self-assessment , guided by explicit guidelines and rules . Firms and individuals calculate the amount of tax they owe to governments and remit it voluntarily . CRA conducts audits to check whether the proper amounts are remitted , but those cover a very small number of taxpayers . <p> The system works on the basis of taxpayers ' trust and consent . So if taxpayers lose confidence in the CRA 's ability to do its job in a professional , non-partisan manner , the legitimacy of the institution itself is jeopardized , along with the public services it supports . <p> The provincial election has been won and the budget passed . Hopefully , everyone around Queen 's Park is taking a bit of time now to recharge their batteries . Come September , there will be lots of work to do . <p> One issue that was not much of a topic of debate during Ontario 's election campaign was the need for the province to return to fiscal balance . There were significant differences between the parties over how much time to take getting there , and what strategy would work best , but all three parties pledged to balance the budget . So what should should ministers and public servants be doing to make sure the commitment to fiscal balance is honored ? And what should citizens expect regarding government services over the next three years ? <p> The arithmetic laid out in the budget is clear . Over the next three years , average annual health spending will be limited to 2.5 per cent growth . Other program spending will decline at an average annual rate of 0.5 per cent . Election rhetoric and punditry to the contrary , Ontario 's per capita spending is already low relative to other provinces . Further reductions will not be easy , especially as they come on the heels of several years of austerity . A wholesale rethink of what government should do and how it should do it will be needed . <p> This is the third in a series of three articles based on the Future of Canadian Manufacturing Project , a study by Ivey 's Lawrence National Centre for Policy and Management . <p> Most popular discussions on the future of Canadian manufacturing focus on the role of government . This attention is understandable , given that political leaders often promise to ' create ' jobs and claim the credit when the economy is strong . The flip side is that they are often get blamed when the economy is weak . <p> The reality , however , is that except when governments are hiring or on a spending spree , it 's the private sector that creates most of the jobs . The public sector has an important supporting role to play through creating a business environment that encourages the growth that leads to job creation . <p> With the decline in Canadian manufacturing over the past decade , governments have gotten more than their share of the blame . Some of the of the criticism they face comes from focusing on the wrong measures of success ( we discussed this in yesterday 's article ) . Some of it comes from promising to create jobs and then failing to deliver . <p> Canada 's Copenhagen commitment has been in the news lately . Recently we heard that Prime Minister Harper had written to President Obama to propose joint action on energy and environmental issues . Although part of the PM 's goal undoubtedly was to advance the cause of the Keystone XL pipeline in the U.S. , his recognition of the link between environmental performance in the Canadian oil industry and pipeline approvals is a positive sign -- for environmentalists and oil executives both . The prime minister was back in the news last week advocating again in the U.S. for approval of the Keystone pipeline . <p> To see what 's at stake in Mr. Harper 's overture to President Obama , we need to understand the current extent of environmental collaboration between the two countries in the area of greenhouse gas ( GHG ) reduction . Especially important are the matching commitments made by the PM and President Obama at the Copenhagen climate change conference in early 2009 . <p> What did the prime minister and president promise in Copenhagen ? <p> Both leaders pledged to reduce their country 's 2020 GHG emissions by 17 per cent compared to the 2005 base year . While the countries ' commitments were matching , the different structures of their economies meant that they likely would follow quite different routes to fulfill their commitment . To understand why this matters , we need to look first at Canada 's current position and forecasts for our GHG emissions in 2020 . <p> This was a welcome piece of news last week : Prime Minister Harper has written personally to President Obama regarding the Keystone XL project and climate change . If the reports are correct , the PM opened the door to discussions about what Canada might do to make Keystone more attractive to Americans concerned about the environment . <p> So it 's a new strategy -- and not a second too soon . You do n't need to be a marketing genius to know that berating your customers is not the best way to make a sale . Yet that is exactly the approach taken by some Canadian politicians to get the US to approve Keystone . <p> The latest example of this ill-tempered approach to diplomacy appeared recently in the Washington Times , where Wildrose Party leader Danielle Smith published an op-ed suggesting that , unlike President Reagan and other presidents , Obama has not been a good friend to Canada because he has n't approved the pipeline . <p> One of the many interesting aspects of the recent controversy over the expenses of Senators Duffy and Wallin is the response of journalists -- both reporters and columnists -- and their vigour in investigating and discussing the stories . <p> These are important stories and journalists would be working hard on them in any case -- but one particular angle makes them special : the senators in question are former journalists . In my view , this particular angle strikes at the heart of how many journalists view themselves and their work . Many journalists regard their work as more than a job . They see it rather as a ' calling ' or vocation . <p> What is a vocation and how is it different from an ordinary job ? The Oxford English Dictionary ( OED ) defines a profession as " ... an occupation in which a professed knowledge of some subject , field , or science is applied . " In contrast , a vocation comes from ( again according to OED ) " ... being so called or directed towards a special work in life . " In essence , a vocation is a ' calling ' aimed at accomplishing something bigger than simply putting bread on the table . <p> Executives will tell you that regulation can be a real irritant . Regulations add to the time and cost of doing business . Sometimes it 's hard to see what benefit they bring to society . Governments are trying to reduce the regulatory burden , but are their actions making a positive difference ? <p> It 's worth stepping back for a moment and thinking about a world without regulation . No more food safety regulation of food processing or restaurants . No more transportation regulation of aircraft or automobiles . No more regulation of toxic substances like mercury or arsenic . No more environmental protection . A world without regulation would be pretty risky . Regulations can actually help firms if they give consumers confidence that products are safe or produced in a sustainable way . <p> Thus , the real question for business is not whether we should have regulations , but rather how to design them to ensure they have real benefits for society and are as efficient as possible . <p> Regulations are laws made by cabinet , or individual ministers or certain administrative bodies , as a result of powers delegated by Parliament . Because regulations are not debated in Parliament , the rules for making them require that they be clearly in the public interest and based on broad , transparent consultation with stakeholders . <p> An interesting experiment in underway in B.C. It has n't yet captured the attention of the national media , but that 's not surprising . It does n't have the snap or sizzle of a big political scandal or a business mega-deal . <p> However , if successful , it may be more important to the economic and social development of the province than any corporate merger . B.C. business is trying to chart a middle path for the province in partnership with a wide array of partners , some of whom have been its traditional opponents . Recently , led by the Business Council of B.C. and the B.C. Chamber of Commerce , the partners met in Vancouver at an event entitled : " The B.C. Agenda for Shared Prosperity " . <p> What 's driving the initiative ? As one participant put it , along with its many natural advantages , B.C. carries the burden of " an entrenched and polarized political culture . " Business , unions , civil society and First Nations in the province have a history of conflict over economic , environmental and social issues . The residue of those conflicts has become a barrier to reaching a broader consensus on a way forward for B.C. <p> It is probably a good thing that there are n't more parliamentary committees on TV . These days , committees rarely show taxpayers that the money they send to Ottawa is well spent . <p> It does n't have to be this way . Indeed , with all the current talk of improving decorum and revitalizing Parliament , the best and easiest place to start is with committees . Here are a few non-partisan ideas I offer to committee members that have occurred to me while appearing before parliamentary committees . <p> 1 . Leave the partisan fury to question period . Luckily for backbench MPs , QP is mostly about ministers and opposition critics sniping at each other ; in committee , MPs can adopt a pacific demeanor . All members should use committees to contribute to the serious work of Parliament : considering the best laws and policies for Canada and holding the government to account . <p> Celebrating the holidays with family and friends , you may have missed the short recent blog by Ottawa Citizen reporter Glen McGregor . The essence of his piece was the argument that reporters need to shed lazy habits ( some of which are facilitated by the ease of Internet research ) and return to the traditional hard work of digging up news and talking to sources . <p> Hard to argue with that , you might think . At least one part of the blog , however , seemed to touch a nerve in academic quarters : <p> " No more quoting political scientists : It 's lazy and signals the reporter could n't find any other apparently neutral or objective source to talk . These people work in academics , not politics , so I 'm not interested in their opinions on anything but their own research . " <p> This touched off an online flurry of debate among academics that were offended by the advice Mr. McGregor offered to his journalist colleagues . Andrew Potter , managing editor at the Ottawa Citizen , wrote an interesting assessment of the debate . <p> For better or worse , Canadians are a pretty modest bunch . Outside of the Olympics , hockey playoffs and the Grey Cup , we are not much given to tooting our own horn . Usually , we prefer to take quiet satisfaction in a job well done .
