<h> Dirty air is the coronavirus ' accomplice : We must attack local sources of air pollution once and for all <p> By Latrice Walker <p> New York Daily News <p> Apr 16 , 2020 6:00 PM <p> The skyline of Manhattan is seen at sunset through a haze of air pollution . ( SAUL LOEB/AFP via Getty Images ) <p> Nobody can say the warning signs were n't there . Well before the COVID-19 crisis took over our lives , some of us sounded the alarm that local air pollution disproportionately hurts New York 's urban communities . Now , there is compelling evidence that increased exposure to air pollution dramatically increases the chance that COVID-19 will kill you , and communities of color -- which frequently have the worst air quality -- have been hit the hardest by the virus . <p> It took a global pandemic , but now it 's clear that we must focus public policy on reducing local sources of air pollution . Our failure to do so is n't just bad for the environment ; it exacerbates the conditions that could have helped create clear racial disparity in COVID-19 infection and death rates . <p> Advertisement <p> Last week , Harvard published a national study that showed even a small increase in exposure to fine particulate matter -- like the kind that is released into the air from power plants and diesel generators -- dramatically increases the death rate for people infected with COVID-19 . While the latest data questions the connection between asthma and COVID-19 fatalities in New York City , what is well documented is the damage that combustion-related air pollutants cause to human health -- especially for children . The Journal of the American Medical Association found that if you live in a city with high ozone levels for a decade , the results are similar to smoking a pack of cigarettes every day for 29 years . <p> Last year , Albany passed the most aggressive climate legislation in the nation , which I was proud to support . But that law regulates only greenhouse gasses that warm the planet , not the combustion-related pollutants that cause smog and asthma in our neighborhoods . Last year , I started calling for changes to the new climate law and its flagship Clean Energy Fund , neither of which include local forms of air pollution . The same is true of New York City 's Local Law 97 , which does not regulate local air pollution in any way , and even flatly exempts the use of diesel emergency generation from air quality regulation . <p> Climate change is real . Greenhouse gases must be reduced as fast as possible . Now COVID-19 is showing us how hazardous local air pollution is as well . Albany must review state programs and ask whether an exclusive focus on global climate impacts may have allowed local air pollution to receive less attention than it should have . <p> The same should be true for other state and city programs . We simply need a greater focus on reducing local forms of combustion-related air pollutants . To that end , I have also developed a bill that would support the development of microgrids powered by non-combustion sources , like fuel cells and solar , that do not produce local air pollution . Clean microgrids enhance resiliency , reduce emissions and ensure that critical facilities -- like hospitals or supermarkets -- do n't have to choose between losing power or running diesel generators in our neighborhoods . <p> In July , during the signing ceremony for the state 's climate bill , Al Gore said that " the number of African American children who die from asthma is 10 times greater than the number of Caucasian children that die from asthma . " He 's right -- death rate differences are that stark . In my assembly district , the rate of childhood asthma , and the exposure to fine particulate matter and ozone , are substantially higher than in the rest of the state . Now , we can see that the rate of COVID-19 infections and deaths is higher among Hispanic and black New Yorkers too . <p> We 've known for years that addressing climate change was a matter of life or death for the planet ; in the last month , we learned that addressing local air quality is a matter of life or death for our citizens .
