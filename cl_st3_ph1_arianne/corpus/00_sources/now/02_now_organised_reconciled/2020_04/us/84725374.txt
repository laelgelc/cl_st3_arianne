<h> Risk of severe storms and a perhaps a few tornadoes in the D.C. area Monday <p> Add to list <p> On my list <h> Powerful storm system blasting east portends a turbulent start to the work week . <p> Forecast radar at 10 a.m. on Monday from high-resolution NAM model . Note that some models are now suggesting the possibility of somewhat later timing of storms between the midday and late afternoon hours . <p> The developing powerful storm expected to trigger a dangerous outbreak of severe weather in the South on Sunday will barrel toward the East Coast during the day Monday . Strong to severe thunderstorms may develop in the Washington region between Monday morning and the late afternoon hours , with the potential for damaging winds and perhaps a few tornadoes . <p> The storms in the region are unlikely to be as violent and widespread as in parts of the South on Sunday but still could be dangerous . <p> The storm intensity and coverage will depend on how warm and humid the air becomes before the cold front triggering the storm activity moves through . <p> Before any strong to severe storms come through on Monday , a period of moderate to heavy showers and perhaps a bit of thunder are likely Sunday night into Monday morning , with half an inch to an inch of rain falling . <p> A period of strong winds also is likely as the cold front passes and in its wake , independent of the storms , gusts to at least 40 to 50 mph , similar to last Thursday . <h> At a glance <p> Storm timeline : 8 a.m. to 6 p.m . Monday . Some models favor storms passing in the morning , while some have delayed them until mid-to-late afternoon . There may even be two ( or more ) waves of storms , one in the morning and a second in the afternoon . <p> Areas affected : Southern and eastern areas will have the greatest chance of becoming warm enough to support severe weather , but everyone in the greater Washington region and Mid-Atlantic should be on guard . <p> The above details are subject to change as the storm potential comes into better focus . <p> The National Weather Service has placed the immediate Washington area in a marginal ( Level 1 out of 5 ) risk zone for severe thunderstorms , with a slight ( Level 2 out of 5 ) risk to the south and southeast . We would not be surprised to see these risk classifications bumped up some in future updates . <p> Risk of severe thunderstorms in the Washington region on Monday . ( National Weather Service ) <h> Discussion <p> The parent storm system approaching our area on Monday morning is potent , as you can see in the graphic below . The parent zone of low pressure can be seen spinning over the Great Lakes , with a central pressure that may approach 980 millibars -- which is comparable to some Category 1 hurricanes . The enormous wind circulation spans much of the eastern United States . <p> Weather forecast map on Monday morning . ( National Weather Service ) <p> Meanwhile , in the upper atmosphere , a truly gigantic trough ( southward dip ) in the jet stream sweeps eastward -- literally engulfing the entire United States . ( see below ) . The trough will usher in intense winds in the middle and upper levels of the atmosphere , from the southwest -- setting the stage for strong wind shear ( increase in wind speed with altitude ) overspreading the Mid-Atlantic . <p> Returning to the surface chart , notice a warm front ( red scalloped line ) draped across the Washington region and an approaching cold front ( blue triangles along a line ) approaching from the west . The area in between represents a northward surge of mild and humid air , called the " warm sector . " <p> On Friday , some of the models suggested the warm front might stall in the vicinity of the District , sequestering the truly mild air over central and southern Virginia . Saturday 's model forecasts unanimously push the warm front through the Washington area , into Pennsylvania , which should allow temperatures to warm into the mid-70s by early afternoon . <p> That means we may have a window of severe weather potential from the morning through late afternoon before the cold front pushes through later in the day . The later timing argues for greater warming of the surface air by the Sun , which should allow the atmosphere to destabilize . <p> Any middle and upper level clouds , however , may temper the degree of destabilization somewhat . Additionally , evaporation of moderately heavy rains from the overnight ( evaporation is a process that cools the air ) may slow down the initial degree of warming . <p> The next graphic the high resolution model 's assessment of how much instability will be present at 2 p.m . Monday . Shown here is a parameter called CAPE ( convective available potential energy ) which is a measure of total atmospheric buoyant energy . Values in those yellow pockets exceed 2000 J/kg , which is a significant value for early spring . <p> It 's worth pointing out that the plume of buoyant energy is narrow , which represents a narrow time window for storms to develop and organize . Not all models show CAPE values this large . <p> Suffice it to say -- while it looks like we will be in the warm sector for a sufficient length of time on Monday -- the degree of destabilization shows variability among the model guidance , and appears to be the greatest uncertainty governing the extent and severity of storms . <p> Guidance from the highest resolution models has come into the range of Monday morning . The graphic below shows the simulated radar from one of these models , at 10 a.m . Note the convective line sweeping through the DMV . Not shown is a second convective line that arrives during the midafternoon , having developed over the mountains and sweeping east . <p> The combination of strong wind shear and at least modest instability suggests a squall line of showers and thunderstorms will develop . That line may develop into wavelike or bow-shaped surges . Pockets of strong to damaging wind can accompany the bowed segments , and small spin-up tornadoes can develop in the " clefts " between bowing segments . <p> The deep layer wind shear is expected to be at least 50 knots on Monday , which is an exceptionally large value in the setting of a warm sector over the Mid-Atlantic . For this reason , it 's possible small supercells with more persistent rotation may become embedded within the storm line , increasing the risk of tornado activity . <p> Capital Weather Gang will again assess the setup and the model forecasts on Sunday . <p> Matthew Cappucci contributed to this report . <p> Jeff HalversonJeffrey Halverson , a contributor to The Washington Post 's Capital Weather Gang , teaches meteorology at the University of Maryland Baltimore County . <p> Jason SamenowJason Samenow is The Washington Post 's weather editor and Capital Weather Gang 's chief meteorologist .
