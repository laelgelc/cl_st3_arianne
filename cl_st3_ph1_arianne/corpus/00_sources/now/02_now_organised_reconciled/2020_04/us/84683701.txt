If you see comments in violation of our community guidelines , please report them . <h> 50 years after the first Earth Day , environmental activists confront another global crisis <p> As a young Navajo woman , Joe was raised to have respect for the land and wildlife . It was n't until her teenage years that she became actively involved in the environmental movement . By the age of 18 , she had already served as a United Nations Youth Ambassador , providing input on indigenous issues and the climate crisis . <p> To Joe and other young environmental activists , Earth Day should be every day . But Joe does n't attribute that feeling to her work in the environmental movement ; she attributes it to her indigenous roots . <p> " Earth Day to me was always like one of those things where at school we 'd have an activity , but it never really held a significant place to me until recently , " said Joe , now an Arizona State University freshman . " It 's one of the pivotal points for the mainstream movement . But it should n't be just this one day . " <p> Then the COVID-19 pandemic hit Arizona . Joe was visiting her grandfather on the Navajo Nation , near Window Rock . Before the thought pieces came out in glossy national magazines , her grandfather , speaking in Navajo , made the connection between the virus and society 's mistreatment of the environment . <p> " He said that when we do n't appreciate Mother Earth or what Changing Woman has given us , things like disease and war come up , " Joe said . " He said the earth is trying to breathe and we need to let Earth breathe . " <p> Activists around the world had hoped to draw attention to the climate crisis this week on Earth Day 's 50th anniversary , but instead they 've been forced to react to another crisis , the COVID-19 pandemic . They see it as deeply interwoven with the issues they 've been sounding the alarm on for years . <h> A catalyst for a modern movement <p> On April 22 , 1970 , about 20 million Americans participated in rallies and festivities across the country for the world 's first official " Earth Day . " With racial inequality and the Vietnam War dividing Americans , the day was envisioned by organizers as a unifying event . <p> Earth Day generated a different sort of activism than the kind Americans had experienced in the previous decade , which was dominated by the turmoil of confronting racism while carrying out a seemingly never-ending war that many opposed . Protests and demonstrations were often met with force . <p> But almost no one opposed Earth Day . It was a day for learning and expressing concern , not for protesting . It was a time to get together and talk about why rivers were catching fire , smog was muddying city skylines , how oil spills were killing millions of animals , and industries were belching toxins indiscriminately into the air and water . <p> The day was the brainchild of Gaylord Nelson , a democratic Senator from Wisconsin who , frustrated by the lack of attention to environmental issues in politics and the media , wanted to organize " teach-ins " on college campuses to draw attention to the issues . He asked Pete McCloskey , a conservation-minded Republican congressman from California , to co-chair the event . They recruited Denis Hayes , a young activist , to organize the campus teach-ins . <p> The enormous turnout -- one tenth the population of the United States at the time -- was followed by unprecedented action from the federal government . Congress passed the Clean Air Act , the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act , and President Richard Nixon , a Republican , established the Environmental Protection Agency . The day is viewed as the catalyst for the modern environmental movement . <p> News outlets covered festivities across the country and for the first time in history , the environment was center stage in mainstream media . The Arizona Republic ran an Associated Press article about the day on its front page . The Today Show , hosted by now-retired Arizona resident Hugh Downs , dedicated its program to Earth Day for the whole week . <p> " Do we have the will to turn our way of life upside down ? To make personal , corporate , and national sacrifices in order to keep this earth alive ? Or do we go on breeding , demanding more and more power , more of everything until we suffocate or die of plague or famine ? " Downs asked his viewers that first Earth Day " Our oceans are dying , our air is poisoned . This is not science fiction and it is not the future ; it is happening now and we have to make a decision now . " <p> Fifty years later , on the anniversary of the first Earth Day , that kind of public mobilization is impossible . The whole world is being asked to stay at home because of the COVID-19 pandemic . Yet , the questions Downs asked five decades ago , are , to activists today , more relevant -- and urgent -- than ever . <p> Sixth-graders Alberto Fernandez , left , Eddie Lopez and Martin Aguero work on digging a hole for one of 100 trees planted across the street from Santa Fe Elementary School in Peoria on Earth Day 2010 . ( Photo : Charlie Leight/The Republic ) <h> Activism in the age of coronavirus <p> As many people have spent more time in nature the past couple months , seeking comfort amidst the anxiety of the COVID-19 pandemic , environmentalists say they hope the 50th anniversary of Earth Day is a reminder that more people should take part in nature 's protection . <p> Environmental groups across Arizona and the world have shifted their Earth Day activities online . Some see it as a way to engage more people , while others worry that the message of Earth Day will be swallowed up by COVID-19 . <p> Sierra Club chapters in Arizona had scheduled 50 service projects around the state for the anniversary , but had to indefinitely postpone most of them . Youth activists have converted their town halls and speaking events to webinars . <p> To many young activists , the convergence of COVID-19 and Earth Day is only another powerful sign of the times . <p> " For the 50th anniversary , we were going to make it bigger and better than the first Earth Day , " said Claire Nelson , the co-lead for AZ Youth Climate Coalition and a senior at Arizona State University Prep Digital . <p> " Then COVID came in , we kind of had to shut that all down , because we care about the safety of people first . That 's the reason why we 're fighting for the environment , right ? " Nelson said . " The coronavirus is kind of foreshadowing the complete disaster that climate change is going to bring upon our communities and our country . It 's showing what will happen and that our government is not prepared to deal with crises on this scale . " <p> To Nelson , Earth Day is also a reminder of the impact of regulations that many now take for granted , like the Clean Air and Clean Water acts . She and other youth activists are urging politicians to enact policies so that Arizona achieves 100% clean energy by 2030 . <p> " After the original Earth Day , we saw regulations put in place that helped a lot , " Nelson said . " I think that shows that massive protests and civil disobedience can work . They got this done , and it still impacts us today . I 'd like to learn from that and continue that legacy . " <p> At a state level , the Arizona Legislature does n't allow its own Department of Environmental Quality to monitor greenhouse gas emissions and it has passed laws that prevent cities from taking local action on climate issues . <p> " Regulations save lives , " said Sandy Bahr , Sierra Club 's Grand Canyon Chapter director . " We 've been going through a few decades of a deregulatory environment . The laws were adopted a long time ago but what they 're doing is present in our lives today . If you say air is cleaner , you can really thank the Clean Air Act . Without it , we would have never seen things like catalytic converters on cars . The automobile industry thought this regulation would destroy it , but of course it did n't . " <h> Responding to COVID and climate <p> Many activists and academics are seeing the connections between the response to COVID-19 and the climate crisis . It is rare for the entire world to feel the impact of a crisis almost simultaneously , though at differing scales . Those in the environmental movement see the pandemic as a moment when almost everyone is feeling a visceral anxiety , and a responsibility to do their part to prevent a worldwide disaster . <p> " Both the pandemic and the environmental crisis are global and they do n't respect borders , " said Mark Roseland , a professor at the School of Community Resources and Development at Arizona State University , and senior sustainability scientist with the Julie Ann Wrigley Global Institute of Sustainability at ASU . <p> " Second , both of them require enormous changes in human behavior and economic activity and third , is that the sooner we begin to adapt , the less they hurt , " Roseland said . " Those are profound similarities , but the differences are , I think , even more important . " <p> Roseland described how the pandemic is killing people all over the world at the same time , while the climate crisis is killing people now and impacting communities in a vastly disproportionate way . The biggest difference , perhaps , is " people can somewhat legitimately say that they were sort of caught by surprise with the pandemic , " Roseland said . <p> " Nobody 's going to be able say that about climate , " he said . " Anybody who says that we did n't see this coming , that we did n't have lots of warning , that we did n't have decades of people sounding the alarm , is just lying through their teeth . " <p> The COVID-19 crisis has also laid bare how crises play out across racial and socioeconomic planes unequally . <p> " COVID-19 has reassured me of the systemic injustices that marginalized communities live with every single day , " said Jessica Bristow , co-leader of the Phoenix Sunrise Movement chapter . " One of the core realities of the climate crisis is environmental injustice . Black , brown , Indigenous and poor communities are disproportionality affected by the crisis . " <p> Nationwide , the black community is feeling the health impacts of COVID-19 worse than any other race . In Arizona , indigenous people have been impacted by the pandemic more intensely than other communities . <p> Still , differences between then and now remain stark . Though youth are leading the climate movement , as they did on the first Earth Day , they do n't only face an uncertain future ; they face not having a future at all . <p> " When I was my oldest son 's age , we were concerned about the earth , but we were also concerned about being drafted , " said Robin Silver , a founder of the Tucson-based Center for Biological Diversity . " My 18-year-old now , he 's concerned about whether or not the earth is going to survive . With the first Earth Day , we could be realistically very optimistic . My son can now be cautiously optimistic at best . " <h> Finding a new way to live <p> Kianna Joe , an Arizona State University freshman and Navajo climate activist . ( Photo : Kianna Joe ) <p> As a child , Kianna Joe split her time between Albuquerque , Mesa , Phoenix and the Navajo Nation , where much of her family lived . Her father was a medicine man , and as a child she 'd travel with him all over the reservation as he assisted community members . Getting out of the city was always a relief . She has fond memories of running around with her cousins , barefoot in the dirt . <p> " I always had this feeling of going back home and being able to breathe , " said Joe . " I can survive out here in the city , I 've adapted , but every time I 'm able to go back to my reservation and see my family and just be out there in the middle of nowhere where there 's no cell service , I can actually take a deep breath . And for that time of being back home , I feel good . " <p> While many are experiencing -- and valuing -- a moment to take a step back and slow down during the pandemic , Joe is experiencing the opposite : She ca n't go back to the place where she can breathe the easiest . For the safety of her community , she and her family are in Phoenix while the rest of her family waits out the crisis on the reservation . <p> " It 's very heartbreaking right now for a lot of indigenous students because we ca n't go back home and we ca n't take our deep breath that we need , " Joe said . <p> " For as long as I can remember , I 've always held a piece of the land in my heart . We can see the difference between how indigenous students had been raised by the land and how non-indigenous students do n't have that connection , " she said . " That 's why I feel that it 's harder for non-indigenous people to understand that climate change and justice is crucial for everyone to continue living . " <p> Much of the mainstream environmental movement has revolved around convincing politicians and the broader public that nature and wildlife are worth saving . But for Joe and other indigenous people , the value of nature is intrinsic and deeply woven into their world view and cultures . <p> " It 's about time that non-natives really caught on to this , " said Carol Davis , Executive Director of Din ? CARE , a nonprofit that has addressed energy and environmental issues in Navajo communities since 1988 . " We 've always known that we had a responsibility to protect our Mother Earth and our Father Sky . It 's how we were raised . " <p> Experts say drastic action is needed to address the climate crisis in time . According to the United Nations , countries would have to cut their anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions , such as from power plants and vehicles , to net zero by around 2050 to keep global temperatures from rising more than 1.5 degrees Celsius , a goal of the Paris climate agreement . <p> That makes this decade crucial for action , said Greg Asner , director of ASU 's Center for Global Discovery and Conservation Science . <p> " We 're being called upon to accelerate our efforts to generate agreements , to use science and technology to underpin processes that have been way too slow in the political arena , " Asner said . " How do we craft a pathway for ourselves , as a society , to live with the earth 's system as a partner , rather than us trying to beat it back ? " <p> An original goal of Earth Day was to institutionalize the environmental movement , to give strength and resources to the thousands of organizations around the country that were organizing on local levels to protect the environment , said Paul Hirt , a professor at ASU 's School of Historical , Philosophical and Religious Studies , and a historian who specializes in the American West and environmental history and policy . <p> " Local efforts to clean the air and water , to protect wildlife and endangered species , were all pretty scattered and disorganized efforts , " Hirt said . " So a goal of Earth Day was to come to an understanding of a sort of national obligation to protect the environment and strengthen those organizations , make them statewide , make them regional , make them nationwide . " <p> All three of those things happened . The environmental movement became an influential , organized movement . Membership in the Sierra Club and similar organizations skyrocketed in the next decade . Media outlets started hiring reporters for the " environment beat , " which had never existed before . <p> But as the climate crisis has become the central point of the movement today , the fundamental goals of the environmental movement -- changing the way people live and interact with the earth -- have remained the same . <p> " For the 100th Earth Day , I hope we wo n't be dealing with devastating effects of the climate crisis , " said Nelson , the Phoenix youth climate activist . " Fifty years from now , I 'd really like to be celebrating , instead of protesting . Hopefully we 'll be able to say , ' we did it , we saved the planet . We did the best that we could do . ' Because right now we 're not doing the best that we can do . " <p> April 22 : High Schoolers for Climate Justice is doing a digital campaign making shrines to the future .
