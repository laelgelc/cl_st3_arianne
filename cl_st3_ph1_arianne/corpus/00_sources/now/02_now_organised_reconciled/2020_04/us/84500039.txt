But with the floods that are predicted to come this spring -- bringing the need for manpower and disaster relief -- there is growing concern that much of those resources will have been diverted to fighting the COVID-19 pandemic . <p> The average annual rainfall in Indiana has increased by almost 6 inches since 1895 , the Purdue University 's Indiana Climate Change Impacts Assessment reports , and it 's falling more often in heavier downpours . These conditions make floods -- including life-threatening flash floods -- more common in Midwestern springs . <p> The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration predicts major to moderate flooding will hit much of the Midwest this year . The good news is that it 's not anticipated to be as severe as last year 's historic flooding that cost billions in damage and soaked millions of acres of farmland . Still , the effects could be devastating for some , especially as resources are redirected toward an entirely different disaster . <p> The agency is already stretched thin battling the rapidly spreading coronavirus while coping with left over issues from years of consecutive catastrophes . Coupled with the fact that the pandemic is one of the most widespread disasters the agency has ever dealt with , those who study disaster response are worried what this means for the ever-growing number of flood victims . <p> " I do think that the federal government 's ability to respond is going to be taxed by this disease , " said Steven Cohen , a public affairs professor at Columbia University . " It 's already being taxed , and if you add to the normal catastrophes that are already taking place ... the federal government is having difficulty . " <p> In a statement emailed to IndyStar , FEMA noted resources are also tight at state and local levels , as every branch of government chips in to fight the pandemic . <p> " Yes , looking and planning for future response efforts is going to be a challenge , " the statement said . " We are working with our states and other partners now on some of these issues and how it may change sheltering or other more traditional resources provided during emergency response . " <h> ' There 's only so many resources ' <p> Inland states such as Indiana tend to be particularly susceptible to flash floods , the most dangerous because of their speed and unpredictability , said Jennifer Scherff , a project manager at My Flood Risk , a web platform that informs property owners about their home 's flood susceptibility . <p> As precipitation continues to increase in the future , the Purdue climate change report warns , flood risks will grow as well . The issue of how to address increased flooding has been mounting as recent years of heavy rains exposed a grim number of inadequacies in existing flood prevention infrastructure across the Midwest . <p> Last year 's heavy flooding exposed severe faults in the region 's levees , causing numerous breaches in several states . Recent studies have also found FEMA 's maps of flood hazard areas , which act as guidelines for building development , could be badly out-of-date , meaning many more people could be living in dangerous zones than previously thought . <p> Floods tend to literally and figuratively exploit the cracks in a system , said Jeff Schlegelmilch , the deputy director for the National Center for Disaster Preparation at Columbia University . <p> Layer one disaster such as the coronavirus on top of another , Schlegelmilch said , and they becomes that much harder to fix . <p> " Disasters exploit the underestimating of what could happen , " Schlegelmilch said . " Unfortunately , you ca n't really make up for decades of underinvestment in one moment of panic . " <p> When disaster does strike , cities and counties reach out to the Indiana Department of Homeland Security when they have exhausted all their resources to respond to flooding events , said David Hosick , IDHS director of public affairs . " <h> Will volunteers stay home as the virus spreads ? <p> Another major point of concern is whether the volunteer force many communities rely on in times of need will be willing to leave their homes as the coronavirus spreads . <p> More than 90% of the work done by the American Red Cross , which provides shelter , supplies and health services for flood victims , is carried out through volunteers , said Duchess Adjei , communications director for the organization 's Indiana region . <p> This mirrors many communities across the Midwest , said Olivia Dorothy , director of the Upper Mississippi River Basin for the organization American Rivers . There are very few paid workers that build flood defenses in her region , she said . <p> " Not only are we going to be lacking in resources ... but we are also not going to have to have the same pool of volunteers we 've had in the past to deploy the flood defense systems , " Dorothy said .
