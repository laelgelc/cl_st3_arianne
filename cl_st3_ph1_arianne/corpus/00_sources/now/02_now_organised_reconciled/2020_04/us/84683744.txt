If you see comments in violation of our community guidelines , please report them . <h> The same spirit that now forces us to work together to address the COVID-19 pandemic can be harnessed to address the challenge of climate change . <p> It 's been 50 years since the first Earth Day . Somehow , even after 50 years of continuing environmental degradation of our planet , the lofty ideals of the environmental movement got mired in the political realities of an increasingly divided nation . <p> Environmentalists can not convince opponents of even the most basic facts of climate science . Many in our country simply choose to ignore scientific knowledge or to look away from the growing evidence that the house of creation is -- sometimes literally -- on fire . Opponents ca n't even find a common language to discuss the issues . <p> We 've got a solution to propose . On this unusual Earth Day anniversary , let 's find new ways to celebrate our common humanity and work together to solve the world 's most wicked challenges . Perhaps COVID-19 has something to teach us : we can not afford to stand idly by as the signs of impending calamity move toward our shores . We share this fragile planet , and we simply have to find ways to work together . <p> The same spirit that now forces us to work together to address the COVID-19 pandemic can also be harnessed to address the long-term challenge of climate change and earth stewardship . We believe that two of the great human forces that have shaped our world -- science and religion -- can be brought together to address the next emerging challenge . And we know it can be done -- because we 've started to make it happen in our community . <p> Buy Photo <p> Earth Day Indiana Festival at Military Park on Saturday , April 20 , 2019 . ( Photo : Matt Kryger/IndyStar ) <p> Last fall , the two of us -- a scientist and a pastor -- got together to discuss these concerns . We started with few assumptions , developed a level of trust over many cups of coffee and free-ranging discussion , and devised a program to bring our communities together . We recognized that the issue of environmental stewardship offers an ideal opportunity to help resolve the perceived -- and we agreed , unnecessary -- rift between science and religion . We identified two fundamental points of agreement : first , that joint commitment by our nation 's scientific and faith communities will be critical to solving the great global humanitarian challenges ; and second , that there is far more common ground on these issues than many might perceive . <p> There is great power in combining these two great forces of humanity . Science provides reliable and objective observation of Earth 's systems , carries predictive power to assess the impacts of human activity , and can provide powerful , forward-looking solutions . Faith provides a moral compass to guide and energize our response to global challenges . It offers a powerful force that works on both global and local scales , and carries enormous potential for communication and collaboration . <p> Over a remarkable three weeks last fall , we brought our two communities together -- a science advocacy group and several local Christian congregations to discuss the scientific , ethical , and religious dimensions of climate change and environmental stewardship . Climatologists and ornithologists sat with religious leaders and congregants , culminating in a field workshop and celebration conducted on a beautiful fall day at McCormick 's Creek State Park . The project helped lay the groundwork for ongoing collaboration and science-faith dialogue in our community . <p> Buy Photo <p> Oscar right , and Archie Wallace , left , look for creek critters during a Earth Day Family Creek Stomp at Holliday Park in Indianapolis on Sunday , April 22 , 2018 . ( Photo : Michelle Pemberton/IndyStar ) <p> Did we solve the problems of global climate change and environmental degradation ? Not yet , but our success in breaking down barriers was felt by all our participants . We think succeeding together comes down to three basic tenets : openness , respect , and willingness to listen . We can indeed find common cause and reasons to work together . <p> Over the past month , as fears of the novel coronavirus have set us apart from one another , we have been inspired by the courageous acts of brotherhood and generosity in communities across the country . Scientists and public health officials are collaborating with religious congregations to help limit the risk of further infection , while finding creative ways to feed the hungry , house the homeless , and take care of the elderly . Might this same spirit of collaboration be brought together to save our planet ? We think so . There are only three things we need : openness , respect , and a willingness to listen . <p> Michael Hamburger is professor of Earth and Atmospheric Sciences at Indiana University and one of the leaders of Concerned Scientists at IU , a science advocacy organization . Robert Whitaker is lead pastor at the Evangelical Community Church in Bloomington.
