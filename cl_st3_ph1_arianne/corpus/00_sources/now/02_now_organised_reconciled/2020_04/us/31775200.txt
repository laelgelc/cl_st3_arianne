Photo courtesy of Joe Bickard <p> WINDHAM -- The first scientific study of Highland Lake to use environmental DNA testing confirms an earlier hypothesis that the 2014-17 algae blooms in the lake were caused by a peculiar and rare type of phytoplankton called pico-cyanobacteria . <p> The research that began in the spring of 2018 also leads researchers to suspect the sudden drop in the algae blooms ' intensity over the last two years is due to the reintroduction of alewives into the lake . <p> The blooms that have plagued the 623-acre lake in Windham and Falmouth were unusual , according to Karen Wilson , an associate research professor at the University of Southern Maine in Gorham and a member of the Highland Lake Association 's research team . <p> " It was n't your typical algae bloom . It did n't smell really strongly , you just could n't see well ( into the water ) and people noticed , Wilson said . <p> A casual observer would notice the poor water clarity or the appearance of algae blooms : slimy , noxious and often toxic green scum that appears on the lake 's surface , typically in the summer . But the condition of the lake greatly concerned the lake association , a non-profit volunteer group . <p> Researchers hope this first scientific study using eDNA testing will help them understand what causes this type of algae bloom and how to prevent it . With eDNA , or environmental DNA , researchers do not have to find an organism itself to test . That 's because eDNA comes from cellular material that is " shed " from an organism into another environment , like a body of water . <p> Wilson and biologist Jeff Dennis of the Maine Department of Environmental Protection found that the lake was experiencing an algae bloom of pico-cyanobacteria . Algae blooms of cyanobacteria , also called blue-green algae , are common in lakes around the world , Wilson said . <p> But this was a bloom of pico-cyanobacteria , which is the smallest type of cyanobacteria -- often so small , it requires eDNA testing to identify it . Wilson and Dennis said that they are not aware of any other lake in North America with a recorded instance of this type of bloom . And the bloom was happening at almost the same time every year . <p> " They just are n't punctual like that , " said Dennis . <p> Researchers began an intensive study of the lake in May 2018 , collecting water samples and other data . They began with a set of hypotheses and went off to " science the heck out of it , " in Wilson 's words . <p> Then , the bloom did n't occur to the same degree . <p> " One of the frustrations of ( studying ) ecology is that there is a lot of variability from year to year , " said Wilson . <p> The bloom still occurred , but was not as intense as the four previous years and would only last a few days , rather than a few weeks , before it suddenly cleared up . <p> " It just did n't have a chance to develop and something interrupted it , " said Dennis . <p> Wilson was curious to test her hypothesis that alewives were essentially shifting the balance of the lake 's food web . Alewives eat zooplankton , which eat algae ( which , in this case , is the pico-cyanobacteria ) . <p> Alewives were non-existent in Highland Lake for over 200 years , noted Wilson , until the early 2000s , when the Department of Marine Resources reintroduced farm-raised alewives into the lake . The alewives did not become abundant until 2012 . The changes in the pico-cyanobacteria blooms from year to year may be a clue as to how the complex lake ecosystem is adjusting to this reintroduction . <p> If the researchers can figure out what is behind the previous years ' blooms -- and why it all of a sudden stopped -- then they might be able to determine the conditions that cause these blooms in other lakes as well . <p> While the research remains ongoing , the scientists leading the study and the association 's vice president , Rosie Hartzler , all agree that one thing they can do right now is work to prevent more pollution from entering the lake . <p> For Highland Lake , which has one of , if not the most developed watersheds in the state of Maine , according to the Highland Lake Association , stormwater runoff and soil erosion are major concerns . That pollution adds too much phosphorus to the water , which allows an excess of algae to grow . <p> " In general , Highland Lake will have a health system and better water quality ( if ) phosphorus is less , " said Dennis . " It 'll have worse conditions if ( there is ) more phosphorus . " <p> He warns that continued development of the watershed and climate change will likely make the problem worse . <p> " ( We 're ) dealing with a very sensitive lake to begin with , " he said . <p> " We know we can do something about ( phosphorus levels ) so that 's how we 're focusing our education efforts , " Hartzler said . But otherwise , " 2020 is a big question . "
