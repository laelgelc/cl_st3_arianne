<h> They Wore Surgical Masks for the First Earth Day , Too <p> Fifty years after the first Earth Day , we need , once more , to be in the streets . We ca n't , of course , this month , so many people will be spending the next three days , instead , at Earth Day Live.Photograph by Santi Visalli / Getty <p> Subscribers to The Climate Crisis newsletter received this piece in their in-boxes . Sign up to receive future installments . <p> Happy Earth Day ! Welcome to this extended edition of The Climate Crisis newsletter ! I hope you choose the green surgical mask when it 's time to walk the dog ! <p> Actually , looking back over photos of the first Earth Day , fifty years ago , I was struck by how many participants were , in fact , wearing surgical masks . And not just for the symbolism : it 's startling to recall just how dirty America was in 1970 . Rust Belt cities ( which had n't yet fully rusted ) suffered air-pollution levels comparable to Beijing 's in recent years ; Los Angeles and smog were synonymous . The air in Mobile , Alabama , was so bad that it was costing people almost four years of their lives . <p> So it was n't hard to get people marching : estimates of participation in that first glorious day run at twenty million , or ten per cent of the American population at the time . That may make it the biggest day of political action in history . All that marching worked : the shift in the Zeitgeist forced Richard Nixon to sign one law after another , from the Clean Water Act to the Endangered Species Act . And those laws worked : air pollution has dropped by more than sixty per cent across America since 1970 , and life expectancy has grown by 1.5 years on average as a result . Thanks to those people who took to the streets , you get to live an extra eighteen months . <p> Now those gains are under threat . Even as we 've learned more about the threat of particulate air pollution , the Trump Administration last week blocked rules that would have tightened standards on particulates . ( Air pollution reportedly kills more than fifty thousand Americans a year -- more people than the country has lost so far to the coronavirus . ) Lobbyists turned bureaucrats are rushing to gut whatever environmental regulations they can , and not just in the U.S. -- also last week , a memo surfaced in Ottawa showing the Canadian Association of Petroleum Producers making the same kind of demands . <p> But the bigger problem is that , after 1970 , we chose to do the easy and obvious stuff , and we did n't transform our systems -- or our outlooks -- in fundamental ways . Instead of pollution that you can see ( and smell ) , in this century we 're increasingly dealing with what the President might call an " invisible enemy " : the carbon-dioxide molecule , with its devilish determination to trap heat that would otherwise radiate back out to space . The damage it does is far greater than , say , carbon with one oxygen atom . Carbon monoxide was a component of that L.A. smog , but you could control it with a catalytic converter ; indeed , a catalytic converter turns some of that carbon monoxide into carbon dioxide . This time , there 's no easy technical fix , just turning off the fossil fuels and replacing them with renewable energy . But that means turning off the giant companies that dominate the hydrocarbon economy . That 's not an argument about science -- that 's a fight about power . <p> And so we need , once more , to be in the streets . We ca n't , of course , this month . So many people will be spending the next three days , instead , at Earth Day Live , doing their best to grow this movement online . <h> Passing the Mic <p> I first wrote about Nicole Poindexter , the C.E.O . of Energicity , for The New Yorker in 2017 , in an article about renewable energy in Africa . I visited her in central Ghana , where she and her team were working to bring solar power to villages . She comes from a standard finance background , and attended Harvard Business School , but she 's found an extraordinary niche to fill in West Africa . Energicity , a startup she co-founded in 2015 , is a utility company that uses small solar arrays to build out electric networks for villages . She 's currently based in Sierra Leone . This interview has been condensed for clarity . <p> What 's your most compelling project at the moment , and how 's it going ? <p> We are currently ramping up in Sierra Leone to provide a hundred thousand people in rural villages with solar-powered electricity through our mini-grids . At this point , we 've deployed to serve ten thousand people and twenty-one health-care clinics that can take care of patients through the COVID-19 pandemic . <p> What do Americans most misunderstand about energy development in the developing world ? <p> There are just so many things , but I 'd say the biggest is that people doubt that solar power , and solar-powered mini-grids , are a meaningful part of the solution to the energy challenges in Africa . First , people do n't understand the magnitude and the urgency of the problem . Over six hundred million people do not have electricity here . Without electricity , learning is hard , health care harder , and participating in the modern economy -- from agriculture to Bitcoin -- impossible . Each day , African people lose their dreams and their lives because they do n't have access to something that is so ubiquitous in the U.S. that we barely think about it . <p> This is a problem we need to address now , and people ranging from Bill Gates to African governments will say that we need to rely on big fossil-fuel-powered plants to provide electricity , and big transmission-line projects to deliver power to people . The problem is that those projects often take a minimum of ten years to deploy , they are polluting and contribute to climate change , and they are expensive . <p> The important advantage is that we 're clean , and reduce environmental impacts , and we can provide electricity now . While most of the companies in our industry are small , serving collectively hundreds of thousands , with proper funding , we can move fast . And , as we mature , we can easily be providing power to tens of millions of people . <p> How do entrepreneurs compare with governments in terms of doing this work , and is there a need for new investors to keep it growing ? <p> A lot of people and , particularly , a lot of governments in Africa really think that the government should have the leading role in providing electricity . The reality is , if you look at the path that we took in the United States , the majority of our electricity infrastructure was developed by private companies . Private entrepreneurs ( Edison , Westinghouse , etc. ) brought innovation , consistency of delivery , and private capital to build one of the most transformative pieces of infrastructure ever . But I am a firm believer in government regulation of electricity to make sure the prices are fair , services are quality and safe , and pollution is minimized . With a problem of this scale -- that , honestly , the world still does n't know how to solve -- entrepreneurs bring the necessary innovation , resources , and urgency to solve it . <p> And , absolutely , we need more investors ! I 'm just finishing a round of fund-raising now . To say the least , it 's been a slog . To put some color on it , a friend of mine who is a leader in the clean-energy space introduced me to several leading impact investors ( hundreds of millions under management ) and across the board they said , " We do n't know enough to invest in Africa . " With six hundred million people who need electricity , and a planet that needs us to do it with innovative clean-energy technologies , the world needs many more bold , transformative investors . <p> The Wall Street Journal carried a particularly fine piece about the chaos in the oil industry , as prices drop to unheard-of lows , amid plummeting demand . " The global oil industry is experiencing a shock like no other in its history , " Fatih Birol , the executive director of the International Energy Agency , says . <p> One response to that shock comes from Oil Change International and the Democracy Collaborative , who released a report outlining in readable detail the prospects for a public takeover of the fossil-fuel industry . Since we 're handing firms bailouts , the logic goes , why not take ownership , and begin the process of winding down the industry before it can do more damage ? The report lists the possible mechanisms , and concludes that " public ownership of the fossil fuel industry can halt its volatile boom-bust cycles , provide short- and long-term security for workers and communities , and implement a managed phase-out of oil , gas , and coal extraction to keep global warming under 1.5 ? C. " <p> Rhiana Gunn-Wright , the climate-policy director at the Roosevelt Institute , takes on the larger question of economic relief in an important Op-Ed for the Times , arguing that , to respond to an economic depression , you need a New Deal , and that , in this case , the new deal should be a green one : " If history is any indication , rebounding from an economic disruption this large requires an equally large spike in demand and production . Outside of war , climate change is the only issue large enough to provide such a spike . " <p> All kinds of fascinating moments lie ahead as the bailout proceeds . The Fed has tapped the giant asset manager BlackRock to help lead the process -- but just this winter BlackRock promised to make the climate crisis a key part of its decision-making going forward . In order to forestall any such good sense , seventeen Republican senators sent the Treasury a letter on April 7th demanding that aid be " administered equitably " to the entire economy . <h> Scoreboard <p> Following up on an earlier dispatch about the legacy of uncapped wells that oil drillers are leaving behind , Justin Trudeau 's government announced last week that it will spend 1.7 billion dollars to put people to work cleaning up some of that mess . It makes sense -- giving oil-field workers a good wage to do something helpful -- but it also may set a precedent that government should have to pay for this work , instead of the companies that profited from the wells in the first place . " If these funds are not repaid by industry once the cleanup work is done , today 's announcement will become another public subsidy to oil and gas , " the Alberta Liabilities Disclosure Project pointed out . <p> Meanwhile , the engineers keep doing their job . Even while we 're locked down , teams of researchers in the United States and Germany announced that they had smashed three key efficiency records for solar panels . <p> One hope has always been that we 'll get a bit of a break from increased CO2 because it will stimulate plant growth , thus soaking up some of the carbon . But researchers keep finding new reasons to doubt that happy story ; last week , a new study showed that , while trees in Australia did take up twelve per cent more carbon while growing in CO2-enriched air , they quickly gave that extra back , cycling it through the soil . That makes the math of climate change even harder . <p> Bad news from Greenland , which saw a record melt of its ice sheet last year . The mechanism was apparently a high-pressure system -- that meant clear skies and more sunlight . Such weather systems are apparently becoming more common in the region as the temperature warms . <p> And more bad news from the American West , where researchers report that climate change has helped lock in place a " megadrought " over the region -- one that rivals anything we can find in the geological record . <p> Sir John Houghton was claimed by the coronavirus , at the age of eighty-eight . An Oxford physicist , he helped chair the U.N . Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change , and accepted the Nobel Peace Prize , in 2007 , on behalf of the group , which shared the prize with Al Gore . A devout evangelical , he also played a key role in convincing at least some of his co-religionists to take global warming seriously . " There is widespread suspicion of science , fostered by the feeling that science goes against the Bible , " he once said . " This is very unfortunate ; it takes a very small view of God , and a very inadequate view of science .
