If you see comments in violation of our community guidelines , please report them . <h> Spring brings more fears for Missouri River flooding <p> Alex Brooks , wildland fire module leader with the Rocky Mountain Ranger District of Helena-Lewis and Clark National Forest , , skies above the North Fork of the Sun River near Gates Creek . Brooks , Sam Sulser , wildland fire module assistant , and Ian Bardwell , wilderness trails manager , traveled 35 miles into the North Fork of the Sun in March to gather snow survey information . ( Photo : Sam Sulser ) <p> A three-man team with Montana 's Helena-Lewis and Clark National Forest set off on horseback in March for a 35-mile , five-day journey into the wild North Fork of the Sun River , a tributary of the Missouri River . <p> They rode horses for the first 12 miles . When they reached a foot of snow , they switched to skis and took turns breaking trail . <p> Greeted by a half inch of new snow each morning , higher and higher they skied , encountering snow depths of 19 inches , then 2 feet , 9 inches and finally , 3 feet , 3 inches . <p> At each elevation , aluminum tubes with non-stick coating were stuck into the snow to collect core samples used to measure the depth and water content of the snowpack . <p> " The numbers are used for everything from dam control along the Missouri River to regulating the locks on the barges of the Mississippi , " said Ian Bardwell , the Rocky Mountain Ranger District 's wilderness and trails manager , who led the snow survey expedition . " It just depends on what level you are looking at it from . " <p> The numbers have rarely carried more weight than they do this year . Flooding last year along much of the Missouri River 's 2,300 miles battered the river 's levee system , flooding towns and farmland and causing multiple closures to the nation 's interstate highway system . <p> The forecast this year is n't much better : above-normal rainfall ; greater than normal spring runoff ; thoroughly saturated soils ; and an aging system of nearly a thousand levees where nobody knows how many were damaged last year and in previous floods or how many were repaired . <p> The 855 levee systems throughout the Missouri River basin protect at least half a million people and more than $92 billion in property . Yet a USA TODAY Network analysis of Army Corps of Engineers ' records found at least 144 levee systems have n't been fully repaired and that only 231 show an inspection date . <p> Of those , nearly half were rated " unacceptable , " which means something could prevent the levee from performing as intended or a serious deficiency was not corrected . Only 3.5% were deemed acceptable ; the rest were found to be " minimally acceptable . " <p> In the Army Corps ' Kansas City district , for example , about 70 projects , spanning 119 levees that requested repair assistance , are eligible for funding , but that does n't mean they 'll be ready if the waters rise like they did last year . <p> " Some of them have been repaired , but from a total system perspective , I do n't think any of them are whole , " said Jud Kneuvean , the district 's chief of emergency management , who expects full levee rehabilitation and repair to take at least another year . <p> In the meantime , the extent and impacts of flooding will depend on when and where the rain falls . <p> " A lot will depend on the weather patterns that set up over the region in the next few weeks , " University of Missouri Extension climatologist Pat Guinan said in a statement . " Unusually wet conditions have prevailed since the beginning of 2019 , with 10 to 14 months recording above average precipitation . " <h> ' Big Muddy ' gets bigger <p> The Missouri River begins in southwestern Montana , where the Gallatin , Madison and Jefferson rivers converge near the community of Three Forks , before gathering water from 10 states and parts of two Canadian provinces to become the " Big Muddy , " North America 's longest river . <p> In recent years , more rainfall has been pouring into the Missouri River basin , raising questions about whether climate change is bringing worsening floods more often . Data from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration dating back to 1895 shows record-setting rainfalls in the area occurring more often . Consider : <p> Last year was the wettest on record in North Dakota , South Dakota and Minnesota . <p> In Iowa , six of the 10 wettest years on record have come in the past 13 years . <p> In North Dakota , five of the 10 wettest years have fallen in the past 10 years . <p> In Montana in 2011 , nearly two-thirds of the state had 15 inches of rain in three days , about what the state normally receives in a year . <p> Nine of the 10 wettest years in history in South Dakota have come since 1977 , and 10 of the top 20 wettest years in Kansas have occurred over that same time span . <p> All that water adds to the challenge faced by Corps policymakers , who juggle sometimes conflicting priorities that include maintaining navigation ; managing the reservoir system to prevent flooding ; providing farmers with irrigation and hydropower ; protecting endangered species ; and preserving recreational opportunities . <p> While the priority is protecting human life and safety , the Corps ' decision-making sometimes puts special interest groups at odds , and the agency remains embroiled in controversy over whether the engineering of the river exacerbates flooding . <p> Things came to a head last year when a bomb cyclone in March melted all the snow in Nebraska and Iowa at once and dumped tremendous rain , swelling not just the Missouri , but the Elkhorn , Platte , James and Big Sioux rivers . <p> Two thirds of the city of Hamburg is underwater from flooding on the Missouri River on Monday , March 18 , 2019 , on the western edge of Iowa . ( Photo : Kelsey Kremer/The Register ) <p> The Niobrara River in Nebraska breached the Spencer Dam on March 14 , sending a wall of water downstream and into the Gavins Point reservoir near Yankton , South Dakota . At the peak , water flowed into the reservoir at 180,000 cubic feet per second -- nine times more than the normal average for March . Meanwhile water was coursing into the rivers downstream of the dam and the effects of all that water were felt in nearly every community downstream . <p> Two other big rain events occurred in May and September . When the Corps ' Kansas City district deactivated its emergency operations center in December , it had been open for 279 days , the longest period on record . <h> ' Dust on crust ' eases travel <p> Each month , Montanans who use water from the Missouri River and its tributaries to irrigate land , generate power and regulate reservoirs , eagerly await the results of the mountain snow surveys , where tributaries of the state 's rivers begin . More than 100 people , including those on Bardwell 's team , make treks into the boondocks to get it , even during a pandemic , for the U.S. Department of Agriculture 's Natural Resources Conservation Service . <p> " We had a lot of dedicated cooperators that went out and still collected the snow pack data for all the water users and folks in Montana , " said Lucas Zukiewicz , an NRCS water supply specialist in Montana . " That 's a huge asset we have is people who are willing to go out and do this for us . I think that 's pretty noteworthy . " <p> Three Forest Service employees in the Rocky Mountain Ranger District skied into the North Fork of the Sun River in March to survey snow conditions for the U.S. Department of Agriculture 's Natural Resources Conservation Service . ( Photo : Sam Sulser ) <p> Round trip , the team from the Helena-Lewis and Clark National Forest traveled 70 miles . The core sample taken at the highest elevation had 11 inches of water in it . Each morning , Bardwell and Sam Sulser and Alex Brooks , all based in the Rocky Mountain Ranger District , awoke to a half inch of additional snow . <p> As of Wednesday , mountain snowpack in the Missouri River basin in Montana was 112% of normal , and 114% of normal in the Sun-Teton-Marias basin , Zukiewicz said . <p> That will assure normal water supplies for the state , if weather conditions play out favorably . <p> " The last thing that we want is some of these anomalous events we 've had the last two years in a row , " said Zukiewicz , referring to heavy spring and early summer rains . " The May rain event and June event , those are just anomalies . You ca n't forecast for those . " <h> ' Matter of time before catastrophe ' <p> Most of the big flooding events in Montana are not on the main stem of the Missouri River because is is heavily regulated by dams , said Arin Peters , a senior hydrologist for the National Weather Service in Great Falls . <p> Even if flooding does n't occur in Montana , Peters cautioned , flows originating in the state can affect those downstream . He notes that Montana makes up about 10.9 percent of the entire Missouri River basin , contributing a remarkable amount to the river 's flows . <p> " It 's a good thing to keep in mind the context , how much our weather patterns and snowpack can make or break , theoretically , a very large flood event downstream , " Peters says . <p> In 2018 , for example , Montana 's April snowpack was 150% of normal , then 7 to 9 inches of rain over six days drenched the Rocky Mountain Front , inundating communities in its shadow . Even the 60,000 residents of Great Falls braced for flooding . <p> Disaster was averted for Great Falls , but the Corps was forced to release water from the Fort Peck Dam spillway , the final stop for the state 's runoff , as a result of surging flows , Peters said . Had that same combination of heavy rain and snow pack occurred in 2019 , flooding in states downstream would have been even worse , he said . <p> " With the way things are changing with our climate , " Peters said , " it 's probably a matter of time before something combines to create a big catastrophe downstream . " <p> This year , however , looks to be a normal runoff year for main stem rivers in Montana , Peters said . " Which is good for everybody downstream who are already worried , " Peters said . <h> Managing the levees <p> Most of the Missouri 's levees fall into one of two categories : either federally built and locally operated or locally built and operated . The Corps inspects -- and helps pay to repair -- only the levees maintained to federal standards that participate in the federal flood program . <p> That exception means no one has a full list of damaged levees still in need of repair . <p> The number of levees that are n't regularly inspected does n't surprise Neal Grigg , an engineering professor at Colorado State University who chaired a Corps-appointed review panel after 2011 flooding . <p> In an ideal management system , every levee " would be under the responsibility of some authority that was responsible and had enough money and good management capability to do that , " Grigg said . <p> But that 's not realistic , he added , noting that the Corps has tried through a task force to get some organization to the levee systems along the river , but it 's problematic , in part , because there are so many conflicting interests . <p> A host of agencies are cooperating to repair levees , but the progress is slow , said Missouri farmer Morris Heitman , who serves on the Missouri River Flood Task Force Levee Repair Working Group . <p> In addition to the Corps , the Federal Emergency Management Agency , the state of Missouri , the USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service and a large number of local levee districts all work to repair levees in his vicinity . <p> " We 're trying to dance with different agencies , " Heitman told the University of Missouri Extension . " All these agencies have their own requirements and parameters , and we 're trying to coordinate those to build a secure system against the river . " <p> Fixes to the 144 levee systems listed in disrepair in the Corps ' Omaha and Kansas City districts are in various stages of completion , and some are n't expected to be done for more than a year . <p> In the Omaha district that includes Nebraska and Iowa , " pretty much all of the levees were damaged in one way or another , " said the corps ' Matt Krajewski . <p> While almost all of the district 's levees that qualify for federal aid have been restored to pre-2019 flood heights , Krajewski said they do n't offer the same level of " risk reduction " because they need final touches such as sod cover and drainage structures to protect against erosion . The Corps hopes to complete those repairs this summer . <p> In the meantime , the Corps is working to prepare its flood storage capacity by releasing more water than normal from its dams . <p> " We 're being really aggressive with our releases and trying to maintain our full flood storage , " said Eileen Williamson , a Corps spokeswoman for the Northwestern region . <p> But the projections for spring runoff do n't look good and may limit how much the Corps can do . <p> In February , the runoff was twice the normal average , said Kevin Grode , with the Corps ' Missouri River Basin Water Management district . But it 's not just the spring runoff that 's a problem , he added . The forecast also calls for " above average runoff for every month in 2020 . " <p> John Remus , chief of the Corps ' Missouri River Water Management Division , said during a March briefing that if those projections are realized , " the 2020 runoff will be the ninth highest runoff in 122 years of record keeping . " <h> Rebuild or not ? <p> Before group limits and social distancing due to the new coronavirus , the congregation of Ebenezer United Church of Christ in Levasy , Missouri , still gathered each Sunday , about 60 to 70 strong . <p> They just were n't meeting in tiny Levasy , just a few stone throws from the Missouri River . A levee breach last spring covered the community in floodwaters nearly a foot high . Now Pastor Jeanne Lischer addresses her flock in nearby Buckner , at the Heart of God Fellowship 's former building after that church built a new facility north of town . <p> Lischer , 62 , worries that the town 's very future could be at stake depending on how much rain and Montana snowpack the river brings this year . <p> While some residents have moved back to their homes , other residents have had to take refuge in mobile homes . Lischer herself relocated to a small cluster of homes in nearby Blue Springs for retired pastors . While homeowners with flood insurance received aid from the Federal Emergency Management Agency , there was no help for the church , where the floor and lower walls had to be ripped out due to mold growth . <p> " The current plan is to rebuild in Levasy with a flood wall , " she said , " but it might not be financially feasible . "
