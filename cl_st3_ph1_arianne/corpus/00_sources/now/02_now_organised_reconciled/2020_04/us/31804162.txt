<p> Plans had called for major festivals and events to celebrate the 50th anniversary in the Bay Area and around the nation , with environmentalists set to highlight accomplishments since 1970 and push for more renewable energy , parks , wildlife protections and efforts to slow climate change . <p> But with the coronavirus pandemic shutting down large gatherings , environmental groups and their allies have had to adapt . Instead , they 'll host an 11-hour live online event Wednesday with Pope Francis , Al Gore , famed marine biologist Sylvia Earle , and other leaders , scientists , musicians and celebrities . <p> Reflecting on what should have been a major moment in the sun after nearly four years of battling the Trump administration 's attempts to roll back hard-won environmental laws , environmental leaders say they are sanguine . <p> " We 're all missing the opportunity to interact with each other in real life , " said Michael Brune , national executive director of the Sierra Club . " But the most important thing is the health of our loved ones , and doing whatever we can to keep people safe . " <p> A crowd estimated at 20,000 people observes Earth Day at Philadelphia 's Fairmount Park on Wednesday , April 23 , 1970 . ( AP Photo ) <p> Brune , whose organization is based in San Francisco , said there are lessons from the pandemic that are relevant to climate change and other environmental challenges . <p> " First , respect science and the role of scientists , " he said . " Number two , everybody should play their part , whether it 's a governor , a CEO or individuals . And third , we need competence in leadership in times of crisis , particularly from the White House . " <p> As Texas Tech University climate scientist Katharine Hayhoe told the Associated Press last week : " Every disaster movie starts with a scientist being ignored . " <p> There 's no question , researchers say , that the warming of the planet will be the main challenge of the next 50 years . Earth already has increased roughly 2 degrees Fahrenheit in recent decades due to carbon dioxide from the burning of fossil fuels trapping heat in the atmosphere , which has sparked increased wildfires , sea level rise and stronger droughts . The 10 hottest years since 1880 all have occurred since 1998 . And depending on emissions in the coming decades , average temperatures could increase another 6 or more degrees , scientists say , wiping out coral reefs , shifting tropical diseases north , and prompting millions of refugees to flee into other countries . <p> But looking back over the past five decades , the amount of environmental progress that has been made is dramatic , and something often overlooked by many Americans . <p> How ? Fifty years ago cars ran on leaded gasoline . There was no curbside recycling . No California Coastal Commission . A whaling station that harpooned whales off the Golden Gate to make Kal-Kan dog food operated in Richmond until 1972 . <p> Bald eagles , sea otters , brown pelicans and gray whales were headed for extinction in 1970 . Now all are recovered . DDT was sprayed on farm fields . Developers were paving hillsides around San Francisco Bay at a frenetic pace , while loggers across Northern California were clear-cutting redwoods with little oversight . And South Bay waters were a vast , stinking " dead zone " choked by sewage . <p> Years ago , air pollution was also dramatically worse . In 1969 , there were 65 days when Bay Area air quality exceeded federal health levels . Last year under the same standard , there were zero -- the result of laws requiring cleaner-burning gasoline , catalytic converters and smog checks for vehicles , and tougher rules on factories and power plant emissions -- even though the Bay Area population has increased 70% since then . <p> Meanwhile , the amount of parks and protected open space in the nine-county Bay Area has nearly quadrupled from 340,000 acres in 1969 to 1.2 million acres in 2017 , largely because Bay Area voters passed tax measures in the East Bay and elsewhere , and created new agencies like the Midpeninsula Regional Open Space District , while Congress established the Golden Gate National Recreation Area , protecting land from the Peninsula to the Marin Headlands and beyond . <p> After generations of filling and paving , by 1970 there were only about 35,000 acres of tidal marsh left around San Francisco Bay . Today restoration projects have increased that to 53,000 acres , with plans underway to restore another 21,000 acres , said Letitia Grenier , a senior scientist with the San Francisco Estuary Institute . The eventual goal is to get to 100,000 acres -- roughly half the total from 200 years ago -- for fish , birds , human recreation and a much cheaper form of flood control than building concrete walls . <p> " We converted marshes to other uses , like cities , salt ponds and hay fields , " she said . " And then we have realized we really need them . We 've been trying to change them back into marshes as fast as we can . " <p> In 1970 , American cars averaged 12 miles per gallon . The 1973 Arab Oil Embargo , worsening smog and environmental laws led to changes . Today , new cars average 26 miles per gallon . Teslas and Toyota Priuses are everywhere in California . <p> And a clean energy revolution is underway : California generated 34% of its electricity last year from renewable energy , nearly triple the amount from 10 years earlier , while 1 million California homes now have solar panels on their roofs . State law requires 100% of electricity to be generated from renewable or carbon-free sources like solar , wind , dams and nuclear by 2045 . <p> " We 've come a long way , " said Fran Pavley , a former state senator from Los Angeles who wrote many of California 's landmark clean energy laws over the past 20 years . <p> " Growing up in Southern California , I remember not being able to go out for recess in elementary school because the air was so bad , " she said , noting that former Gov . Ronald Reagan , a Republican , created the state Air Resources Board to curb smog . <p> As environmental rules tightened , California 's economy continued to grow , and at $3 trillion today is the fifth largest in the world . <p> " It shows you the role of regulations , and that markets can adapt , " Pavley said . " Companies can come up with cleaner fuels and manufacture cleaner vehicles . They always say they ca n't , but they can . " <p> Many of the changes are due to landmark federal laws like the Clean Air Act , Clean Water Act , Endangered Species Act and the creation of the EPA , all of which happened in a three-year period from 1970 to 1973 , often with bi-partisan support , including from former President Richard Nixon . California has since passed many of its own tougher laws , which often have been copied by other states and eventually adopted by the federal government . <p> But environmental progress has hit a wall in recent years . President Trump has appointed oil and coal industry lobbyists and officials into top jobs at the EPA and Interior Department . Those agencies have pushed for increased oil drilling off California 's coast , in the Arctic and on public lands across the West . Last week , the EPA issued a plan to weaken mercury pollution standards from coal-fired power plants . Trump pulled out of the Paris Climate Agreement to curb global warming emissions and is trying to block California 's longstanding right to set its own auto pollution standards . <p> " Unfortunately a lot of the momentum has been lost , " said Jeff Koseff , a professor of civil and environmental engineering at Stanford University and co-founding director of the Stanford Woods Institute for the Environment . " Maybe we can regain it now . But the last four years have been a bit of a blow in terms of getting things done . I hope we can get it back to the point where it is front and center . We have to because climate change is our future . " <p> Waves break over the seawall on San Francisco 's Embarcadero during high tide in 2011 .
