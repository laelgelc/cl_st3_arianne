<p> But while the economic fallout from Covid-19 threatens to be worse than the 2008 crisis , this time , business , green and other groups have come forward with a long list of climate-friendly ways the government could get the economy humming . <p> " We should prioritise projects that support the transition to zero carbon , " says Mike Burrell , head of the Sustainable Business Council . " There 's never been a better time to do infrastructure . Borrowing is as cheap as it will ever be and the borrowing is going to happen anyway . <p> " Let 's look through the half-dozen things on the pie chart of what contributes to New Zealand 's greenhouse gases and say , ' How do we reduce each one ? ' " <p> Ross Giblin <p> The Warm Up New Zealand initiative provided free insulation to more than 285,000 homes at risk of being cold and damp . <p> WARM UP THE REST OF NZ <p> There 's healthy disagreement between various groups about the scope and priorities of the Government 's spend-up . <p> One project with broad support is improving housing . <p> The Warm Up New Zealand programme has put insulation and/or heat pumps in hundreds of thousands of houses , but up to 40 per cent of homes remain cold and damp , the Green Building Council says . <p> Burrell says retrofitting the remaining homes is a no-brainer . " It 's simple , it generates jobs , it 's low risk , and it will have a positive impact on emissions . " <p> Environmental organisations such as EDS agree , citing the energy savings from higher-standard homes . <p> Commercial buildings should be spruced up too , says Andrew Eagles , chief executive of the Green Building Council , since they 're enormous users of energy . <p> " A canny government investment of energy grants paid to building owners in full could slash energy use by upgrading insulation , replacing all lightbulbs with low-energy LEDs , and installing energy-efficient heating and ventilation , " says Eagles . <p> " Retrofitting just 1200 of Aotearoa 's largest commercial buildings to make them zero energy would reap energy savings equal to the annual electricity generated by all of the country 's wind turbines , saving businesses millions of dollars . " Eagles also supports tightening the building code for new buildings , to avoid costly retrofitting later . <p> SUPPLIED <p> Investing in solar panels and other forms of clean , green electricity generation could re-power New Zealand 's economy . <p> BOOST RENEWABLES <p> Another proposal with broad support is boosting clean electricity generation , as well as improving the grid that carries electricity around . <p> Using electricity more efficiently in buildings could free up some generation capacity , but more generation will be needed if , for example , electric transport takes off . <p> Groups such as EDS and Greenpeace support boosting solar power in homes and offices , as well as replacing existing fossil fuel power plants with renewables . <p> Support for these ideas comes from across the political spectrum . Burrell says renewable energy " would be a safe bet " . <p> The National Party 's climate change spokesperson , Scott Simpson , agrees : " A focus on renewables is good , and I like the suggestion to explore uptake of solar , " he says . " I 'd like to think every item of spending has an eye on what is good for the climate . " <p> SIMON O'CONNOR/STUFF <p> The feebate scheme aimed to encourage Kiwis to buy low-emissions cars and electric vehicles , but its fate is uncertain . <p> MORE EVS -- BUT HOW ? <p> A feebate scheme was dropped earlier this year when it could n't get past Government coalition partner NZ First . The scheme would have imposed a fee of up to $3000 on the cost of the least-efficient , New Zealand-new vehicles at the border , and made the climate-friendliest cars up to $8000 cheaper . <p> The cost of buying used vehicles that are already in New Zealand would n't have been affected . The Green Party remains supportive -- and Prime Minister Jacinda Ardern has said the proposal 's not dead yet . <p> However , National 's Simpson prefers smaller nudges towards uptake of electric vehicles ( EVs ) : " The feebate model is not a great way to incentivise uptake . There are obvious policies they could pick up , like extending the free road-user rates and ... inexpensive things like allowing them to use bus lanes and T2 lanes . " <p> Burrell says a major missing part of the EV puzzle is a nationwide network of charging stations . " The cost of light freight vehicles is coming down quickly , so that will fix itself , but what wo n't fix itself is charging infrastructure -- that 's where the government needs to talk to the private sector . <p> " It might be that the private sector will invest in charging stations , for example , but do n't want this undermined by government investments , " Burrell says . <p> MARTIN DE RUYTER <p> As road emissions are rising , some experts want the government to invest in electric vehicles and charging infrastructure . <p> WORK WITH BUSINESS <p> A charging network is just one area where Burrell says the private sector could invest with government . <p> Burrell reckons businesses have a serious adjustment coming , because the Government looks set to hold the purse strings to billions of dollars in additional spending . " Since the 1980s , we 've been very much a light-touch economy , and we are about to become a heavy-touch economy at a scale we have n't seen since the 1930s , " says Burrell . <p> Robert Muldoon 's spend-up during his time as prime minister in the 1970s and 80s -- dubbed Think Big -- gave New Zealand both enduring benefits ( the renewable energy-pumping Clyde Dam ) and costly dead ends ( a defunct synthetic fuels plant ) . <p> " The Government can avoid the worst excesses of the Think Big period , or we could have a repeat , " says Burrell . He wants the Government to look for climate-friendly projects that the private sector is already willing to invest in , and give them a boost . <p> " It might be as simple as giving a tax write-off if you put in an EV charging station , or incentivising companies to take a risk by making the path forward clear , " he says . <p> DOMINICO ZAPATA/STUFF <p> Greenies are calling on the government to protect and replant our native forests to stimulate the post-Covid-19 economy . <p> GIVE NATURAL AREAS A TIDY-UP <p> EDS chief executive Gary Taylor says the Government could get a huge workforce out planting and caring for public land . By protecting native forests from nibbling pests and invaders , workers would help safeguard huge natural carbon stocks , and support green tourism in the future , several ecologists and green groups suggested this week . <p> It might also help tourism -- assuming tourism returns . " Large numbers of workers could assist the Department of Conservation to strengthen our green infrastructure by creating new tracks and facilities , tackling pest and weed infestations ... replanting erosion-prone land in native forests and helping clean up old rubbish tips in danger from sea-level rise , " says Taylor . <p> Forest &amp; Bird came up with a comprehensive list of ideas -- including re-deploying tourism and forestry workers in the regions to help with track repairs and upgrades , fencing , and maintenance on conservation land . It makes sense to do it now , while tourist numbers are low , says the group . <p> Such spending would help boost employment and school attendance in the regions and keep some local communities viable , Forest &amp; Bird says . The same could be said of dune restoration at beaches and looking after streams and rivers by fencing and planting . <p> ANDY JACKSON/STUFF <p> New government buildings -- hospitals , prisons and schools -- could be told to meet the highest environmental standards . <p> SET THE STANDARD <p> Given that New Zealanders will be paying down this debt for generations , it would be a tragedy if the projects they 're buying did not last into the future . The Climate Commission has urged the Government to make sure any new infrastructure can withstand climate change , and is not , for example , going to be flooded by sea level rise . <p> Eagles , of the Green Building Council , says all shovel-ready projects should be able to prove they are lower-carbon than normal , using green certification schemes that are already being employed by the industry . " Emissions from the construction sector have leapt 66 per cent in a decade . New Zealand will not be able to hit the Government 's zero carbon goal without tackling emissions from the building and construction sector . " <p> Eagles wants to see a particularly high standard applied to government buildings -- hospitals , prisons and schools . " One great benefit to this is that it will also create thousands more ... green-collar jobs , " he says . <p> In 2009 , the Emissions Trading Scheme ( ETS ) was weakened in response to the global financial crisis . <p> This time , NZ Initiative chief economist Eric Crampton says , the Government needs to push on quickly with strengthening the ETS . <p> The Government was navigating measures to strengthen the ETS when Covid-19 happened . Finishing that process soon is crucial , says Crampton . <p> The shape of the ETS will help determine the future price of carbon emissions -- and that price will help determine what infrastructure is required . " If we know the ETS prices will be tripling or quadrupling over the next 10 years , say , that would let forecasters start making projections about transport demand and we will know what to invest in , based on expected price of petrol and other things . <p> " If we start putting the cart before the horse and saying , ' We are just going to have this green future ' , and do n't let infrastructure decisions fall out from the projected demand , we might be making a mistake . " <p> Crampton says the Climate Change Commission 's modelling will be important . <p> The commission acknowledged recently that Covid-19 threw its modellers a curveball , but it 's working to prepare projections before it makes emissions-cutting recommendations to the government next year . <p> ChinaFotoPress <p> The Green party is suggesting the country invest in a high-speed electric rail network between big cities and nearby towns . <p> CLEAN , FAST TRAINS <p> The Government 's last infrastructure announcement -- made before Covid-19 broke out -- was rather heavy on road spending . But mass transit is another area with broad support for a spending boost . <p> EDS , Forest &amp; Bird and other green groups want electrifying trains to be a priority . The Green party has announced it wants to spend $9b on fast electric passenger trains connecting provincial centres such as Whangarei , Palmerston North and Ashburton with their nearby cities of , respectively , Auckland , Wellington and Christchurch . Eventually , the trains would travel at up to 160kmh . <p> STUFF <p> The National party wants government to fund water storage as regions will be increasingly prone to droughts in future . <p> STORING WATER <p> Part of being climate-resilient will be preparing for the inevitable effects of climate change , as well as shrinking emissions . Simpson and his National Party colleague , agriculture spokesperson Todd Muller , want the Government to use this chance to build more water storage . <p> " Water storage has multiple benefits , " says Muller . " It 's a global competitive advantage -- the oil of the 21st century ... and we have it . " <p> Done right , says Muller , storing more water could supply the grid with hydro power , help farmers in dry areas grow food , and smooth out flows in rivers and streams that swing between floods and droughts . <p> Helping farmers use water more efficiently -- and prove that they 're doing so -- is also part of the answer , Muller says . <p> However , other groups are wary of propping up farming expansion in places where water is already scarce . <p> Youth climate organisation Generation Zero warns the value proposition of some so-called shovel-ready projects does not stack up , citing " irrigation dams in places unsuitable for agriculture " as an example . <p> PHIL WALTER/GETTY IMAGES <p> The country has been looking for a technical solution to reduce the amount of methane produced by livestock like cows . <p> BOOST BURP RESEARCH <p> New Zealand has spent tens of millions of dollars on research into new technology to cut farming emissions , such as feed additives , and a hoped-for vaccine that could cut cow 's methane-laced burps . <p> An independent review last year recommended the Government boost that spending to improve the scientists ' chances of succeeding with a breakthrough . <p> The Covid-19 recovery is a chance to bulk up that spending , says Simpson .
