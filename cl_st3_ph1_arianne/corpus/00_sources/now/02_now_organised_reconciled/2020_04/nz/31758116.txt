<h> Social inequalities could be strengthened by ongoing forces of climate change <p> Waitara sisters Joan and Esther Olsson say they do n't want to leave their home of 40 years , which is one of several along the Waitara East beach coastline , which is falling victim to the signs of climate change . <p> The effects of climate change will be felt the most by the people with the least resources to cope . Deena Coster reports . <p> The consequences of climate change could ultimately force two elderly pensioners out of their beachside home and into the unknown . <p> Sisters Joan and Esther Olsson are among the residents who live in the Waitara East beach settlement in north Taranaki . <p> Waitara is about 15 kilometres north of New Plymouth and has some of the highest levels of deprivation in the region , according to the New Zealand Index of Deprivation . <p> In the four decades the Olssons have owned their home , the landscape around them has altered significantly . <p> " It 's all changed , everything 's changed , " Joan , 76 , said . <p> She and 78-year-old Esther have no plans to move , but say that if the safety risk posed by rising sea levels , or damage wrought by more frequent storms - both issues which plague the area - force them out of their home , they would probably have to move into a flat . <p> " I do n't want to shift , " Joan said . <p> " I love this place . " <p> SIMON O'CONNOR/STUFF <p> Esther Olsson , 78 , has lived at Waitara 's East Beach for four decades and has seen first hand the damage high seas had caused . <p> It would be an emotional wrench for Esther as well . <p> " This is our special spot . " <p> Research shows the effects of climate change will not be spread evenly across the population and will be felt more keenly in areas where existing socio-economic and health inequalities exist . <p> In New Zealand , children , the elderly , people with disabilities and chronic disease and low-income groups are particularly vulnerable . <p> In October 2017 , the Human Health Impacts of Climate Change for New Zealand report from the Royal Society warned of indirect social effects , including disruption to health services and other social and economic challenges , including food security . <p> It also said an adverse impact on mental health and the health of the community was likely . <p> Another report , from the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs , highlighted a " vicious cycle " involving climate change and inequality . <p> It said initial disparities caused disadvantaged groups to suffer disproportionately from the adverse effects of climate change , resulting in even greater subsequent inequality . <p> It plays out like this : inequality increases the exposure of the disadvantaged group to the negative effects of climate change ; these differences then increase the disadvantaged group 's susceptibility to damage , and decrease their ability to cope and recover . <p> Last year , an expert from the United Nations issued a warning about " climate apartheid " - where the rich will be able to afford to buy their way out of the worst effects of climate change while the poor suffer the most . <p> In New Zealand , 17 per cent of children live in households below the poverty line , defined as 50 per cent of the median income before taking into account housing costs . <p> STUFF <p> Research into the impacts of climate change show the effects will not be spread evenly across the population and felt more harshly where existing socioeconomic and ethnic health inequalities exist . ( File Photo ) <p> About 148,000 children go without their basic needs being met , including not having decent shoes or getting enough food , while 65,000 suffer severe hardship . <p> And it 's a problem not going away anytime soon . <p> The 2020 Salvation Army State of the Nation report said nothing had changed in terms of addressing entrenched inequality and poverty in New Zealand . <p> So what happens when you add climate change and its consequences into the mix ? <p> Popular ideas suggested online regarding how individuals can help tackle climate change in their own lives include eating less meat , or cutting back on air travel . <p> Arguably , people living in poverty , or on its margins , are already doing this by default . Not out of concerns over the climate , but because they ca n't afford it . <p> For some whose work is dedicated to giving a hand up to those in need , the climate crisis is not on the radar . <p> Sharon Wills , manager of the New Plymouth Foodbank , said there was little time for the " big picture stuff " since the service ran from pay cheque to pay cheque , much like its clients . <p> " We struggle to get enough food and money just to keep running . " <p> ANDY JACKSON/STUFF <p> New Plymouth Foodbank manager Sharon Wills says the service , like its clients , are more focussed on surviving week to week , and do n't have time yet to consider climate change and its impacts . ( File Photo ) <p> One of the dramatic swings she had seen , as a result of wetter weather , was an increase in power bills . <p> Wills said this directly impacted on how much money people had left in their pockets , and therefore the demand on the foodbank 's stores . <p> " It puts a strain on our supply . It 's just a vicious cycle . " <p> For the disabled and ageing sectors of society , climate change was a real consideration , New Plymouth-based advocate Lance Girling-Butcher said . <p> Extreme temperatures not only affected the health and welfare of the older generation - they could also contribute to fluctuating power bills . <p> Tracey Bryant , Waitara Alive Board chair and general manager of Wise Charitable Trust , said work was going on to find ways for lower socioeconomic sections of the community to adapt . <p> " We are certainly aware that there are changes and Waitara is a vulnerable community . " <p> SIMON O'CONNOR/STUFF <p> Disability and positive ageing advocate Lance Girling-Butcher says climate change is certainly a factor for both these vulnerable groups . <p> Bryant said the lack of transport options and the scant resources of some Waitara residents raised questions about their ability to stockpile groceries or safely leave the area , if it was hit by an extreme weather event . <p> " If they ca n't afford to keep fuel in their petrol tank , how are they going to evacuate ? " Bryant said . <p> One fledgling idea on the table to possibly help was setting up a community energy network , which would provide a standalone power supply so residents were not reliant on heavy infrastructure . <p> It could ultimately lead to cheaper power bills in future too , Bryant said . <p> However , the viability of such a project in the town was only being considered and it was too early to know if it would go ahead , she said . <p> ANDY JACKSON/STUFF <p> Waitara Alive board chair Tracey Bryant is also general manager of Wise Charitable Trust . Both groups are asking questions about how they can help lower-socioeconomic groups cope with climate change .
