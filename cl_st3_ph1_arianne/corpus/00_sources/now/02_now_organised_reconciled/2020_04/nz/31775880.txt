<h> Kiwi kids left behind in a Covid-19 world <p> What about the children ? They might not get infected as severely but the pandemic and economic crisis could hit the youngest Kiwis hard if we do n't take the chance to change , argue Penelope Milsom and Helen Walls . <p> Children without an underlying health condition are thought to contract only mild Covid-19 infection . As such , besides concerns about their potential to spread coronavirus , considerations of our children have largely been left out of New Zealand 's Covid-19 response . <p> However , this crisis has enormous implications for our tamariki and these impacts now and in their futures will disproportionately affect those children already disadvantaged by poverty , health inequities , domestic violence , and social marginalization , as well as the future effects of climate change . <p> Such impacts are , for many Maori children , themselves a legacy of colonialism and discrimination , with one in four Maori and one in three Pasifika children currently living in material hardship compared to one in 10 European children . We urge the Government to move beyond the mindset of Covid-19 as a short-term problem requiring isolated fixes with the aim of returning to business as usual as quickly as possible . Instead , we suggest a holistic approach to decision-making during and after this crisis -- one that seeks to minimise the economic , social , and politically-driven inequities deepened by Covid-19 . <p> The International Monetary Fund ( IMF ) declared the global economy has entered a recession that may well be worse than then 2009 global financial crisis . New Zealand 's economy will not be spared . Despite the government 's $12.1 billion spending package , Covid-19 will result in job and income losses for many New Zealanders . Compounding the impacts of the existing housing crisis , many will no longer be able to afford rent forcing more families into overcrowded housing or unacceptable living situations . The most severe economic impacts will be felt by those already in low paying and precarious employment . As such , Covid-19 will undoubtedly result in an increase in the already 167,600 New Zealand children living under the lowest poverty line and in significant stress . <p> Increases in household poverty closely linked with unhealthy housing will have significant knock-on effects for our children 's health . We already know , for example , that children living in the most deprived areas of New Zealand are hospitalized for bronchiolitis ( a childhood respiratory disease ) at a rate nearly five times higher than those living in areas of low deprivation . <p> While it is commendable that the government has introduced a $2.5 billion welfare rescue package , as both Child Poverty Action Group and Auckland Action Against Poverty have indicated , the proposed supports are insufficient to ensure our most vulnerable children can live safe and dignified lives , including during this time of crisis . <p> New Zealand kids need more than a ' rescue welfare package ' . They need permanent transformation of the Working for Families Welfare Scheme to channel sufficient economic support to families who need it most and ensuring healthy affordable housing for all middle- and low-income families . <p> Our children suffer from some of the highest rates of child maltreatment in the OECD with nearly 150 children requiring hospitalisation for assault , neglect or maltreatment in 2018 alone , and this is ' just the tip of the iceberg ' of child maltreatment in our communities . Closing schools and confining children to their homes with the ' lockdown ' approach is likely exposing vulnerable children to more frequent abuse ( physical , sexual and/or psychological ) , particularly due to the heightened stress and anxiety experienced by many caregivers during this time of economic hardship and social isolation . <p> There is now a substantial body of evidence linking child maltreatment with poor physical and psychological health , and poor behavioural and educational outcomes , in childhood and adolescence , as well as in later life . Punitive social assistance reforms will not resolve these urgent problems . Rather , the key underlying risk factors of poverty and deprivation exacerbated by Covid-19 must be addressed . <p> We also anticipate Covid-19 will affect child nutrition . Nineteen percent of New Zealand children are already reported to live in food-insecure households . Thousands of children rely daily on school food programmes that are now widely inaccessible under lockdown . Increased economic hardship generated by Covid-19 is likely to lead to greater consumption of less expensive calorie-dense ' filler ' foods -- and reduced consumption of more nutritious foods -- or children simply going without . This will increase the already unacceptable levels of childhood nutritional deficiencies for a high-income country like ours , leading to greater risk of childhood illness amongst our most vulnerable . It will also add to the already 39 percent of New Zealand children between 5 -- 19 years who are overweight or obese , contributing later to our epidemic of non-communicable diseases including cardiovascular disease and diabetes . <p> In addition , while healthcare services remain open during Covid-19 , the enforceable lockdown strategy may well be deterring care-seeking for vulnerable children , particularly for Maori , who have historically experienced discrimination by law enforcement . Financial barriers to health care access are also likely to exacerbate existing access issues in the medium to longer term as unemployment rises , resulting in more serious childhood infection , injury or illness , contributing to child health inequities overall . This is concerning given that already , children living in the most deprived areas are three times as likely to require hospitalisation than those living in least deprived areas . <p> Finally , as Covid-19 necessarily consumes political attention and this year 's United Nations Climate Change Conference is delayed until 2021 , there is a real concern that urgently needed action on climate change will be deferred . Unless we adopt more ambitious and healthy climate policy supporting a rapid and just transition to a zero carbon economy , children of Aotearoa , particularly those already vulnerable to other deleterious impacts of Covid-19 , will be affected by climate change at every stage of their lives , including through rising temperatures and climate volatility affecting food security , mental illness , and the spread of infectious diseases . <p> It is essential that the Government focuses on an emergency response to Covid-19 to suppress transmission and if possible , eliminate the virus from New Zealand . We do not question the need for public health measures to achieve this . <p> However , any measures do need to be considered in terms of their wider societal consequences , and negative impacts mitigated where possible , especially where they exacerbate existing health , economic and social inequities . We therefore urge the New Zealand government to consider not only those most at risk of severe infection but also groups disproportionately affected by the knock-on effects of this pandemic , particularly our most vulnerable children . <p> Looking forward , post-outbreak resilience planning requires more than investment in pandemic preparedness , we need a policy framework that has social , economic and climate justice at its core and that cuts across traditional government departments and sectors to address the threats to the health and well-being of our children exposed and exacerbated by Covid-19 . <p> Given a number of common linkages between child health inequities , maltreatment and climate change , this new policy framework could implement interventions that would provide double- or even triple-duty actions . These could include , for example , solutions to child poverty that have been presented in the 2019 Welfare Expert Advisory Group of which the government implemented just a handful of the 42 recommendations . Immediate adoption of these recommendations in full would increase the dignity , health and wellbeing of our most vulnerable children . <p> Another example is nutrition policy that makes sustainable healthy plant-based diets more accessible to all children , tackling obesity , under-nutrition and the associated health inequities , as well as reducing carbon emissions . Such policies could be supported by redirecting subsidies from industries that contribute substantially to New Zealand 's greenhouse gas emissions to more sustainable agricultural and food system practices . <p> Covid-19 has shown that with political will and great leadership , radical policy change is possible . We therefore ask this Government , which expressly claims a commitment to child health and wellbeing , to muster the political will to ensure the protection of our children is at the heart of policymaking in New Zealand . We simply ca n't return to ' normal ' . Normal was the problem . <p> Credible information is crucial in a crisis . <p> The pandemic is pushing us into an unknown and uncertain future .
