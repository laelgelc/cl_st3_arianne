<p> There are primates on almost every continent , Chris Packham tells us early in the first episode of the BBC 's new three-parter , Primates ( and , no , that 's not including humans , smartass ) . The fact that until now , our closest relatives had never been given the platform they so clearly deserved is odd , but clearly someone at Television Centre had a brainwave in commissioning this , because the Natural History Unit -- that section of the Beeb responsible for Planet Earth , Blue Planet , Our Planet , Frozen Planet ( notice a theme ? ) -- have scored another winner . <p> It 's not hard to see why . In a format that works by anthropomorphising animals in the wild , monkeys are the most relatable species on the planet . After all , it 's much easier to set up a comic situation with a baboon than with a jellyfish , right ? The cast of Primates is wonderfully varied , from the Brazilian bearded capuchins , who use tools to break open nuts , to the silverback gorillas living in the Rwandan interior , some 4,000 times the size of the smallest primates , the mouse lemur . With so much rich variation to document and a biological order that interacts with humans more than many , many others , the only way they could have made something more popular might have been a programme called Dogs . <p> While David Attenborough is off teaching children geography during lockdown , Primates ' other masterstroke has been to enlists natural history broadcasting 's other superstar , Springwatch 's Packham , to narrate . Once you get past the cognitive dissonance of hearing him talking affably about leopards and gorillas instead of badgers , bats and nesting birds , it 's a perfect match -- and possibly the surest sign yet that the BBC is positioning Packham to be its next Attenborough . <p> GQ spoke to Packham about narrating , filming Springwatch from home and the mixed effect the global shutdown has had for efforts to fight climate change . <p> Primates are such a massive constituency of animals . There are so many different species out there and they 're so varied . How come a series like this had n't been done before ? <p> Chris Packham : Within natural history , we would have covered most of those groups before , with the exception of that one new species , the Tapanuli orangutan , which appears for the first time on British TV . Taxonomists argue about it ; there are somewhere between 450 to 500 different species , but you 're right , there is enormous diversity within those groups -- everything from the large silverback male mountain gorillas down to the mouse lemur , which is a tiny , tiny species in Madagascar . Some are diurnal , some are nocturnal , fulfilling a great range of niches . I thought the programme did a great job of spreading the interest between them , because I think we 've seen a lot of chimpanzees , bonobos , gorillas ... They 're what I call " T-shirt animals " ; they are the go-to pin-up stars . And there are good reasons for that . Those great apes are long-lived , social animals , they invest a lot in parental care , they take a long time to reach maturity , they have a sense of self , they know each other as individuals , not just as a chimpanzee or a gorilla . And they have a language . So I 'm really pleased that the makers of Primates used that affinity we have with great apes as a springboard to explore all the other species as well . Primates gives as much space and time to all the other groups as well . <p> You 're narrating this programme . Is that as satisfying as actually getting out there in the field and standing in front of camera ? Do you feel like you 're missing out ? <p> Of course , yes . But it is n't justified . I always say to the people that employ me : " You 've got to justify my existence here by using me in the right way . " There are some programmes where a presenter , I think , is a real asset , because it gives that person the ability to engage directly with the audience and lead them through what could be a complex narrative , or it could be something which is emotional and people respond to other humans emotionally if they 're telling them that story . But here , it would have been probably unnecessary and impossible to haul me to the top of every tree or take me too every cliffside . <p> You just mentioned one of the most exciting things inPrimates : it 's the first time a Tapanuli orangutan has been shown on British TV . How does that feel ? <p> Very special . Obviously , it 's exciting that here in 2020 , we are still discovering large mammal species on a fairly overcrowded and small planet . The idea that there was still corners of it that are sufficiently unexplored to turn up these things is tremendously exciting . In the same breath , we have to say that as much as we were excited about announcing the discovery of the species and showing it for the first time , we are also having to tell people that it 's already severely endangered . So there 's sort of a cruel irony there . <p> Seventy-five per cent of these species are in decline . Fifty per cent of those are in danger of extinction . And it 's a group of animals that play fairly important ecological roles . They 're what we call ecosystem engineers : they eat food , they move from one part of the forest to the other , they shit out the seeds . So it drops with a package of fertilizer onto the forest floor and the trees grow . Without them doing that job , you would n't get seed dispersal . We should never conserve things because of the way they look ; we conserve them for what they do . Without orangs , those forests will go into decline . And there 's nothing we can do about it unless we start eating seeds and climbing around in the canopy and pooing . And no one 's gon na do that . <p> You 've spoken a lot about emphasising the climate emergency in nature programs , and you 've called on the filmmaking community to do more of that . It sounds like you 're broadly happy with that aspect ofPrimates ? <p> I 'm constantly goading the people who make these films . That 's what I get up in the morning to do ; I want to make a difference . I 'm not here to make friends . I am constantly , politely goading them . But I have to say , I have n't needed to be as diligent , because within the BBC , in the last two years or so , we 've seen a sea change in attitude , whereby everyone has woken up to the fact that we have to be a lot more honest about what 's going on . We have to tell people the truth as a means of helping to motivate and make a difference . I felt the message in the programme was quite forthright . We 're not hiding anything . We 're telling people the truth about the perilous fate of this group of animals . I think it 's good . <p> As we enter the second month of coronavirus lockdown , there is a reported resurgence of wildlife around the UK , but you have also mentioned a setback in climate action as a result of lockdown . Has the lockdown helped the natural world ? <p> There are a couple things . Firstly , there 's apparency . Apparency is where things are there , but you do n't notice them . Everyone 's saying , " All the birds are singing louder this year . " All it is is that there 's no traffic , so you can hear them better . It 's that simple . People have got a little bit more time on their hands and many people are spending the hour that they exercise engaging with nature . They 're seeing things that , probably , they would have walked past . That 's the key thing . That 's great , because it means people are engaging with it . I 'm not criticising the fact that people are listening to birdsong . <p> The second thing is : yes , there are real , tangible differences . One of the sweetest stories that I like , which comes from relatively contemporary science conducted in Italy , was that when we burn petrochemicals in cars , some of the emissions bind to the molecules that plants use to generate scent . Plants obviously produce flowers as an advertisement , but the way they attract pollinators such as bees works in several ways . Firstly , it works in an olfactory way , from distance . So the pollinators fly along , they get the scent of the flower , they fly towards it -- then they see it and it becomes a visual effort . But if they ca n't smell it to start with , their ability to forage is dramatically reduced . We know that in some urban areas , under normal situations , bees need to be 50 per cent closer to the flowers to be able to detect them , because of the byproducts of cars . So with all of this reduction in traffic in our cities , those bees will be finding flowers more efficiently ; they will prosper . <p> We have proved that when we need to , we can do pretty much anything <p> Obviously , everything -- necessarily ; that 's not a criticism -- is focused on dealing with coronavirus at the moment . All the campaigning that I was doing has quite rightly and understandably been put on hold . No one 's getting out there to monitor what 's going on , so there are concerns that behind the scenes things might not be quite so rosy . But cast your mind back a year to last April , when we were all doing Extinction Rebellion in London . The message in response was quite clear : it is this big , existential problem beyond our scope and we ca n't do this , we ca n't do that . We ca n't cut down on air travel . No , no , no . It 's impossible . Well , it 's just been outed . In two months , we 've taken draconian , drastic action to deal with this crisis . And we have proved that when we need to , we can do pretty much anything . So those people that wanted to push back about our ability to respond and change ? They 're out to dry . They 've lost it . <p> You 've been filming in your back garden forSelf-Isolating Bird Clubon Facebook and there 's been talk aboutSpringwatchand how that 's going to happen from isolation -- can you give us an update ? <p> As I 've been here , at home in the New Forest with Megan , my stepdaughter , we 've been doing these live broadcasts every morning for the last month . Over the last four weeks , we 've had four and a half million views , which is pretty good . They 're for people who are doing what we 're doing , which is connecting with nature in our gardens . It 's all about that birdsong ; it 's about seeing the butterflies , seeing the wildflowers come out . We 've got viewers all over the world . We had someone joining us live from Spain this morning . We 've had people sending things from Namibia , China . It 's a global community of people who are finding respite and solace in the nature that 's on their doorsteps , which is a microcosm of what Springwatch does . <p> Springwatch is all about celebrating the UK 's wildlife communities and those animals that we share our spaces and lives with . We try to stay away from any anything exotic ; we love our badgers and our foxes , because these are animals that everyone can get to meet . This year , the model is going to be obviously compromised , because none of us can move . But what I can tell you is that we will be making a series and the plans are fluid . We 're beginning to put as much in place as possible and it will depend on what the government advice is at the time . We have had a wealth of cameramen filming in their gardens and in the areas that they are allowed to visit , so we 've put together quite a lot of material already . We 've got that in the bank . And we are determined to put a programme out . We 're going to very much concentrate on people engaging with wildlife , even if they 've just got a window to look out of . We 're going to celebrate that .
