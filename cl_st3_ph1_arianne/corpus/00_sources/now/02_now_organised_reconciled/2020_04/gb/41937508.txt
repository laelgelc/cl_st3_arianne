<p> Figuring out how geoengineering can affect climate change is difficult , owing to the fact we do n't have any spare Earths hanging around to use as test subjects . However , a new study published in Nature has found a way around this problem -- and excitingly , it involves volcanoes . <p> The eruptions of El Chich ? n in Mexico in 1982 , and of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines in 1991 , are among the largest of the 20th century , and both are larger than any eruption of the 21st century so far . These powerful eruptions sent huge quantities of sulphur dioxide into the upper atmosphere , causing measurable decreases to the amount of light reaching the Earth 's surface for several years . <h> Advertisement <p> " It just so happens that sulphur in the atmosphere is really only moved around in recent history by these massive volcanic eruptions of El Chich ? n and Mount Pinatubo , " says lead author Jonathan Proctor , of the department of agriculture and resource economics at the University of California , Berkeley . " The smaller volcanic eruptions simply do n't have the power to punch through . " <p> Explosions such as these inspired the stratospheric veil , also known as the ' Budyko blanket ' after climatologist Mikhail Budyko , who first suggested it in 1974 as a form of geoengineering . The veil is specifically a method of solar radiation management . The basic idea is that additional sulphur-based aerosols are added into the atmosphere , which then increases the amount of sunlight reflected back into space , and distributes the light that does pass through more widely . <h> Read next <h> Scientists are home-brewing lava to crack deadly volcanic mysteries <p> ByAmit Katwala <p> It is hoped that by doing this the planet 's surface would cool , and crops around the world would grow more effectively due to a decrease in ' heat stress ' . This refers to a collection of problems caused in plants by hot weather , including not producing fruit , dropping its leaves , and rotting . In addition , it was expected that the better spread of sunlight would make better use of their leaves ' surface areas and also encourage them to grow more , as had been observed in unmanaged ecosystems . <p> But as Solomon Hsiang , an associate professor at the Goldman School of Public Policy at Berkeley and one of the study 's authors , says , nobody up to now had tried to use data from the volcanic eruptions to investigate the effects of stratospheric veils . " Everything we learned about geoengineering that was inspired from a climate physics standpoint , came from these original eruptions . But up until now , no one has used these historical events to understand the possible economic or social or ecological consequence . " <p> The study uses the records of the changing atmospheric sulphate and light levels in the wake of the two previously mentioned eruptions , and compares them to the yields of wheat , rice , maize and soy around the world during the same period . The sulphate levels were taken from satellite and Earth-based observations , while the crop data was recorded by the United Nations statistics division 's food and agriculture organisation , with studies only include countries that had posted results every year between 1983-2009 . <p> What the researchers found was that while this cooling process would happen , and would be beneficial to the crops , blocking the sunlight also decreases their photosynthesis activity , which negates almost all of the benefits . The better distribution of light did mean the plants grew larger as a result , but this growth did not end up increasing the yield of edible material . When applying these findings to stratospheric veils , the conclusion , in Proctor 's words , was that these veils were " an ineffective way to mitigate the damages that climate change poses to agricultural production " . <h> Advertisement <p> A potential answer would be to get farmers around the world to change their crops for ones better suited to warmer climes , but this would almost certainly prove to be very difficult to achieve , given that it is already difficult to change farming behaviour around the world more generally , according to Hsiang . " Even in the present climate , we have difficulty getting farmers to use their land to the best of their abilities , to maximise its potential output , " he says , referencing farmers in parts of Asia , South America and Africa who do not correctly fertilise their crops , or try to cultivate plants that are not suited to their climate . " Trying to fine tune it to deal with the effects of solar radiation management would probably be as difficult as just getting them up to the baseline , " he explains . <p> But Hsiang says the study did not want to pass judgement on the effectiveness of stratospheric veils in their entirety , suggesting that there may be net benefits in different cases . Or , to put it more simply , he compares the use of veils to chemotherapy . " We know that chemotherapy is a therapy that helps aid individuals with cancer , but it also has very traumatic side effects . There are some cases in which those risks are judged to be worth the consequences in order to avoid potentially larger harms . " <p> All the same , Hsiang and Proctor hope that their novel approach of estimating the impact of interventions to climate change to a specific form of production will inspire future studies . As long as there is data available for comparison , then this could be applied to the productivity or health of other ecosystems or human populations in certain areas or occupations . " This study is a bit like a template or cookbook for how you could explore other alternative impacts in the future , " says Hsiang.
