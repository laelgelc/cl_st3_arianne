<h> Roger Domeneghetti on the 60th birthday of the boot that has left more of a footprint on Britain than any other <p> Almost four years after its creation The New European goes from strength to strength across print and online , offering a pro-European perspective on Brexit and reporting on the political response to the coronavirus outbreak , climate change and international politics . But we can only continue to grow with your support . <p> It was in April 1960 that Brits first started sliding into their DMs . In 60 years since , the Dr. Martens boot has stomped its way off the factory floor , across football terraces onto catwalks and into high street stores . Yet this iconic item of fashion , which has come to represent British culture on the international stage was actually born in the heart of Bavaria at the tail end of the Second World War . <p> In 1945 Klaus Maertens , a German army doctor , was recuperating after breaking his ankle in a skiing accident . The 25-year-old found the standard issue boots with their hard leather soles both uncomfortable and a hindrance to his recuperation . <p> Maertens was bemused by the fact that it was possible to get a smooth ride in a car using air-filled tyres but nothing similar for your feet . So he set about designing a boot with softer sole . <p> When the war ended he acquired a cobbler 's last and produced a prototype by sewing cut up pieces of tyre to the bottom of his shoes . It lessened the impact but did not prevent the pain . Maertens did not have any significant success selling his shoes until he met up with an old university friend in Munich in 1947 . <p> Dr Herbert Funck , a mechanical engineer , was impressed by his former classmate 's design and suggested using PVC rubber shaped by moulds . <p> Each sole contained two layers , one stitched to the shoe 's leather upper , the other heat-sealed to the first , thus creating the air pockets . <p> The pair took out a patent and went into business together in Seeshaupt . Using old military supplies and rubber acquired from Luftwaffe airfields they began producing comfortable , durable shoes and found a ready market in German housewives . <p> Around 80% of their sales in the first decade went to women over 40 . A second factory , in Munich , was opened in 1952 and by 1959 their company had grown so large they looked to expand into overseas markets . <p> The pair advertised their shoes in footwear trade magazines across Europe and these caught the eye of Bill Griggs , a Northamptonshire boot manufacturer . <p> Shoemakers had first been attracted to the county in medieval times . The area 's thriving tanning industry and plentiful supply of oak bark were ideal for cordwainers , as they were called . <p> It 's central location also made it a profitable trading post . By 1901 , 41,989 people were working in the town 's footwear industry and one of them was Benjamin Griggs , who set up the eponymous R Griggs Group Ltd . <p> Over the next half-century the family firm , based in the small town of Wollaston , built a strong reputation for making sturdy shoes and boots for workers . <p> By the late 1950s Benjamin 's grandson Bill was in charge and , after seeing the advert , he acquired the exclusive UK rights to produce Maertens and Funck 's innovative shoes . <p> In return the pair received a royalty payment for each shoe sold . Griggs then set about altering Dr Maertens ' design . <p> The heel was slightly adjusted to improve the fit , the upper was made rounder and slightly fatter and contrasting yellow stitching introduced . <p> The sole was given a new pattern as well as a grooved , two-tone edge . This unit was trademarked ' Airwair ' . <p> The word adorned a black-and-yellow heel loop which also featured the slogan " With Bouncing Soles " . <p> Lastly , Griggs anglicised their creator 's name and the Dr. Martens boot was ? born . <p> With eight lace holes and an oxblood leather finish , they were launched on April 1 , 1960 and christened the ' 1460 ' . <p> Aimed at miners and factory workers this was the firm 's primary , and for a while only , model . <p> Later editions , such as a shoe with three eyelets , were favoured by postal workers and police officers . <h> You may also want to watch : <p> The boots may never have become anything other than unsung , utilitarian worker 's footwear had it not been for a young musician looking for more comfortable clothes while playing on stage . <p> In 1967 Pete Townshend walked into a hardware store to buy a boiler suit to wear at a gig . As he wandered around the shop he came across a pair of orange Dr. Martens . <p> He bought them and wore them that night . Like posties and bobbies on the beat , Townshend found that the boots ' air-cushioned sole helped him do his ? job . <p> He subsequently told Martin Roach the author of Dr. Martens : A History of Rebellious Self Expression that " their toughness combined with their softness and flexibility is what made them so perfect for the pseudo-athletic routines I performed on stage . I literally bounced . " <p> But the boots did n't just represent comfort and athleticism for Townshend , they also heralded an aggressive , austere style that rejected what had come before.They " released me from psychedelia and all the nonsense that went with it , " he said . <p> As the 1960s gave way to the 1970s DMs became a staple of a variety of youth subcultures . <p> They were most warmly embraced by skinheads who used them to complete their working class uniform . <p> Now skinheads are widely associated with the racism of neo-Nazis or the alt-right but this early , 1960s incarnation was different . As Don Letts , the filmmaker and DJ , who was born to Jamaican parents in London in 1956 , noted in his BBC documentary The Story of Skinhead : " Politics was n't really something that we talked about . That was on our parents ' level . We just wanted to bond over music , clothes , and girls . " <p> Yet the skinhead dress code was still a political statement ; a conscious articulation of class . <p> By adopting Fred Perry polo shirts , traditionally found on the tennis courts of middle England , and pairing them with Levis and Dr. Martens , skinheads were subverting English elitism . <p> The boots became the everyman 's shoe , transcending class , background and wealth . <p> The MP Tony Benn began wearing the boots to signify his solidarity with the working classes . <p> In 1975 , Elton John wore a pair of over-sized 54-inch tall brown Dr. Martens boots ( actually made of fibre glass ) in Ken Russell 's film version of The Who 's rock opera Tommy . <p> It further cemented their iconic status . As the decade wore on the lines that separated skinheads and mods blurred and they subdivided into a range of different tribes , such as punks , psychobillies , goths and adherents of two-tone and glam rock . <p> One thing continued to unite them : Dr. Martens boots . Having kickstarted the trend , Townshend went on to document it in his 1982 single Uniforms ( Corp d'Esprit ) , singing : " It do n't matter where you 're from , What matters is your uniform ... Dr. Martens on your feet ... For credibility on the street . " <p> Association with groups like The Who , The Clash and The Sex Pistols gave the boots international exposure and their anti-establishment credentials saw Dr. Martens adopted by grunge bands like Nirvana in the 1990s . <p> At the same time , back in Britain , they were worn by Britpop bands like Blur . It was also an era when the boots became popular with young women . <p> Hollywood stars such as Demi Moore and Winona Rider wore them on the red carpet while supermodels like Kate Moss and Naomi Campbell wore them on the catwalk . <p> This started a trend of brides wearing white patent leather boots on their wedding day which in turn saw the company release a bridal version of Dr. Martens in gold with white laces . <p> The brand slumped in popularity in the early 2000s . It was a period that saw missteps such as controversial adverts which portrayed dead rockstars like Kurt Cobain and Sid Vicious in heaven sporting Dr. Martens with their flowing white robes . <p> But their appeal is as hard-wearing as the boots themselves and -- until the coronavirus intervened -- sales have been going from strength to strength . There are Dr Martens stores on our high streets , Miley Cyrus wore a pair ( and little else ) in the video of her song Wrecking Ball and they are favoured by the likes of Pharrell Williams and Gigi Hadid . <p> The boots are still a sign of self-expression but they have become more of a fashion item than a sartorial sign of rebellion . <h> Become a Supporter <p> Almost four years after its creation The New European goes from strength to strength across print and online , offering a pro-European perspective on Brexit and reporting on the political response to the coronavirus outbreak , climate change and international politics .
