The strain of climate change on villagers meant that many of them fled to the Vale of Evesham and Severn Vale . <p> Lancaut <p> Located on the Gloucestershire border with Wales , just two miles north of Chepstow , stands the deserted village of Lancaut . <p> Nestled in the Wye Valley , not much remains of Lancaut except from the small church of St. James , which has no roof . <p> The name is an anglicised version of the Welsh ' Llan Cewydd ' , which translates to ' Church of Saint Cewydd ' . Saint Cewydd was the obscure 6th century Welsh ' rain saint ' . <p> As of 1306 , the village had 10 tenant households , but by 1848 there were only 16 inhabitants in the whole settlement ( Image : Daren **28;1140;TOOLONG on Instagram ) <p> The east of Lancaut was bordered by Offa 's Dyke - a large , linear earthwork structure which roughly follows the border between England and Wales . It was completed in the 8th century , to show the border between Anglican Mercia and the Welsh Kingdom of Powys . <p> Although it is situated on the eastern bank of the River Wye , Lancaut was posessed by the Welsh until the 10th century , when it was incorporated with the English king 's manor of Tidenham . <p> Lancaut was never a large settlement , and there is no clear taxation history for it . In 1306 , the manor of Tidenham had ten tenants residing in Lancaut . <p> In 1551 , there were 19 adults in Lancaut and five households were recorded just over a decade later in 1563 . By 1710 , four families were living in the village , but only two inhabited houses remained in 1750 . <p> A lead font from St James Church is on show at Gloucester Cathedral , and is believed to date back to 1130-1140 . <p> Ditchfords <p> Although only two of them made it onto the 1968 list of deserted settlements in Gloucestershire , there are three deserted settlements at Ditchford , in the Cotswolds . <p> Upper Ditchford , Middle Ditchford and Lower Ditchford continued as separate farms after the demise of their villages . <p> The earthworks of Lower Ditchford are visible to the northwest of Knee Brook , to the north of Ditchford Mill Farm , near Todenham . <p> In the extensive earthworks of Lower Ditchford , at least twelve building enclosures and eight builidings have been identified . The fields surrounding the site are thought to have been seasonally flooded water meadows . <p> Lower Ditchford earthworks <p> Upper Ditchford can be found to the west of the Fosse Way between Moreton-in-Marsh and Stretton on Fosse , now in Gloucestershire but once located in Warwickshire . <p> Both Upper Ditchford and Lower Ditchford were abandoned as a result of the 19th century land enclosure acts , which saw small landholdings being enclosed into larger farms and ended the ancient system of arable farming in open fields . <p> The villages at Ditchford were regarded as ruinous in 1491 by John Rous , a priest of Warwick . <p> Upper Coberley <p> The earthworks of Upper Coberley , which is a short distance east of the current Cotswold village of Coberley , have been recorded to the west of Westbury and Lower Farm . <p> These earthworks allude to a roadway and the outlines of at least ten buildings , however the area where Upper Coberley once stood is now mostly covered with trees . <p> Farmland near Coberley , which is not far east of Upper Coberley <p> In 1086 , a hide ( an old measurement of land ) was recorded in the Domesday Book in Upper Coberley , as part of the manor of Northleach . <p> Upper Coberley was recorded with Northleach in later records , too , but the two were recorded separately in 1327 , and seven people were assessed in the smaller village . <p> Several decades later , in 1381 , at least 24 people in the village were taxed , and the village was present in records right up to the 16th century . <p> Today , the earthworks clearly point out that there was once a more extensive settlement than the three farms which currently exist on the site . <p> Lemington <p> Earthworks are visible between Upper Lemington ( which is now known as just Lemington ) and Lower Lemington . It is believed that there was one larger settlement , as opposed to two smaller ones . <p> The earthworks that run to the north of Lemington Manor are known as Upper Lemington , and they continue northwards to the church at Lower Lemington . <p> Both settlements were recorded as Lemington right up until the 16th century , which supports the idea of them being part of one big area . <p> Throughout the 17th and 18th centuries , a stable population was recorded at Lower Lemington - there were around 20 households in 1650 , but only six paid Hearth Tax in 1672 . <p> Earthworks near the church at Lower Lemington <p> The distinguishing of Lower and Upper Lemington is thought to be the result of two different land holdings and manorial descents . The settlement is now considered to be a shrunken settlement , rather than a deserted village . <p> If a settlement contains fewer than three inhabited houses , then it is regarded to be a deserted village . However , if it has more than three houses , it is said to be a shrunken medieval village . <p> Sennington <p> The earthwork remains of a settlement known as Old Sennington can be seen from the air , the name of which appears to be an alternative name for the nearby Sevenhampton . Other variants include Senhampton , and these names lend a clue that there may have been seven settlements . <p> The remains encompass at least two north-south holloways , and in 1889 a report of coins being found was published . This indicated that the original chapel of Sevenhampton may have been located at the settlement once . At present , the church of Sevenhampton is located 800m to the southeast . <p> In 1936 , some excavations took place at the sight of Sennington and revealed stone walls alongside 12th and 13th century pottery . <p> The earthworks of Sennington are very pronounced <p> Many small hamlets in the parish of Sevenhampton became deserted during the 14th century . However , no separate settlement of Sennington is recorded , and it may be possible that these earthworks are just remains of one of the smaller settlements of Sevenhampton . <p> Taynton Parva <p> Taynton Parva has a rich history , which is clearly visible from its earthworks which feature a motte and bailey , a ringwork , a moated site and fishponds , and a church . <p> Taynton Parva sits between the villages of Tibberton and Taynton , in the Forest of Dean . Its castle is the most vivid feature on the site , with the moated site to the east and the location of the church to the southeast . <p> The village is less clear from the air , as there are now a lot of trees on the site . Although it has been surveyed , the remains are still open to interpretation . <p> In the Domesday Book , a population of six was noted . In 1327 , nine people were assessed and by the 16th century , there was only a record for Taynton , which was not divided into Taynton Magna and Taynton Parva . <p> The church of Taynton Parva was in existence by 1134 but , by 1643 , it had been burnt down by royalists . In recent years , this has been confirmed by the discovery of metalwork in the area , particularly lead .
