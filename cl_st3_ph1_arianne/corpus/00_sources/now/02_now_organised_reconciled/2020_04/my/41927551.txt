<h> Humans to blame for spread of coronavirus and other ' zoonoses ' <p> The novel coronavirus originated in the animal kingdom and jumped to humans . -- AFP photo <p> Whether it came from a bat or a pangolin is not certain , but one thing is : the coronavirus outbreak that has killed tens of thousands and turned the world upside down comes from the animal world . <p> It is human activity that enabled the virus to jump to people , and specialists are warning that if nothing changes many other pandemics of this nature will follow . <p> The name given to diseases transmitted from animals to humans is " zoonoses " , based on the Greek words for " animal " and " sickness " . <p> They are not new -- tuberculosis , rabies , toxoplasmosis , malaria , to name just a few , are all zoonoses . <p> According to the UN Environment Programme ( UNEP ) , 60 percent of human infectious diseases originate from animals . <p> This figure climbs to 75 percent for " emerging " diseases such as Ebola , HIV , avian flu , Zika , or SARS , another type of coronavirus . The list goes on . <p> " The emergence of zoonotic diseases is often associated with environmental changes or ecological disturbances , such as agricultural intensification and human settlement , or encroachments into forests and other habitats , " said a 2016 UNEP report . <p> " Changes in the environment are usually the result of human activities , ranging from land use change to climate change . " <p> Gwenael Vourc'h of INRAE , a French public research institute , also blames human activity for the crossover between species . <p> " Given the growth of the human population and its ever more intense use of planetary resources , the destruction of more and more ecosystems multiplies contacts , " she says . <p> A key area of concern is deforestation to make way for agriculture and intensive livestock farming . <p> Domesticated animals are often a " bridge " between pathogens from the wild and humans . The widespread use of antibiotics in the livestock industry has also led to bacterial pathogens building up immunity to front-line drugs . <p> Urbanisation and habitat fragmentation are also highly disruptive of the balance between species , while global warming can push disease-carrying animals into new territory . <p> -- ' Unprecedented in human history ' -- <p> The novel coronavirus is believed to have emerged in a wet market in the central Chinese city of Wuhan late last year . <p> Scientists think it originated in bats and could have been passed on via another mammal like a pangolin , an endangered species whose meat and scales are highly prized in parts of Asia . <p> But researchers have yet to come up with a definitive answer on how it migrated to people . <p> The only sure thing is that human activity facilitated the jump . <p> " The process that leads a microbe , such as a virus , from a population of vertebrates such as bats to humans is complex , but driven by people , " says Anne Larigauderie , executive secretary of IPBES , the panel of UN experts on biodiversity . <p> " People , through their actions , create opportunities for the microbes to come closer to human populations . <p> " The rate of global change in nature during the past 50 years is unprecedented in human history , and the most important direct driver of change in nature is land use change . " <p> Beyond the current outbreak of coronavirus , IPBES estimates that zoonoses kill some 700,000 people a year . <p> A study by American researchers published last week and completed before the new coronavirus outbreak identifies rodents , primates and bats as hosts of three-quarters of viruses transmitted to humans . <p> But domestic animals also carry about 50 percent of the zoonoses identified . <p> In terms of endangered wildlife , the study shows that those who share the most viruses with humans are precisely " populations declining due to exploitation and loss of habitat " . <p> Christine Johnson , of the University of California veterinary school , who led the study , blames the human urge to " alter the landscape " . <p> " This also increases the frequency and intensity of contact between humans and wildlife -- - creating the perfect conditions for virus spillover , " she says . <p> -- ' Global tragedy ' -- <p> According to Larigauderie , this coronavirus outbreak may just be the tip of the iceberg . <p> " Increased trends in land use change , combined with increased trends in trade , and global travels , are expected to increase the frequency of pandemics in future , " she says . <p> " Transformative change is needed in order to find a solution to this global tragedy . " <p> Vourc'h is also calling for a systemic response . <p> " Beyond the essential response to each epidemic , we must think about our model ... rethink our relationship with natural ecosystems and the services they provide , " she says . <p> The 2016 UNEP report , which noted that " ecosystem integrity underlines human health and development " , said effective strategies already exist to control most neglected zoonoses . The main constraint , however , appeared to be " lack of investment " . <p> At 86 , Jane Goodall has spent most of her life studying and defending animals , especially chimpanzees in Africa , especially from Tanzania . And she pulls no punches on where she lays the blame . <p> " It was predicted that this was going to happen and it 's going to happen again until we learn the lessons , " warns the British primatologist . <p> " It is our disregard for nature and our disrespect of the animals we should share the planet with that has caused this pandemic. " -- AFP
