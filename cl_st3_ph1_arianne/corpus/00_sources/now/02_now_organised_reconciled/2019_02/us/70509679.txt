<p> In recent years my work has become more and more socially and politically engaged -- a colleague recently described it as " activist composition " . I 've written a multimedia saxophone concerto challenging society 's ideas of femininity , I 've written about anorexia , and I 've tackled female boxing ( which meant learning to box ) . My newest work is no different . I need to feel a sense of ownership and visceral experience of whatever it is that I 'm writing about , so for a project focusing on climate change , it felt essential to find a way to get to the remotest and most untouched part of our planet -- Antarctica . <p> For many of us living in urban environments , it 's difficult to understand the closeness that we can have to the environment . I wanted to travel to one of the most extreme parts of the world , a place almost as intangible and alien to the majority of the world 's population as climate change itself , and create a video and orchestral piece that gives people a sense of the absolute beauty and incredible nature @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ active in how they think about our planet . <p> My initial intention was to create a work that focused entirely on the Antarctic , but , as so often happens , my intentions were altered by the experience itself , and my composition became about the extraordinary voyage as much as the continent itself . In January 2018 , I flew from London to Buenos Aires , then down to Ushuaia -- fin del mundo -- and from there I sailed across the Drake Passage down to the Antarctic peninsula . The crossing depends on the winds , but it 's roughly one week each way . We then spent two weeks sailing around the peninsula . <p> Sailing on the Bark Europa , a square-rigged tall ship , comes with the obligatory duties of being on watch . Essentially four hours on , eight hours off . ( The main crew has a much more gruelling schedule of four hours on , four off . ) Whatever the weather , this means getting up at midnight , 4am or 8am , clambering up the stairs to the deck @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ rigging , hanging over the yards , steering on the helm , hauling ropes , watching out for icebergs was all part of the experience . All while wearing several layers of clothing that you have struggled into in the dark ( not wanting to wake your fellow crew members ) , topped off with a safety layer in the form of a climbing harness , to ensure that the humungous waves crashing over the already severely heeling ship do n't throw you overboard . <p> Sailing across the Drake Passage was an exhilarating , exhausting and bitterly cold eight days . I 'm an experienced sailor and was prepared for all of the above , but not for the sea sickness . I 've never suffered from it before and was confident that I would be one of the lucky passengers who made it through without any tummy issues . I was wrong . The first time we stepped on land was at Deception Island . It was overwhelmingly beautiful . <p> But , before you step anywhere there , you have to place your boots in a bright pink antibacterial bath to ensure you do n't contaminate the habitat ( and again when you come back on board the ship as well , so as not to contaminate subsequent sites ) . The shoreline was covered in thousands of penguins -- mainly chipstrap penguins -- accompanied by small groups of Weddell seals and a huddle of grunting young male elephant seals . I immediately started recording . <p> The initial excitement had temporarily clouded my other senses , but unfortunately my nose soon started working and I realised I was surrounded by the very potent , unpleasant smell of penguin poo . The first was the dry silence , particularly when we were drifting through the Lemaire Channel . The second was the the sound of the most active penguin colonies , the deafeningly loud constant barking alongside all their other sounds -- exclamations , cries , squeaks and strange grunting . The third was the ice . I became obsessed with the variety of crackling , sizzling and fizzing qualities of the brash ice on the ocean 's surface . <p> All these elements became a significant part of the final work , through the audio recordings I made as well as through more expressionist interpretations . One challenge was the strange , eerie silence of Antarctica when we were anchored offshore surrounded by slow moving white giants of ice . I struggled for a long time with how to recreate this feeling through sound , finally landing on crystal singing bowls .
