Depending on a cell 's purpose in the larger structure , contact or diffuse chemical signals direct it to its final destination . <p> The journey from egg and sperm to a fully grown body requires more than just multiplication . Plants , animals , and people are all made of trillions of cells , carefully organized into larger structures like tissues and organs . Slime molds are amoebas , but are similar in size and shape to human white blood cells and share the fundamental aspects of cell dynamics , such as migration and engulfment of disease-causing pathogens . <p> Individual cells of the slime mold Dictyostelium discoideum can exist independently , living freely in the soil and eating bacteria and fungi . When food is scarce , independent slime mold cells clump together and function as a multicellular organism . <p> When slime mold cells clump together , sometimes 100 cells , other times 10,000 cells , they differentiate into two distinct types . <p> The first type , pre-stalk cells , eventually forms a column that supports a sphere composed of the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ structure a fruiting body . The pre-stalk cells will die as the pre-spore cells eventually float off in the wind to a better environment where they can grow and divide again as independent amoebas . <p> Inside the clump , before the fruiting body takes shape , cells attach to form long trains and swirl around , immersed in a chemical signal that they secrete . First identified in the 1970s , this diffusive chemical , called cAMP , attracts cells . <p> Traditionally , the degree of attraction to cAMP signals was thought to separate the cells into pre-stalk and pre-spore cells . More recent genetic experiments revealed , however , that molecules related to adhesion , or cell-to-cell touch , may also be important . <p> Pre-stalk cells ( green ) and pre-spore cells ( magenta ) of Dictyostelium discoideum respond to physical or diffuse chemical signals while organizing themselves in a multicellular structure called the fruiting body .
