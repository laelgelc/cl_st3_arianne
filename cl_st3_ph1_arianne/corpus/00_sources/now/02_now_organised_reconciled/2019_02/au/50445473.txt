<p> THE small Hunter Valley town of Gloucester made international news this week , thanks to a legal verdict that that the winning side is hailing as the first time an Australian court has refused the consent of a coal mine on the basis of its contribution to climate change . The mine in question is Rocky Hill , which would have been a relatively small coking coal mine - producing coal for steelmaking rather than electricity generation - about five kilometres outside of the picturesque township . State planning authorities had already rejected Rocky Hill because of its physical impacts on Gloucester . In that light it was probably always -- as Justice Brian Preston observed in his decision -- a mine " in the wrong place " . However , it is Justice Preston 's description of Rocky Hill as being " at the wrong time " that is causing all of the celebration on one side , and all of the concern on the other . The case was initiated by Rocky Hill 's proponent , Gloucester Resources Limited , which was challenging the government 's refusal of the mine . Gloucester Groundswell argued Rocky Hill should be refused because its greenhouse emissions -- most of which come from the overseas use of its product -- would " adversely impact upon measures to limit dangerous anthropogenic climate change " . With the IPCC and other bodies urging increased action on climate change , the environmental movement has pushed with increasing vigour to have approval bodies consider the downstream or " scope 3 " emissions of greenhouse gas gases that are released by the combustion of the coal . Until now , the approvals process in NSW has generally sided with the coal companies . Last year , Justice Terry Sheehan in the Land and Environment Court rejected an application by the Wollar Progress Association , which had challenged coal company Peabody over an extension of its Wilpinjong mine , about 50 kilometres east of Gulgong . That case , too , looked at the extent approval authorities should consider downstream emissions in approving mining projects . With the environmental movement wanting to ensure the decision becomes a line in the sand , and with the industry trying not to appear too concerned about the situation , it will be instructive to see what happens next . On the industry side of the fence , top-end-of-town law firm Clayton Utz said on Tuesday that Rocky Hill meant there was now " an even stronger case now for legislative and policy changes to create a consistent approach to the assessment of coal mining developments nationwide " . The NSW Department of Planning 's major project register shows more than two dozen coal projects seeking approval . The EDO and activist groups including Lock the Gate have identified three proposed open-cut mines - Bylong in the Bylong Valley , Vickery near Gunnedah , and United Wambo near Singleton along with the Wallarah 2 underground on the Central Coast and the Hume underground near Berrima - as projects that should be assessed on the criteria used to reject Rocky Hill .
