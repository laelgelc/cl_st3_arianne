<p> Defeating a mining proposal on climate grounds involves clearing several high hurdles . <p> Generally speaking , the court must be convinced not only that the proposed mine would contribute to climate change , but also that this issue is relevant under the applicable law . <p> To do this , a litigant needs to convince a court of a few key things , which include that : <p> the proponent is responsible for the ultimate burning of the coal , even if it is burned by a third party , and <p> this will result in increased greenhouse emissions , which in turn contributes to climate change . <p> In his judgement , Judge Preston took a broad view and readily connected these causal dots , ruling that : <p> The project 's cumulative greenhouse gas emissions will contribute to the global total of GHG concentrations in the atmosphere . The global total of GHG concentrations will affect the climate system and cause climate change impacts . The project 's cumulative GHG emissions are therefore likely to contribute to the future changes to the climate system and the impacts of climate change . <p> Other courts ( @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ has successfully cleared various legal hurdles ) have tended to take a narrower approach to statutory interpretation , with climate change just one of numerous relevant factors under consideration . <p> In contrast , Judge Preston found climate change to be one of the more important factors to consider under NSW legislation . <p> To rule against a coalmine on climate grounds , the court also needs to resist the " market substitution " argument -- the suggestion that if the proponent does not mine and sell coal , someone else will . <p> This argument has become a common " defence " in climate litigation , and indeed was advanced by Gloucester Resources in the Rocky Hill case . <p> Judge Preston rejected the argument , describing it as " flawed " .
