<h> Solving the mystery of Serengeti 's vanishing wild dogs <p> IN 1991 , a strange thing happened in the wilds of Tanzania 's Serengeti National Park . <p> African wild dogs , Lycaon pictus , are not feral family pets , but an entirely separate species . <p> The Serengeti , a World Heritage Site , is home to a spectacular range of carnivores , from lions to cheetahs and more . And with more than 1.5 million zebras , wildebeests and gazelles making annual migrations across the park , there 's plenty of food for them to feast on . <p> One of the park 's lesser-known predators is the African wild dog , Lycaon pictus . These are not feral family pets , but an entirely separate species . Researchers started tracking wild dog numbers in 1964 , and over the years , have watched as the numbers of wild dogs in the park dwindled . <p> But in 1991 , the dogs simply disappeared from the Serengeti plains , and a huge scientific debate ensued . <p> Why did the wild dogs in the park suddenly go extinct ? <p> Blame the Researchers ? <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ up with a controversial hypothesis : it was researchers themselves who had led to the demise of the dogs . <p> The " Burrows hypothesis , " as it came to be called , suggested that the wild dogs were stressed when researchers immobilized them and put radio collars on them . <p> That stress caused the animals ' immune systems to be suppressed , the hypothesis suggested , which then allowed rabies lurking in their blood to eventually kill them . The hypothesis shook the conservation world , which has relied on tools like radio collars to track critically endangered , and often elusive , species . <p> Since Burrows first floated his controversial hypothesis , the debate has continued to rage . <p> " The implications of this hypothesis extend beyond wild dogs in the Serengeti , " said Craig Jackson , a researcher at the Norwegian Institute of Nature Research ( NINA ) who was first author of a new paper debunking the Burrows hypothesis .
