By Fonteh Akum for ISS TODAY <p> Since Gabon 's coup attempt on 7 January , President Ali Bongo Ondimba has been making changes to restore confidence in his government . Bongo , who was recovering from a stroke in Morocco at the time of the attempted coup , named a new cabinet under Prime Minister Julien Nkoghe Bekale . <p> He returned to Libreville to sit through the swearing-in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ reshuffled the cabinet again on 30 January -- this time in five ministerial departments . <p> Government spokesperson Nanette Longa said this new reshuffle was to " deepen the coherence of ministerial portfolios " . Youth activists were then intimidated and arrested under suspicion of being connected with the coup plotters . These moves showed the regime would brook no dissent . <p> Gabon will , however , need to do more to assuage spiralling sociopolitical discontent in the country . The president 's protracted absence is increasingly unsettling both the population and the governing elite . <p> The failed coup showed the depth of dissatisfaction with government , which appears to have reached the ranks of Gabon 's elite republican guard . Since a similar coup attempt was reversed by French paratroopers in 1964 , Gabon 's armed forces have largely stayed out of politics , despite securing the Bongo family 's hold on power . The Bongos have been in charge since 1967 . <p> Political stability in Gabon has been rattled by ongoing waves of sociopolitical unrest since 2015 . This has largely taken the form of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ student movements ( see graph ) . <p> Two events in the country 's recent past crystallised factions in the political class and fanned the flames of discontent . These are the battle for presidential succession following Omar Bongo 's death in 2009 , and the August 2016 presidential election that pitted Bongo 's son against his former foreign minister , Jean Ping . <p> The election cemented the divide between the traditionalist and the modernist factions of the ruling Gabonese Democratic Party . After succeeding his father , Bongo strengthened his control of the ruling party by marginalising and then ostracising the modernists who largely opposed dynastic succession and repression . Many were forced to either join the established opposition or form new political parties . <p> In a crowded field of 10 candidates , the interior minister declared Bongo the winner , with a little over 5,000 votes more than Ping , leader of the Union of Forces for Change opposition . The other eight candidates polled just over 7 000 votes in total . State institutions such as the Constitutional Court , the National Assembly and the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ incumbent , ensured his contested re-election . <p> Election observers highlighted irregularities including the highly questionable vote count in Bongo 's home Haut-Ogoou ? Province , which registered a 99.9% turn-out with 95.9% voting in favour of his re-election . <p> Nine candidates running against Bongo provided him with an apparent justification for victory by splitting the vote among his many rivals , in a one-round winner-takes-all election system . This despite a usually fractious opposition having coalesced around Ping , aware of the difficulty of unseating a president who succeeded his father to prolong 49 years of dynastic rule . <p> The post-electoral crisis led to a need for consensus in governing . However , Bongo preferred selective consensus building by drawing from the fringes of the opposition , to show evidence of outreach . Pierre-Claver Maganga Moussavou , the candidate who placed fifth , was appointed vice-president . <p> Gabon 's worrisome sociopolitical backdrop has been compounded by a flagging petro-dependent economy . Since the 2016 elections , Gabon 's economic outlook has n't improved much . The government has requested and received US$395.9 million from the International @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ macro-economic stability . <p> But fiscal stabilisation does n't necessarily address the socioeconomic and political challenges that resurface during protests . These include lack of access to jobs , diminishing social mobility and the deteriorating quality of health and education services . <p> Meanwhile , Gabon 's military expenses in 2017 , at $288-million , were the highest ever recorded . This as the country 's debt-to-GDP ratio rose to its highest in 10 years , at 66.5% . Gabon 's government chooses funding for security -- from both real and imagined threats -- over investment in real political and socioeconomic reforms , which could help address discontent . <p> To mend fences after the 2016 post-electoral crisis , Bongo convened national political talks -- the Angondj ? dialogue -- from March to May 2017 . The talks delivered only cosmetic changes to the four main agenda items -- institutional reform and consolidation of the rule of law , electoral reform , political modernisation , and national cohesion and peace consolidation . Some opposition parties participated , but Ping 's bloc boycotted the process , which was largely viewed as @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ flawed process . <p> The dialogue 's agenda also failed to address the elephant in the room -- a constitution without presidential term limits . Ping 's refusal to recognise Bongo 's electoral victory almost three years since the last election and four years to the next one sustains post-electoral discontent . <p> To avoid future electoral crises , institutional roadblocks must be removed . Since the attempted coup , government 's reaction to public protest has been security-based , intending to protect the regime rather than address the causes of discontent . This threatens stability and does n't bring any relief to the problems brought on by Gabon 's economic stagnation . <p> Gabon needs broad-based dialogue that delivers legitimate , accountable , responsive and depersonalised governance . Who will make the call ? With its president in long-term recovery , the power vacuum only lengthens the shadow of uncertainty .
