" <p> The survey started in 1971 as a review of commercially important fish like cod and halibut , but has grown into an annual scientific assessment of all sea life hauled up from the deep . <p> Last September , a coast guard vessel carried crew members and federal scientists through the area roughly between the tip of Quebec 's Gaspe Peninsula and the tip of Cape Breton Island , to probe an ecosystem that lives largely out of sight . <p> Trawls brought sea creatures to the surface every two hours , and scientists sorted , measured , and assessed the age and stomach contents of as many specimens as they could before starting all over again . <p> Rolland said the density of some fish species have dropped significantly , especially for flat fishes like cod or American plaice @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ loss in population compared to 20 years ago . <p> The complicated factors behind the change are many : climate change , fishing and predators like seals all have likely played a role , he said . <p> But as some populations have dwindled , others have moved in or bounced back in a big way . <p> Most groundfish species appear to be in a population decline , but Rolland said crustacean populations like lobster and crab populations are on a continual rise . <p> Small fish species like the tiny alligator fish -- named for its ridged back and likeness to the southern predator -- have appeared in higher numbers . <p> Others like tuna , striped bass and the red fish have seen an increase . <p> Fisheries and Oceans Canada say almost 50,000 fish and crustaceans were individually measured on the 2018 mission . <p> " The wet lab is not the prettiest place to be , " Rolland said . <p> " It 's smelly , it 's wet , there 's water everywhere ... it 's noisy , too , because we 're on @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ rough seas , too .
