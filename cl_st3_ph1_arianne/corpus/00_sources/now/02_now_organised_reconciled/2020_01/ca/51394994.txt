<h> Clear-cutting Ontario 's environmental laws <p> " The environment be damned " seems to pretty much sum up Doug Ford 's attitude toward Ontario 's natural legacy . Since he was elected premier , Ford has been on a mission to weaken the province 's environmental laws in ways likely to last well into the future . Dozens of regulations put in place by previous governments have been , or could be undone . Watersheds , forests , parks , farmland -- these and other natural riches once protected against unfettered corporate development are apparently now open for business . And Ford is mostly getting away with it . The quiet passage of key legislative changes under cover of omnibus bills serves him well . If we the public do n't know , then we wo n't protest . The restructuring of the province 's environmental laws also garners less attention than the government 's crisis-level cuts to everything from public health to daycare to education . Appointed by an all-party committee , the ECO was an impartial officer of the Ontario legislature charged with upholding the province 's Environmental Bill of Rights . The commissioner also reported on progress to fight climate change by reducing greenhouse gas emissions and improving energy conservation and efficiency . The Canadian Environmental Law Association strongly opposed the move and Ontario Nature called it a " misguided decision . " Another environmental group said it shows Ford is " clear-cutting Ontario 's environmental laws . " The province 's Environmental Assessment Act ( EAA ) is another casualty in the making . To " streamline " assessments for developers , the government has re-categorized what activities could be considered low risk to the environment . For example , the transfer of public land to private ownership is currently categorized as a medium-risk activity . The government 's proposal suggests it should be considered low risk . No environmental watchdogs can honestly argue the province 's environmental legislation does n't need improvement . Ontario has " the oldest environmental assessment legislation in Canada " that " falls short of its intended purpose , " said Auditor General Bonnie Lysyk in her 2016 report . Which is exactly what the government says it 's doing -- fixing what 's broken or weak by " modernizing " and " streamlining " and " improving efficiencies " and " cutting red tape . " But such euphemisms are feint attempts to disguise what many non-governmental watchdogs say is the government 's real agenda -- to make it easier for industry to exploit the province 's natural heritage regardless of the consequences to present and future generations .
