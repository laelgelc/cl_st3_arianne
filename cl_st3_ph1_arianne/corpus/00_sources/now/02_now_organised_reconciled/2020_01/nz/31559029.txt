<h> Australia bushfires : An account from smoke-filled Sydney <p> The Sydney restaurateur , hot and weary , grumbled his frustration . The white cloths over his troublingly empty outside tables were smeared with falling ash . He 'd replace them and a couple of hours later he 'd have to do it again . <p> Finally , he gave up and substituted ash-coloured paper for the cloth . <p> The smell of burning eucalypts from the scores of bush fires to the west , north and south of Sydney carried sweetly on the breeze , the smoke yet to invade the city . <p> We hoped it would n't . Two days later , the city awoke to a smell so acrid it seemed the fires were in the streets . The smoke had slunk in overnight and , by dawn , hung low and thick . Street lights switched on at mid-morning were a feeble yellow smudge in the haze . The birds vanished . <p> Even a short walk brought watery eyes and the clammy feeling of something foreign building in the throat . It seemed a mistake to linger outside as broadcasters passed on warnings @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ days , perhaps . <p> Suddenly , the emerald city 's acres of shady parks , soft sands and the bluest of bays looked and felt like a pollution-choked Asian metropolis . Jakarta , perhaps , or Delhi . Silvery bodies drifted through the haze , faces masked or wrapped with a scarf . <p> By afternoon , weather apps were mistaking ever denser smoke for clouds and cruelly forecasting rain when there was nothing to break our long drought . Office workers tumbled confused into streets when fire alarms were tricked by the smoke . The harbour 's buzzing ferries halted , fearing collisions . Even the great bridge that spans the harbour and the soaring Sydney Opera House roof were ghosts in the haze . <p> A 15-year-old daughter picked up from school said she felt flattened by the smoke and fearful of the fires building into mega-blazes to the north-west . She knows the climate is coming for her . <p> We should have taken more notice when , a year ago , a thin film of red , dried-out topsoil carried hundreds of miles from the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . It blows in almost daily now , on mean winds that rob farmers of their crumbling earth -- their most valuable possession in a drought that has taken everything else . <p> Over in the Blue Mountains , west of Sydney , the few farm animals left on the stretching plains cluster under the trees . Many animals have been sold for slaughter because the paddocks are dust bowls . The national sheep flock , we learn this week , is headed to its lowest level since 1904 . <p> Australia 's biggest inland rivers have dwindled into cesspools . The Government has sent people to pull the remaining fish out and move them to what healthier waters remain . Trucks rumble across roads far north and west of Sydney carting water to the growing numbers of small towns where reticulation systems have run dry . <p> The first restrictions on water use hit Sydney in mid-December . They 're mild enough for now but , we are warned , time limits on showers will be next . <p> One rogue bastard drought did it all , say the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ tougher action to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions . Others says climate change is behind the drought , dust , flames , smoke and ash . <p> No decent rain is forecast until at least May . <p> A hundred bush fires were raging across New South Wales in the run-in to Christmas . Australia has had its hottest days on record . Smoke shrouds Sydney again and the Government declared a seven-day state of emergency . <p> It 's going to be a fretful summer . <p> *After the publication of this article , the death toll in Australia 's bushfires this season rose to 23 , with six people still missing . <p> New Zealander Bernard Lagan is the Australian correspondent for the Times , London . <p> This article was first published in the January 11 , 2020 issue of the New Zealand Listener .
