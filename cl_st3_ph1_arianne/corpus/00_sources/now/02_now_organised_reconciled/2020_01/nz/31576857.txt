<h> NZ 's useful Huawei ambiguity <p> Boris Johnson must decide soon whether to stare down intense US pressure to block Huawei from Britain . Robert Ayson details how fellow Five Eyes partner New Zealand may be benefiting from the disagreement over exactly where it stands in this new ' Great Game ' pitting the world 's two super powers against each over the future of 5G . <p> Fresh from a thumping general election victory and an anti-climactic Commons vote confirming the U.K. 's departure from the European Union , Boris Johnson 's Government is struggling to make up its mind about Huawei . <p> Britain 's choice between telecommunications efficiency and network security has the makings of a transatlantic showdown . A team of Trump administration officials arrived in London recently to raise new security concerns . There have also been bad tidings about the future of the special relationship , with warnings that the UK 's intelligence links with United States could suffer if Huawei becomes part of Britain 's 5G mix . <p> But I 've also been struck by the way New Zealand 's positioning is mentioned , if ever so briefly , in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ that New Zealand has banned Huawei lives on in a recent report in my favourite newspaper , The Washington Post . In case you fiercely object to my reading choices , a similar judgment about New Zealand 's approach to Huawei can be found in Sputnik . <p> These views echo the widespread reaction in late 2018 to the news that New Zealand had said ' no ' to Spark 's proposal to include Huawei in its initial 5G bid . At that moment it was almost too easy to conclude that New Zealand had joined a small group of security partners who had issued a ban . After all , America was already encouraging its fives eyes partners to eschew Huawei , and Australia , New Zealand 's close ally , had already taken a strong position in that vein , which was reinforced after Scott Morrison became Prime Minister . <p> But New Zealand had not taken an all @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ catchy title of " GCSB Statement " made no specific mention of Huawei . " Having more on the public record about these specific risks would have been a good thing , but the responsible Minister Andrew Little was adamant that this was not a vendor-specific decision ( something the US now wants from the UK ) , and that the GCSB was simply doing its job under the legislation it works to . <p> " We would never ban a particular company or a particular country " , Little told the New Zealand Herald , ' That 's not the way it works . " <p> The Prime Minister would also lend her voice to this clarification . By the end of 2018 it was clear that China 's unhappiness over Huawei was a leading feature in a more strained bilateral relationship , and we are talking about New Zealand 's leading trade partner here . In another television interview , Ardern left open the possibility that with the right " mitigation " of the risks identified by the GCSB , there might still be a chance for Huawei to have some role in the country 's 5G architecture . <p> In dealings with China and the telecommunications industry this technically correct version of events may be the better message for New Zealand to emphasise . Officials can presumably say that despite what you may sometimes read and hear , Wellington is not closed for business with China 's telecom giants . <p> But on other occasions it may suit the Ardern Government if it is believed that New Zealand has banned Huawei .
