<h> Suleimani 's killing brings anxiety to an already-anxious world <p> There was enough tension in the air already before a US drone took out Iran 's top soldier , Andrew Anthony writes from London . <p> We live in anxious times . Not because the world is a more dangerous place -- it 's arguably less dangerous than it has ever been -- but because people perceive themselves to be under growing threat . Although it may be a global condition , this existential dread is particularly rife in the UK . <p> It 's said that more than eight million Britons suffer from some kind of anxiety disorder . This year 's games in the third round of the FA Cup -- a traditional landmark in the football calendar -- were delayed by a minute to raise awareness of mental well-being . <p> Although it was no doubt a good idea , it 's hard to believe that there is much more awareness to be raised . These days it 's a rare celebrity who has n't shared his or her mental-health struggles , depressions , phobias and anxieties . <p> Even the royal family -- once @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the confessional spirit of the times . Princes William and Harry have both spoken out about their psychological troubles . And their uncle Andrew , the Duke of York , tried to gain public sympathy with talk of his post-Falklands War stress -- though so disastrous was his TV interview last year , in which he attempted to deny accusations of sexual predation , that he is probably more anxious now than he 's ever been . <p> The nagging background hum is the tension of Brexit , worry about climate change ( the Australian bush fires have featured like a preview of the coming apocalypse on TV news ) , terrorism , growing inequality and an increasingly complex , algorithm-driven world . Why these developments should prove more stressful than past challenges such as , for example , two world wars and the threat of nuclear extinction is something of a mystery . <p> Whatever the reason , there is a strong sense of impending catastrophe doing the rounds over here . Things came to a head three days into the new decade when it was announced that Iranian @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ a US drone . Within hours , the phrases " Franz Ferdinand " and " World War III " were trending on Twitter . Never let it be said that the British " keep calm and carry on " , to quote the old Blitz mantra , when there 's an opportunity to freak out . <p> Had they been asked the day before he was killed who Suleimani was , most Brits would have shaken their heads in bafflement . But the moment the mastermind behind Iran 's proxy militias in Lebanon , Syria and Iraq met his maker , everyone was suddenly a nervous expert on Middle Eastern geopolitics . <p> The outgoing Leader of the Opposition , Jeremy Corbyn , wrote an impassioned letter to Prime Minister Boris Johnson demanding that he voice his objection to the " assassination " of Suleimani . As Corbyn used to be a regular on Iran 's propaganda channel , Press TV , and is on record as referring to one of Suleimani 's proxies -- Hizbollah -- as his " friends " , it was no surprise that he spoke @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ with the Iranian regime to protest the mass slaughter in Syria that Suleimani presided over . <p> It goes without saying that none of us can predict the future . But you do n't have to channel Nostradamus to suggest that , in the short term , Suleimani 's death is likely to raise tensions in an already very tense part of the world . That said , he was no Franz Ferdinand , and the plight of the early 20th-century Austro-Hungarian Empire has little in common with Iran 's current predicament . <p> None of that , alas , will stop the anxiety from spreading . On the plus side , it 's not just arms dealers who will make a killing .
