<h> Deja vu over political response to bushfires <p> Observing political reactions to Australia 's bushfire crisis brings to mind decades of obfuscation over climate change policy , says Marc Hudson . And time is running out for history to keep repeating itself . <p> As someone who has studied Australian climate policy and politics closely , this summer 's bushfire crisis have been both heartbreaking and bewildering . <p> The grave warnings politicians ignored for so long have now come to pass . <p> The fires may be without precedent , but these dark weeks have also brought an overwhelming sense of d ? j ? vu . <p> It 's hard to believe , but the Morrison government 's fumbling response to the fires and the broader climate crisis is in many ways history repeating . <p> From the disastrous optics of Prime Minister Scott Morrison 's trip to Hawaii to blaming conservationists for the fires , our politicians keep making the same blunders and rolling out the same failed strategies . <p> Prime Minister Scott Morrison was savaged by critics for refusing to meet former fire chiefs.AAP <p> Here are five recurring themes in Australian politics @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ 1 . Blaming ' greenies ' <p> As the fire season ramped up in November last year , New South Wales Nationals leader John Barilaro accused the Greens of preventing governments from conducting hazard reduction burning , implying the party should shoulder blame for the fires . <p> " We 've got to do better and I know that we do n't do enough hazard reduction ... because of the ideological position from the Greens , " he said . <p> Nationals leader Michael McCormack , pictured in Question Time , has ridiculed those making a link between climate change and bushfires.Lukas Coch/AAP <h> 3 . Experts ignored by politicians <p> Since April last year , former fire chiefs have implored the Morrison government to act on climate change and better prepare the nation for extreme fire seasons ahead . The government would not meet the experts to hear the advice , let alone implement it . <p> Successive governments have form when it comes to ignoring experts on climate matters . In September 1994 the CSIRO 's then top climate scientist , Graeme Pearman , briefed the Labor government @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ as a debate over whether to institute a carbon tax heated up . Leaders not fronting up <p> Morrison 's decision to take a family holiday in Hawaii as the bushfire crisis grew lost him serious political skin . <p> Some argue , rightly , that symbolism is less important than substance , and so Morrison 's trip is itself irrelevant . But symbolism creates or destroys both morale , and the possibility of stronger political action . <p> In 1992 newly minted Labor prime minister Paul Keating sent environment minister Ros Kelly to the Rio Earth Summit , prompting one journalist to observe he was " preoccupied with winning the upcoming election ( and ) said he was n't going all the way to Rio to give a six-minute speech " . <p> It made Australia the only OECD nation not represented by its head of state , and sent the message that Australia was not taking a serious approach @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ jobs , jobs ' mantra <p> The Bureau of Meteorology this week confirmed this season 's horror bushfire crisis is linked to climate change . Planetary warming is clearly a threat to the nation 's economic well-being . <p> However Australian governments have routinely created a false dichotomy between environmental protection and jobs . Most recently , we 've seen it in the Coalition government 's support for the Adani coal mine in central Queensland , and its repeated mantra of " jobs jobs jobs " . <p> The strategy has been used before . After the Franklin Dam fight in 1983 , concern over environmental issues entered the political mainstream . But as former Labor science minister Barry Jones said later , that changed in 1991 when economic recession hit . <p> " Jobs , jobs , jobs became the priority and in some quarters there was a cynical reaction suggesting that environmental issues were luxuries which characterised affluent times ...
