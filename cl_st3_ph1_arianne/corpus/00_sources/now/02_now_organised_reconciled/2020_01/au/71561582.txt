<h> Pursuit home <h> How Australian wildlife live with bushfires - but the price can be high <p> The massive scale of these bushfires heightens the risk to animal populations and species - and as climate change bites the onus will increasingly be on us to protect and foster our wildlife <p> By Professor Alan York , University of Melbourne <p> Share selection to : <p> Distressing scenes of kangaroos fleeing amid flames , of koalas with singed fur and burnt feet and of animals burnt to death have Australians and people around the world worrying over how devastating the current bushfires will be for our unique wildlife . <p> Australia has a long evolutionary history of fire , and its plants and animals have developed many strategies to cope with occasional fire events . Plants , for example , might resprout from structures below ground or be protected by thick bark , or be able to regenerate from seed stored in the soil or protected by woody capsules . <p> But animals have to rely on their mobility . Within the overall burnt area , there are patches that are less affected , like moist gullies and in rocky terrain , and these patches can often provide refuges for animals to shelter in . <p> After the fire , as the bush recovers , animals can move out to find new areas to live or take advantage of the new food resources in the recovering landscape , like new vegetation that can be very palatable and has yet to develop defences . Burnt areas can also leave many seeds on the ground and predators will benefit from prey having less cover . Animals in other areas of the landscape not directly affected can also move in to take advantage of these new resources . <p> But the massive extent of these current fires means the impact on wildlife will likely be worse than what @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ highly likely that there will still be unburnt refuges within individual fires , although how many animals have survived is unknown . Of greatest concern is the lack of large unburnt areas to provide a source of animals to recolonise the regenerating landscape . <p> The fires have also been so extensive that many populations of particular species will have been severely reduced and will take many years to recover . <p> We also know that when they lose vegetation to shelter in many small mammals , like bandicoots , the agile antechinus and the bush rat , will face a heightened risk from introduced predators like foxes . Recent research has demonstrated that these predators are often attracted to recently burnt areas . <p> So , will any species be in danger of extinction ? <p> It is inevitable that there will be reductions in populations in some parts of the landscape and some local extinctions ; this is not unusual during bushfires . <p> Species at greatest risk of total extinction are those with small , isolated populations , like the visibly spectacular and critically endangered Southern Corroboree @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ in the wild , with their home in the Snowy Mountains , which is threatened by several converging fires . <p> Fortunately , we have an insurance policy , with captive breeding programs at several institutions in NSW and Victoria . <p> The future survival of this and other rare iconic species may depend on future reintroductions once their fragile habitat has recovered . <p> Increasing habitat loss and fragmentation due to land clearing and urban expansion in eastern Australia has meant that animal populations are increasingly becoming isolated from each other .
