<h> Climate change and financial risk <p> Climate change is already a reality . Ever-more-ferocious cyclones and extended droughts lead to the destruction of infrastructure and the disruption of livelihoods and contribute to mass migration . <p> Actions to combat rising temperatures , inadequate though they may have been so far , have the potential to drive dislocation in the business world as fossil fuel giants awaken to the need for renewable sources of energy and automakers accelerate investments in cleaner vehicles . <p> But measuring economic costs of climate change remains a work in progress . We can assess the immediate costs of changing weather patterns and more frequent and intense natural disasters , but most of the potential costs lie beyond the horizon of the typical economic analysis . <p> The economic impact of climate change will likely accelerate , though not smoothly . Crucially for the coming generations , the extent of the damage will depend on policy choices that we make today . <p> Policymakers and investors increasingly recognize climate change 's important implications for the financial sector . Lower- and middle-income economies are typically more vulnerable to physical risks . <p> For financial institutions , physical risks can materialize directly , through their exposures to corporations , households , and countries that experience climate shocks , or indirectly , through the effects of climate change on the wider economy and feedback effects within the financial system . <p> Exposures manifest themselves through increased default risk of loan portfolios or lower values of assets . For example , rising sea levels and a higher incidence of extreme weather events can cause losses for homeowners and diminish property values , leading to greater risks in mortgage portfolios . As a result , insurance is likely to become more expensive or even unavailable in at-risk areas of the world . <p> Climate change can make banks , insurers , and reinsurers less diversified , because it can increase the likelihood or impact of events previously considered uncorrelated , such as droughts and floods . <p> Transition risks materialize on the asset side of financial institutions , which could incur losses on exposure to firms with business models not built around the economics of low carbon emissions . <p> Fossil fuel companies could find themselves saddled with reserves that are , in the words of Bank of England Governor Mark Carney ( 2015 ) , " literally unburnable " @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ <p> These firms could see their earnings decline , businesses disrupted , and funding costs increase because of policy action , technological change , and consumer and investor demands for alignment with policies to tackle climate change . <p> Coal producers , for example , already grapple with new or expected policies curbing carbon emissions , and a number of large banks have pledged not to provide financing for new coal facilities . <p> The share prices of US coal mining companies reflect this " carbon discount " as well as higher financing costs and have been underperforming relative to those of companies holding clean energy assets . <p> Risks can also materialize through the economy at large , especially if the shift to a low-carbon economy proves abrupt ( as a consequence of prior inaction ) , poorly designed , or difficult to coordinate globally ( with consequent disruptions to international trade ) . <p> Financial stability concerns arise when asset prices adjust rapidly to reflect unexpected realizations of transition or physical risks . For example , the Network of Central Banks and Supervisors for Greening the Financial System ( NGFS ) , an expanding group that currently comprises 42 members , has embarked on the task of integrating climate-related risks into supervision and financial stability monitoring . <p> Given the large shifts in asset prices and catastrophic weather-related losses that climate change may cause , prudential policies should adapt to recognize systemic climate risk -- for example , by requiring financial institutions to incorporate climate risk scenarios into their stress tests . <p> In the United Kingdom , prudential regulators have incorporated climate change scenarios into stress tests of insurance firms that cover both physical and transition risks . <p> Efforts to incorporate climate-related risks into regulatory frameworks face important challenges , however .
