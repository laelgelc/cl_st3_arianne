<p> In September 2019 , the editor of The Conversation , Misha Ketchell , declared The Conversation 's editorial team in Australia was henceforth taking what he called a " zero-tolerance " approach to climate change deniers and skeptics . Their comments would be blocked and their accounts locked . <p> His reasons were succinct : " Climate change deniers and those shamelessly peddling pseudoscience and misinformation are perpetuating ideas that will ultimately destroy the planet . " <p> From the standpoint of conventional media ethics , it was a dramatic , even shocking , decision . It seemed to violate journalism 's principle of impartiality -- that all sides of a story should be told so audiences could make up their own minds . <p> But in the era of climate change , this conventional approach is out of date . A more analytical approach is called for . <p> The ABC 's editorial policy on impartiality offers the best analytical approach so far developed in Australia . John Stuart Mill , in his seminal work , On Liberty , published in 1859 , was a robust advocate for free speech but he drew the line at harm : " ... the only purpose for which power can be exercised over any member of a civilized community , against his will , is to prevent harm to others . " <p> It follows that editors may exercise the power of refusing to publish climate-denialist material if doing so prevents harm to others , without violating fundamental free-speech principles . <p> Other harms too provide established grounds for limiting free speech .
