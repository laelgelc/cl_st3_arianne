<p> The White House is preparing to take the first real stab in four decades at overhauling a bedrock environmental law with tentacles that reach throughout the federal government . <p> Mary Neumayr , the Capitol Hill and executive branch veteran who now heads the Council on Environmental Quality , is scheduled later this week to unveil a new National Environmental Policy Act regulatory proposal , after President Trump telegraphed the move on New Year 's Day . <p> That statute requires U.S. agencies to conduct environmental-impact assessments of development projects ranging from cell towers to natural-gas pipelines and highways . <p> The environmental community is already up in arms and ready to pounce , contending that the new regulation is likely to marginalize the climate-change and greenhouse-gas-emission impacts of the myriad projects annually approved by federal agencies . The U.S. Chamber of Commerce , the American Petroleum Institute , and other big-business associations are championing the effort as a way to expedite project approvals . <p> But aside from battling against Democrats and environmental firebrands off Capitol Hill , the White House is set to face off against a potentially more formidable adversary : a robust body of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ environmental , public health , and other groups have taken to the courts to argue that agencies fell short of conducting enough climate and greenhouse-gas analysis to comport with the NEPA statute . Despite earlier warnings , the modern climate-change movement arguably did n't first take shape until former NASA scientist James Hansen spelled out the possibility of rapidly warming temperatures during testimony on Capitol Hill in 1988 . <p> NEPA is ambitious , if nebulous . The law calls for regulatory policy to " create and maintain conditions under which man and nature can exist in productive harmony , and fulfill the social , economic , and other requirements of present and future generations of Americans .
