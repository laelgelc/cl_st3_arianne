As dispassionate outsiders we may think it is an easy choice to make because if your life is at potential risk who would not chose safety ? But it 's a much more difficult decision than whether or not simply to leave , it 's a series of very complex and uncertain judgements . No matter how bad the fires may seem , most of us would hold onto the belief that we might be lucky , that the fire might take a different direction , that it might rain , that help might arrive or for any other number of reasons our house might survive . The research evidence overwhelmingly shows that when we are under pressure our capacity to make rational choices is impaired and this means that we make bad decisions . Being given a short time to quit your home is about as stressful as it gets and that means it is fantastically difficult to remember what is important before we even decide whether or not to leave it behind . This means that even as the immediate stress of packing and departing begins to subside the first signs of longer-term psychological trauma may begin to emerge as each victim runs the choices through their minds and questions whether or not they did the right thing . In the current situation it is a cruel irony that towns and villages tend to be built on slopes or hill tops to avoid flooding , yet a slope can mean that a bushfire moves at 30 plus miles an hour or even faster in a strong breeze . Many householders who wait to the last moment to leave have failed to realise just how quickly their escape route may be blocked by flames . Those who have lost loved ones will also suffer the agonising reflections upon what they might have done differently and whether they might have helped or saved someone . All human societies have evolved ceremonies and social rituals to deal with grief , but while the fires continue there must be other priorities and for many this means they will be prevented from expressing these very deep emotions associated with loss . Pain denied can often turn inward and as loss is often accompanied by guilt many survivors will blame themselves for things that were not really their fault . This is why easy access to counselling and support services is so important and why the Australians have placed such a heavy emphasis on deploying health professionals to all of the refugee centres . <p> We all appreciate how stressful it must be to flee from the flames and this should make us even more aware of the deep courage it needs to walk into the fire @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ beat back the inferno are displaying bravery of the very highest order through the most amazing self-control and discipline . There are few things more frightening than fire so to willingly confront the full fury of a furnace that has no limits or constraints takes a very special kind of person . However , even the most heroic firefighter is still a human and the fearlessness of today may have psychological consequences tomorrow . Some psychologists view courage almost like a ginger root -- you can keeping cutting away at it , but providing you leave some behind it will grow back again . This means that resilient people can often recover from the most demanding of circumstances , but it also means that they do not have an infinite capacity and that they need time to recuperate . The more bravery that is consumed the longer it will take to regenerate , and if it is not given sufficient time then eventually it will be all used up . The pressures of constant stress and fatigue as days drag into weeks can significantly increase the rate at which people begin to lose their robustness so those who usually appear to be calm and collected in the most trying of circumstances may suddenly find it impossible to deal with even small shocks . This is why it is so important to monitor the mental health of all those engaged in fighting these terrible fires because increasing pressure leads to tiredness which in turn leads to mistakes which may in turn cause more pressure or even serious accidents . The Australian authorities will be aware of these concerns and are undoubtedly monitoring the situation . However , it can be very difficult to assess psychological health in a social group that is likely to place a high value on appearing to be tough . How would you feel if you went back home only to find a pile of cinders and blackened rubble ? For many the shock of seeing the destruction will lead to immediate pain that then develops into depression . The evidence shows that for many people the initial elation at surviving a natural disaster is soon replaced by deep unhappiness as they realise the enormity of what has been lost . Some become almost paralysed by a sense of hopelessness at the difficulty of rebuilding and recovering from the disaster , while others throw themselves into the work of reconstruction . All will want to share their experiences and it is important that communities come together to make sense of what has happened to them and to draw upon their collective strength as they try to remake that which was lost . Even those who appear to have coped well with the tragedy may be more emotional or volatile than usual . Many will express their psychological discomfort by being easily upset or becoming angry at the slightest provocation ; many will want to seek security in physical contact through hugs , kisses , or simply being close to others . In the short to medium term the drive to clear up and rebuild will be a welcome distraction , and all the busyness will mean that many people appear to be fine . However , even the toughest may be psychologically fragile for many months or years to come and in many cases the stresses of the emergency may only emerge in the future . Nowadays we are all much more aware of post-traumatic stress disorder ( PTSD ) , but many sufferers find it difficult to admit that they have a problem , especially if the nightmares , flashbacks and anxiety attacks only manifest themselves months or even years after the events . When the fires have been conquered many of those who have taken part will justifiable experience a great elation and joy at a job well done . However , this happiness may quickly be replaced by a sense of anti-climax , almost a feeling of emptiness ; boredom may shade into depression , and there will also be the ever-present possibility of PTSD . People who have been in dangerous situations will often reflect upon the experience and think through how they might respond if confronted with similar dangers in the future . There will also come a day when the reconstruction is completed and what appears to be normality returns to all of the towns and villages that have been ravaged .
