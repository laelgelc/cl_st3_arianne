<h> The price of water offers real opportunity for New Zealand <p> Governments should realise the increasing value of water - it is a commodity , like oil , writes Roger Hanson <p> A textbook example of how to manage the resources of a country is given by Norway , a country with a similar population to New Zealand but much wealthier . <p> Norway is the world 's third richest country - New Zealand is 20th . Norway 's wealth stems from their huge gas resources , but what sets Norway apart is their management of income derived from that resource . <p> The first big hydrocarbon find in the North Sea was made by BP in the British sector in 1965 . This was followed by many major finds in the coming decades . <p> The British economy , particularly in the 70s was in dire straits and to help fill the yawning balance of payments gap generated by the huge increase in social spending , government policy was to extract hydrocarbons as fast as possible to acquire the funds to pour into state expenditure . <p> Norway @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the Ekofisk field in 1969 . In 1972 the Norwegian government , with astonishing wisdom , agreed on a list of " 10 Oil Commandments " stating how the hydrocarbon resource was to be managed from both business and environmental aspects . <p> The idea was to avoid a desperate " dash for cash " but instead to optimise Norwegian involvement in the design and construction of the oil rigs . <p> The Norwegians ensured that the pace of exploration and development was slow enough that the country 's limited labour and business resources could be fully utilised . <p> There were some hiccups , such as the 1973 oil crisis and an uncontrolled gas blowout on the Ekofisk Bravo platform in 1977 , after which the Norwegians instigated some of the toughest environmental legislation in the world . <p> By 1990 the government coffers were filling nicely . <p> With oil price fluctuations in mind , the government decided to set up the Government Pension Fund-Global , also known as the Oil Fund . The idea was to invest surplus revenues from hydrocarbon royalties in a fund that could @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ generations when hydrocarbons were long gone . <p> The generic name for this type of investment is a Sovereign Wealth Fund . By 2017 Norway 's Oil Fund had become one of the biggest in the world , worth over one trillion US dollars . <p> New Zealand could become the Norway of the south because New Zealand 's geographic location puts it in a great position to benefit from a new resource boom -- fresh water . <p> The UN has stated that by 2030 , the global demand for fresh water will exceed supply by 40 per cent ; that means the price of water is set to increase significantly . It looks like Cape Town will narrowly avoid being the first city in the world to completely run out of water , but cities like Cape Town are the canaries in the mine . <p> A BBC report last month listed major cities , like Cape Town , most likely to run out of drinking water . They are : Sao Paolo , Bangalore , Beijing , Cairo , Jakarta , Moscow , Istanbul , Mexico City @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ include : huge demand through increasing population , climate change , drought and poor water management . Beijing , for example , ca n't access 40 per cent of its surface water because it is polluted and London loses 25 per cent of its water through leaks in its distribution system . Global warming means , worldwide , that evaporation rates will increase and crops will require increased watering . <p> New Zealand is in the enviable position of being in water surplus . Governments should realise the increasing value of water - it is a commodity , like oil . <p> No country gives their oil away . In 2008 , during a severe drought , Barcelona paid 22 million euros for 18 shiploads of water , six per month for three months . <p> There is surely a strong case for New Zealand to sell and tax any water exported . These monies could be deposited in a Sovereign Wealth Fund which , in the coming decades , would be worth billions .
