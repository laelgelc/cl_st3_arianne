<h> Ozone loss may have caused mass extinction <p> Californian scientists have found a new way to account for extinction and to explain mass murder on a planetary scale . <p> Seven out of 10 land animals perished at the end of the Permian , 252 million years ago . So did 95% of marine species . And the deadly factor at work may have been the destruction of atmospheric ozone , the protective screen in the stratosphere that eliminates harmful ultraviolet light . <p> Jeffrey Benca of the University of California Berkeley and colleagues report in the journal Science Advances that they irradiated a series of dwarf pines with doses of ultraviolet-B radiation up to 13 times stronger than any on Earth today . <p> They used 60 pines of the species Pinus mugo , irradiated them for 56 days , and then spent three years examining 57,000 pollen grains produced over that period . <p> UV-B wavelengths are associated with mutations in DNA , the inheritance mechanism of all life on Earth . Their cones shrivelled within days of emerging . Once restored to present day , open air conditions , the pines all recovered . <p> Plants underwrite all animal life : repeated bouts of forest sterility could , researchers think , have played a role in the collapse of the planet 's biosphere . <p> Research like this is at the heart of climate science : it is a tenet of earth sciences that the present is key to the past . So it follows that what happened in the past could be relevant to the present . <p> And since biologists have argued that the double punch of habitat destruction and climate change could be precipitating a sixth great extinction , there has always been intense interest in the triggers of the previous five .
