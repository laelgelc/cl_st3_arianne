" In the world we have engineered and built , Carson wrote , we " often forget the true nature of ( our ) planet and the vistas of its history , in which the existence of ( humankind ) has occupied a mere moment of time . " Even so , all roads lead back the ocean , and a reminder of our place in the order of things . <p> This is the sense one gets while reading Jeff Goodell 's new book , " The Water Will Come . " Not only do we return to the sea , as Carson suggested , but the sea returns to us , rising faster as the effects of climate change are felt on our shores . One of the book 's strengths is the way Goodell tells the stories of people who are already being affected by rising seas . <p> The climate change he warns us about is not projected onto some distant future ; it is happening here and now , and Goodell means to show it to us . Nowhere is this story brought home more forcefully than in Miami . <p> Miami is the only city to which Goodell returns . Two of the book 's 12 chapters are centered there since the city encapsulates the precariousness of the current moment . While developers continue to build multi-million-dollar condos at the shoreline , scientists predict that much of Miami will be inundated by rising seas in the near future . Goodell speaks to city officials , architects , developers and residents as he seeks to understand how people are responding to the threat of rising water around them . <p> In Miami , the problem is particularly severe as water comes at residents from above in severe rainbursts , from beside in rising tides and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ geology already saturated with saltwater . The deeper challenge is behavioral . " The real x factor here , " he writes , " is not the vagaries of climate science , but the complexity of human psychology . At what point will we take dramatic action to cut CO2 pollution ? " <p> This is the question that haunts the book and every story in it .
