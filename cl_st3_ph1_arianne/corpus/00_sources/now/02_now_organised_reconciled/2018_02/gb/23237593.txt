<p> For most people , a beef-eating environmentalist is a contradiction . Ask climate experts what they think about meat and they will tell you that we should be eating a lot less of it . The United Nations has calculated that animal farming is responsible for 14.5 per cent of all greenhouse gas emissions and red meat and dairy production accounts for well over half of that ( 65 per cent ) . <p> However , there is a small and growing community of farmers , scientists and academics who think rearing livestock and eating more , or at least maintaining , the amount of red meat we consume is part of the solution to climate change . <p> They argue that well-managed livestock are the key to producing healthier soils that can take carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere and store them in the ground . <p> The trick is to get livestock to copy the movements of wild herding animals in nature . This is where the problems start because farmed animals , unlike their wild counterparts , can be much more selective about what they eat , grazing hardest on the plants they like best . Over time , the cow 's favourite grass is overgrazed , becoming so weak that other weeds begin to push it out . <p> To get rid of the weeds and get the good grass to grow , the farmer resorts to chemical fertilisers . And because the grass is less nutritious than conventional systems , the farmer has to add grain to the cattle 's diet to keep them producing quality milk after calving or to fatten them up before they go to slaughter . <p> It is this type of industrial animal farming that attracts widespread damnation from environmentalists . <p> Rotating the herd to allow pastures to recover for longer creates a carbon storing process in the soil <p> The farmer will put their cows out to graze on a tiny acre of grass for one day , forcing them to eat everything on the ground , not just their favourite grass . Nicolette Niman , an American rancher and environmental lawyer , argues in her book Defending Beef that " cattle are not , in fact , a climate problem at all ; instead cattle are actually amongst the most practical , cost-effective solutions to the warming of the planet " . <p> But can cows actually reverse climate change ? <p> One wildlife ecologist thinks they can . Allan Savory has spent decades advocating the use of mob-grazing techniques to tackle desertification in Africa . His theory goes against conventional wisdom , arguing that rather than reducing the numbers of livestock on grasslands , we should be increasing them . <p> In a Ted Talk that has since garnered over four million views , Savory says that managing livestock on rotation can take enough carbon out of the atmosphere to return us to pre-industrial levels .
