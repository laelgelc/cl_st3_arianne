If that forest disappears , experts say much of that carbon could wind up in the atmosphere and warm the planet . <p> On Tuesday , the Forest Service revealed a plan that , if enacted next year , could let developers build roads and chop down trees in more than half of the Tongass National Forest in southeastern Alaska . The 17-million acre forest , which is bigger than the entire state of West Virginia , has been protected from development since 2001 . It 's also remarkably biodiverse and home to robust populations of salmon , bears , moose , and deer . <p> " Logging not only reduces the forest 's ability to store carbon and the number of trees , it disrupts the soil badly , " Brian Buma @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ University of Colorado Denver , told VICE News . " That can impact salmon streams . " <p> The Tongass is particularly adept at keeping carbon out of the atmosphere because its plant life is so dense . The forest and all its biomass hold 3 gigatons -- or 3 billion metric tons -- of carbon , more than any other U.S. forest . In fact , that 's as much as all of the forests in Oregon and Northern California combined , according to Bev Law , a professor researching global climate change biology at Oregon State University . <p> " Storing carbon in the forests is low-hanging fruit , just letting it stay there , because carbon in the forest is carbon that 's not in the atmosphere , " Law said . <p> In his final days of office , President Bill Clinton enacted the " roadless rule , " which protects 58.5 million acres of land in the U.S. forest system across 38 states from development , including the Tongass .
