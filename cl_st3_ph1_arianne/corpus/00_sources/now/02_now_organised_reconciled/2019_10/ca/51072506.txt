<p> It seemed everyone and their uncle were talking about brownface/blackface this past week . Although the news cycle is now starting to move on , as a person of colour -- brown to be exact -- I still have a few things I would like to add to the conversation . <p> When I initially saw the now-famous images of Justin Trudeau in costume , his skin darkened , it reminded me of being called dirty as child , because of my brown skin . Today , I would like to think that our society has evolved toward greater awareness of how racist and how hurtful such remarks are . <p> Appearing in brownface or blackface is unequivocally wrong , but the incidents happened 19 years ago , and earlier . Trudeau very quickly apologized for his actions and took full ownership of what he had done . Still , the outraged reaction continued , although it was not members of visible minorities who were the most upset . If that translates to more non-visible minorities acting as allies toward those from marginalized communities , great . We welcome it with open arms . <p> Rather than directing @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ focus it on the present . Let 's turn the outrage into action that will help improve the quality of life and opportunities afforded to visible minorities in our country . Even if much has improved , discrimination continues . <h> Related <p> Let 's focus on diversity and inclusion in positions of authority . I can assure you that visible minorities care far more about being afforded better opportunities , being treated well , not being discriminated based on the colour of their skin and how these factors affect their children and grandchildren than how their prime minister dressed at a costume party long before he was in office . <p> If we 're going to judge a person , let it be on their track record and actions now . <p> Democracy is supposed to be about deciding who the best person is to represent the interests of the masses by assessing candidates ' merit , not through embarrassing or tearing down the merit of others . We are Canadian , after all ; we are polite , we play fair . It 's worth noting the timing @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ that this story was first brought to light by Time magazine , an American publication ; it 's hard to escape the conclusion there was a political motivation behind it . These photos were then amplified in the Canadian media . <p> I am focused on election coverage , perhaps more so than in previous elections . I am the Mom of a politically aware visible minority teenager who will be voting in a few years ; this has made me increasingly aware of the ramifications of smear campaigns and attack ads . What message are we sending our future generations by highlighting our opponents ' mistakes in the distant past ? <p> Canada faces many enormous issues , including oil pipelines , Indigenous issues , Quebec 's removal of certain employment rights from visible religious minorities , immigration , and job creation in an increasingly competitive world . We need to stay focused on these and other issues that will shape our future . <p> Our visible-minority communities care about representation in positions of authority so that our children can be inspired to dream big dreams . We care @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ at the table . Diversity is about being invited to a party . Inclusion means being asked to dance . <p> There are many issues worth engaging on , including climate change and health care . Here 's what not to fight about : distracting images from almost 20 years ago . But let 's take the energy they generated and channel it where it matters , to improving Canada moving forward , and making our voices heard at the ballot box on Oct. 21 . I 'll be there , with my naturally brown face and all . <p> Fariha Naqvi-Mohamed is the founder and editor in chief of CanadianMomEh.com , a lifestyle blog.
