<p> Every fall , I take my environmental studies class camping at Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore on Lake Michigan . Some years the beach extends more than three metres to the water . This year , in many spots , there was no beach at all . <p> The story is the same throughout the Great Lakes . During my summer research trip to Lake Ontario and the St. Lawrence River , I lost track of the number of submerged docks and buildings ; swimming near the shore of Lake Huron was a bad idea because of the high risk of electrocution from inundated boathouses that still had the juice flowing . <p> Water levels in the Great Lakes have always fluctuated . But climate change is throwing past patterns out of whack . Almost every Great Lake reached record levels in 2019 . Instead of a gradual rise and fall , the lakes are going from extreme to extreme . <p> For example , Lakes Michigan and Huron hit record lows in 2013 , and docks on Georgian Bay did n't reach the water . To compensate , the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers proposed putting riffles , basically water speed bumps , on the bottom of Lake Huron 's outflow at the St. Clair River . <p> Now Lake Huron is close to record high levels and docks are under water . If those St. Clair riffles had been installed , the water levels on Lakes Huron and Michigan would be even higher today . This is the type of short-sighted thinking we need to avoid . <p> Moving back <p> Water needs breathing space . While there is some scientific uncertainty about exactly what climate change will do to water levels , the extreme highs and lows will get worse . Volatility is the new normal . <p> Like climate change , when it comes to addressing Great Lakes levels , the biggest hurdles are n't scientific -- they are political , economic and social . <p> Any new infrastructure along Great Lakes shorelines must be flexible , adaptable and resilient . <p> But we must also realize that the answer is n't more infrastructure .
