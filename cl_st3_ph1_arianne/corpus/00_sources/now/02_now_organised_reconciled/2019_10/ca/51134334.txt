<h> Moose Musings : " Unwilding " Ontario 's Northland <p> If the Ford government gets its way , new hunting regulations will soon be enacted to the delight of Ontario 's moose hunters . The government proposes , controversially , to allow anyone in northern Ontario with a small game hunting license to kill up to two wolves and an unlimited number of coyotes per year . The stated reason : to curtail a decline in the population of moose , a prey coveted by hunters of the Homo sapiens variety . <p> What 's a few wolves and coyotes if it means more moose for the annual hunt ? The problem is , and as science-based studies emphasize , keystone predators such as wolves and coyotes play a vital role in maintaining healthy ecosystems . <p> Under pressure of hunters , conservation groups say , the government is foolishly prioritizing hunting moose for sport over a healthy and balanced natural environment . <p> Wolves and coyotes are being conveniently scapegoated , asserted Toronto Star writer Kaitlyn Mitchell in September . They are not responsible for Ontario 's dwindling moose population . The conservation group Ontario Nature says that since the 1980s , hunting season lengths and road access have increased , as well as the use of all-terrain vehicles , wireless communication , and moose calf hunting . <p> The success rate of hunters has jumped significantly , too , from 20-30 per cent with guns and from 5-10 per cent to 20-30 per cent with bows . It seems that hunters are hunting too many moose and still want more . The proposed regulations are wanton for other reasons . No population management targets and no rationale for the proposed bag limits are offered , Ontario Nature reports . And why the mass slaughter of coyotes ? <p> Eastern coyotes do not consume moose in significant numbers . Also , the number of moose killed per wolf pack will not significantly decrease as the pack size is reduced , Ontario Nature says . <p> Wolves , coyotes and moose have lived together in a complex predator-prey relationship for thousands of years . This rapport naturally regulates itself , MNRF says , benefiting each species and the ecosystems they rely on . <p> What some moose hunters do n't seem to know or care about is that , as keystone predators , wolves and coyotes play a critical role in keeping ecosystems healthy . They help keep moose and deer populations in check which can benefit many other plant and animal species . <p> The reintroduction of wolves into Yellowstone National Park in 1995 -- what some conservationists refer to as " rewilding " -- offers a glimpse of just how radically keystone predators can impact natural environments . <p> British writer George Monbiot , known worldwide for his environmental activism , calls rewilding a " trophic cascade , " which is an " ecological process that starts at the top @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to the bottom . " Trophic refers to food and food supply . <p> Wolves were extirpated from Yellowstone 70 years earlier . Because of their long absence , an over-population of deer had grazed the park 's natural vegetation to almost nothing . With the reintroduction of wolves , and as their numbers increased , deer began avoiding areas frequented by the wolves . The spaces deer eluded began to regenerate . Denuded valleys became forests of aspen , willow and cottonwood . <p> In turn , the forests attracted many species of birds , and perhaps most importantly , beavers known for their ecosystem engineering and ability to create niche habitats for other species by damming streams . <p> Wolves ultimately transformed the ecosystems of a vast park , Monbiot says .
