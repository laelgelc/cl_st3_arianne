<h> Post <h> Energy performance certificates hold back heat decarbonisation <p> Jan Rosenow of the Regulatory Assistance Project explains how the criteria for energy performance certificates are out of step with heat decarbonisation <p> In 2014 , we bought an old Victorian house in Oxford , UK , well aware it needed major renovation work . Our energy performance certificate ( EPC ) , which shows the energy performance of a building , was a poor grade " E " on a scale of A to G , with A being the highest performing category . We have since carried out a major refurbishment programme , installing underfloor insulation , triple and double glazing , underfloor heating , internal solid wall insulation and an air source heat pump . We demolished the old kitchen at the back of the house and replaced it with a modern extension built according to the latest building codes . After all of these improvements , we expected a significant improvement in our EPC rating , yet we are still received an " E " grade . <p> It is possible the assessor made a mistake , up to 62% of EPCs contain errors @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ likely due to the heat pump we installed . Official UK government policy is to roll out heat pumps across the housing stock . The Climate Change Committee , a government advisory body , believes 2.5 million heat pumps need to be installed by 2030 to meet climate targets . In contradiction to this goal , the EPC system penalises people for investing in heat pumps by regularly issuing poor ratings for their homes . Installers have also complained that EPCs do not recommend heat pumps as an energy performance improvement measure . <p> There are at least three reasons why the installation of heat pumps does not result in improved EPC ratings and why it is usually not recommended by the EPC . <p> First , part of the EPC score is based on the carbon emissions of the fuels used in the building . The current algorithm , as determined in the so-called Standard Assessment Procedure , uses the figure of 0.519 kilogram ( kg ) of carbon dioxide ( CO2 ) per kilowatt hour ( kWh ) for electricity . This compares to a carbon factor of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ factor contributing to the low EPC scores is the estimated energy demand . Especially for larger properties such as ours , the estimated energy demand cited on the EPC is often higher than the actual measured energy demand . This is partly a result of assuming a poorer operational efficiency of heat pumps than field trials suggest . <p> Finally , EPCs are also based on the cost of heating a property , and electricity prices are a lot higher than natural gas prices -- approximately threefold in the domestic sector . This is partially a result of a disproportionately high burden on electricity for the costs of environmental programmes , which could drive homeowners to rely on less clean natural gas . <p> There is good news . The next version of the Standard Assessment Procedure will use a lower carbon emissions factor for electricity of 0.233 kg CO2 per kWh , compared to 0.210 kg CO2 per kWh for gas . This will most likely result in heat pumps leading to significant EPC improvements . <p> The new rules will come into force when the building codes are @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ update is an important step towards rewarding properties with heat pumps with an improved EPC . But , given the rapid decarbonisation of the power system , it is likely that in five years the Standard Assessment Procedure will already be out of date . <p> To avoid this happening , and better take into account the rapid decarbonisation of the grid , I would recommend using a dynamic carbon factor that can be updated as the power system gets cleaner . This would more accurately reflect the real carbon impacts and value of low carbon heating systems such as heat pumps . <p> Experiences from the UK provide useful lessons for other countries . If we are to take heat decarbonisation seriously , all policies need to be aligned and based on reality .
