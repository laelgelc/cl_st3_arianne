<p> A remote island in the Arctic ocean northeast of Siberia was identified as the resting place of the world 's last woolly mammoth population . <p> A new study shows that these island mammoths outlived their North American and European counterparts by some 7,000 years , before going abruptly extinct . <p> A genetic analysis reveals that these mammoths , on their isolated island , likely fell victim to inbreeding . This decreased the population 's genetic diversity , and made them less able to adapt to possible natural disasters . <p> About 4,000 years ago on a remote island in the Arctic , the last woolly mammoth died out . <p> Elephantine in shape and size , mammoths ( official name Mammuthus primigenius ) dominated the northern hemisphere during Earth 's last ice age for nearly 90,000 years , before changing climates and human hunting drove them to extinction . <p> Scientists have uncovered mammoth skeletons and frozen carcasses everywhere from Spain to Siberia , and the understanding was that these creatures had wholly disappeared by about 11,000 years ago . <p> But a handful of mammoth populations survived on two tiny , isolated islands nestled between @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ rising seas . Researchers think one of these refuges , name Wrangel Island , became the last mammoth hold-out ; these tusked giants outlived their North American and European counterparts by some 7,000 years before going abruptly extinct . <p> That means mammoths as a species lasted far longer than scientists previously thought . When the last woolly mammoth kicked the bucket , the Great Pyramid of Giza had already been built in Egypt . <p> According to a new study , published in the journal Quaternary Science Reviews , the Wrangel Island inhabitants did n't die of the same causes as other mammoths . Rather , the study authors argue , the isolated animals started to inbreed , which weakened their genetic diversity .
