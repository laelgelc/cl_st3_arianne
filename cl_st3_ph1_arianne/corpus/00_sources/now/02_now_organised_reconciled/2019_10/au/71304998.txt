<p> Crucially , the scheme , which will initially cover facilities in the power-generation sector only , will not be a " carbon market " , as commonly understood . It will not feature an overall cap on carbon dioxide ( CO2 ) emissions . Rather , it will be a CO2 intensity trading scheme . <p> The CO2 intensity of a power plant is the amount of CO2 it emits ( in grams ) per unit of electricity it generates ( in kilowatt-hours ) . Under China 's proposed scheme , every power plant will be allocated a CO2 intensity benchmark . Plants that do better than their benchmark will have excess quota to sell , while plants that do worse will have to buy additional quota . This arrangement creates an economic incentive for power plant operators to reduce the CO2 intensity of their plants . <p> CO2 pricing for power could become an important means of increasing the competitiveness of renewable energy , but the system would need to incentivise zero-carbon power generation , not just marginally more efficient coal power generation . <p> A coal plant operator can reduce its CO2 intensity in two ways @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , or it can burn higher-quality coal . Under a standard ETS , liable entities pay for all their emissions , so cheating by a few per cent does n't make a huge difference . But under an intensity-based scheme , operators are liable only for the fraction of emissions that exceeds their benchmark , so even very minor cheating compromises the scheme 's incentives . <p> Another problem is that price signals are ill-suited to many parts of China 's economy , including some of the most emissions-intensive sectors . There is a push for making wind and solar " subsidy-free " , meaning that their tariffs are to be matched to coal plants . But coal plants enjoy gargantuan direct and indirect subsidies .
