These pollutants are effectively removed in aerobic biological treatment through the activated sludge process , a 100-year-old technique utilising biomass consisting of bacteria and protozoa which feed on the incoming waste . <p> The biomass consume oxygen during feeding and thus , a certain quantity of pollutants are removed ( or oxidised ) in the process . Oxygen is therefore supplied through the process @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ biomass and waste mixture to allow oxygen to transfer into the fluid . <p> Figure 1 : Subsurface image of fine bubble aerated flows in a new wastewater aeration technology being tested at NUI Galway . <p> The overall objectives of an aeration system is then to A/ effectively transfer oxygen from the air into the activated sludge , B/ mix and disperse the oxygen uniformly throughout the tank and C/ do so at the lowest energy cost while not negatively impacting subsequent settlement of activated sludge during clarification . <p> Mechanical technologies such as surface aeration or fine bubble diffused aeration ( FBDA ) technology comprise the majority of the current aeration technology market ( 3 ) . <h> Cost of wastewater aeration <p> Wastewater treatment can cost up to 100 kWhs ( or ? 10 ) per person per year ( 4 ) . It comes down to the demand for oxygen in a reactor and how efficiently oxygen is transferred to meet this demand . <p> For example , to aerobically treat wastewater , about 58 kgO2 per person is required every year ( based on typical BOD and ammonium values of municipal wastewater ( 6 ) . <p> The first level of inefficiency to supply this demand is derived from the method in which oxygen is transferred @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ aeration technology being employed . <p> Aeration technologies are then rated by the so-called standard aeration efficiency ( SAE ) , which is essentially the amount of oxygen introduced per unit energy consumed ( kg O2/kWh ) . <p> Thus , the higher the SAE , the lower the cost of treatment and vice versa . This is analogous with a car 's performance through the ' litres/100km ' or ' miles/gallon ' metric and is stated by the aeration equipment supplier based on ideal clean water tests under standard conditions . <p> However , a ' use at your own peril ' type disclaimer is often recommended by experienced wastewater engineers and scientists ( 3 ) when adopting such values as the question is also prompted " what happens when the equipment is put in real ' in-use ' conditions ? " . <h> Getting technical : Ideal costs versus real costs <p> Typical aeration technologies have an SAE of 1.8 to 3.0 kg O2/kW.h which means that the cost of aeration treatment ideally should be about 19 to 32 kWhs per person per year ( based on the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ year ) . <p> This is a far cry from the 70 kW.hs per person per year that is often measured in the field . The difference between measured clean-water and in-situ aeration efficiencies are down to three main issues : A/ the initial interpretation of total energy costs , B/ effects of real ' in-use ' wastewater conditions and C/ limited aeration control . <p> Usually the choice of aeration technology at the design stage is only based on the SAE value without interpreting the total energy costs of the project . <p> Because aeration is essentially the process of oxygen transfer and mixing , the additional mixing energy should always be included during the initial design and opex considerations as it effects the bottom line aeration energy usage . <p> Adopting a less efficient technology that provides suitable mixing may be a preferred route if the total energy costs are considered . <p> Regarding the second aspect , the performance of many aeration technologies tend to reduce substantially in real process conditions primarily due to the effect of what is known as the ' alpha factor ' . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ SAE and is mainly due to the presence of surfactants ( resulting from detergents , soaps and foaming agents ) in industrial and domestic wastewaters . <p> These surfactants have the effect of creating a barrier for oxygen to transfer from the bubble to the wastewater and can severely diminish the oxygen transfer efficiency . <p> The alpha factor -- which can result in up to an 80 per cent reduction in performance ( 3 ) -- varies per technology and tends to have less of an effect in technologies that have inherently higher circulation , turbulence and mixing effects . <p> In addition to this , some technology 's performance varies over time due to fouling . For example , diffuser heads in an FBDA system can accumulate a biofilm over time which can in turn increase required blower pressure and power . <p> Fouling can also result in larger bubbles which are less efficient in transferring oxygen , thus calling for more airflow and blower power which work even harder against fouled diffuser heads . <p> The combined effects of the alpha and fouling factors are time dependent @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ design stage in order to understand the whole life cycle costs of a proposed aeration system along with maintenance schedules for diffuser cleaning . <p> Finally , the lack of control over an aeration system can have a significant impact on the energy intensity whereby oxygen is often supplied when it is not needed . <p> The challenge is to match the supply and demand of oxygen through the ability of turning up or down the equipment or by switching off the aerator and resorting to mixers when no oxygen is required . <p> Often , aeration equipment has a limited turn-up or torn-down capability and by doing so often results in equipment working outside of its best efficiency point or the inability to supply air against the necessary water pressure in the tank . <h> Technology and approaches <p> In summary , there remains to be considerable inefficiencies present in the process of aeration today which is the result of a combination of
