<h> New Zealand needs to peddle faster on climate change <p> So far New Zealand has taken little action to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions , says Professor David Frame . <p> OPINION : International climate policy is like a long-distance cycle race in which countries collectively aspire to set a fast time , but are not on course to do so . <p> New Zealand , along with the EU and a few other developed countries , has been in the leading bunch , or peloton , though even this is moving slowly . <p> What should be our strategy ? We could break away from the peloton and try to stay in front . We could try to play an active role in the peloton . We could try to stay in the peloton but freeload on the efforts of others by not taking our turn at the front . <p> We could fall behind the peloton . Or we could abandon the race . <p> Though they tend to dominate public discussions of climate change , breaking away and dropping out are both bad ideas @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ get caught by the peloton and you 've burned too much energy even to stay with them . So you end up further back than before . <p> Abandoning the race is not a credible option so long as international reputation matters to us . <p> Reputation is about how much we are seen to be doing , relative to others . There 's no agreed method for making that assessment . The dominant comparator has been the percentage emissions a country will reduce by year ' Y ' . <p> This happens to suit Europe very well , though it is as inadequate and unfair as it is simple . Those who do well under that measure have been blessed with favourable circumstances for decarbonisation : they are peddling downhill since they are able to exploit the combination of a high starting point and cheap reductions , for example by large-scale replacement of coal-fired electricity generation by gas and renewables . <p> A more robust measure , better aligned with the goal of restricting global warming to no more than 2 degrees , is how far a country is already @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ sector comparison -- and makes it plain which sectors in which countries matter most . <p> Very few countries are currently fully aligned with the 2 degrees or global decarbonisation goal . Many are tracking in the wrong direction : Singapore , with far higher income per capita than New Zealand , aims to increase its emissions until 2030 . <p> In Europe , Spain , with similar per capita income , and similar emissions growth since 1990 as New Zealand is permitted , under the EU arrangements , to continue to grow its emissions until 2020 . <p> The UK has some strong targets , some of which it will miss . <p> Where does New Zealand stand ? We have done OK so far without trying very hard , because forestry in particular provides us with the ability to freewheel . <p> New Zealand has achieved little real reduction in gross emissions beyond those resulting from increased efficiency of production . <p> For the most part , we have met targets by paying for reductions elsewhere . This ought to be perfectly respectable as an interim measure . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ elsewhere ; it is another to pay for imaginary emissions reductions elsewhere , which is largely what New Zealand has been doing . <p> But the next phase of the decarbonisation looks like a hill climb . Forests in New Zealand are likely to become a source of emissions in the next decade . Future access to carbon units is not guaranteed and prices are likely to be higher . <p> Different tactics are required for deeper , long-term decarbonisation . Countries such as Sweden and the UK are adopting this perspective . New Zealand 's current position seems to assume the indefinite availability and affordability of international credits . Neither is certain . <p> In any case , offshore credit purchasing does nothing towards the long-term goal of decarbonisation of our economy . <p> This could be aided by reforms to the currently ineffectual Emission Trading Scheme ( ETS ) . <p> Get rid of the " interim " two-for-one arrangement ; set a floor price ; reconsider its unique 100 per cent openness to international markets ; fix these points , consider a special regime for agriculture ( currently @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ it . <p> Finally , we need an organised and coherent approach to unlock emissions reduction potential across the economy . Transport , where electrification holds promise , and industrial energy for a start . Then agriculture . <p> Properly addressing these domestic issues need not be expensive -- it is more about organisation and strategic thinking than the imposition of large costs . And the failure to think about the next phases of climate policy brings clear risks . <p> Whatever the outcome of the Paris climate change talks , New Zealand and other countries will be forced to think seriously about future domestic climate change policies . <p> The ETS review , if it is put in this broader context , is an opportunity to get things right -- and for New Zealand to remain comfortably in the leading peloton . <p> Professor David Frame heads the Climate Change Research Institute at Victoria University in Wellington .
