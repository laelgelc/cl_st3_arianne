<h> A tribute to Ken Saro-Wiwa <p> I found myself crying on my floor on Tuesday morning , November 10 , when I was reminded that it was the 20th anniversary of the brutal killing of Nigerian activist Ken Saro-Wiwa and eight of his comrades from the Ogoni people in the Niger Delta . <p> A community whose oil has been a curse to them ; oil that has caused environmental degradation , unbearable living conditions due to the waste dumping by Shell and even death . Why was I sobbing like this for a man and his comrades when they had passed away when I was only three years old ? A man that I never met ? <p> The internalised patriarchal whispers in my head started their backtalk : " Why are you being emotional ? " I realised , I was crying out of frustration and pain , because little has changed since their deaths . I was crying because Saro-Wiwa 's words , written in 1995 still hold truth today . We continue to " protect injustice and oppression " , all over Africa @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ before he was executed , Saro-Wiwa explains the injustice the people of Ogoniland go through and what his organisation - the Movement for the Survival of the Ogoni People ( MOSOP ) - was fighting for . He said " the denigrating poverty of my people who live on a richly endowed land , distressed by their political marginalisation and economic strangulation , angered by the devastation of their land , their ultimate heritage , anxious to preserve their right to life and to a decent living , and determined to usher to this country as a whole a fair and just democratic system which protects everyone and every ethnic group and gives us all a valid claim to human civilisation . " <p> I observed South Africa 's response to the day closely and the silence was loud . The media was silent with only a few platforms making an attempt to remember . Our government was silent , and " climate change activists " were silent . <p> Why were we silent and what role was this silence playing ? I wondered if the silence was loud because we @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and that we too are guilty of the crimes to humanity that the #Ogoni9 and others fought and died for ? <p> I wondered if being reminded of Saro-Wiwa and the struggles of the Ogoni people in Nigeria hit too close to home ? Will it remind us of the murder of workers in Marikana by the state machinery while protecting white monopoly capital because of the crumbs they feed them ? Will it remind us of the inhumane living conditions of miners in hostels and the preventable deaths and injuries that happen while their dig resources from their land whose benefits they will never see ? <p> Will it remind us of farmworkers in South Africa who feed the nation , yet are still dispossessed of their land and have to endure white racism and oppression from their employers . Will it remind us that we too allow big companies to pollute our air and the waters of communities because profits matter more ? <p> Will remind us that the South African government and the private collude at the expense of people ? And it will remind us that " @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ pay the price " as economist Jeffrey Sachs states in the Oxfam Even It Up report . <p> The work of silence forms a political objective , silence is not neutral , silence echoes complacency to the causes of oppression and the actors inflicting oppression . Silence further silences those voices like the Ogoni people speaking out against injustice , speaking out for their dignity . <p> We have witnessed their ultimate silencing through death . Saro-Wiwa and his comrades were silenced through death like death was used to silence 34 miners shot in Marikana . In the documentary " Miners Shot Down " detailing the killing of 34 workers and the injury of others , the director , Rehad Desai , says in the film " today these miners are being shot at by our new government to ensure that it 's business as usual , we could see that lives were now being sacrificed for money and that the young democracy we had so much hoped for was under threat " . <p> Like Lonmin and other mining companies in South Africa , there has been no accountability on @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ expense of human lives . Like there has been no real accountability for Shell in Nigeria besides the few millions that it gave to the families of the nine victims in which they " insisted the money was not an admission of guilt " , as written by Eromo Egbejule . <p> Furthermore , as many activists have highlighted , the $83.3m that Shell paid to residents in Ogoniland for two oil spills amounts to nothing compared to the devastation caused , as Egbejule further points out . <p> An American scholar said in reference to slavery in the United States that slavery is something that lives and is living . Many have affirmed that even within the African context , forms of oppression like slavery , colonialism and apartheid , lives and are living . <p> When Saro-Wiwa was awarded the Right Livelihood Award , he wrote a letter for his acceptance speech in 1994 . He says " my experience is that we are face to face with a Modern Slave Trade similar in many ways to the Atlantic Slave Trade in which European merchants armed African middlemen to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ intangible profits . <p> " As in the Atlantic Slave Trade , the multinational company reaps huge profits ; the African middlemen - in this case the Nigerian nation-state - are debt-ridden and in chaos ; their common victim , the Ogoni and similar resource-bearing communities face extinction . " Saro-Wiwa 's sentiments are similar to Mzoxolo Magidiwana , one of the Marikana leaders who was disabled by the shooting when he said " we work like slaves , even our fathers were rockdrillers , either they die or go back home still as a rockdriller . Poverty forces you to forget your ambition , leave school and work as a rock driller at the same mine " . <p> For me , the importance of remembering the 20th anniversary of Ken Sero-Wiwa 's murder is a politically necessary act for all oppressed people . It is a reminder to us that people have resisted their enslavement and have mobilised to dismantle it . <p> When miners in Marikana organised the strikes that led to their deaths , when Saro-Wiwa organised 300 000 of his people into protects in 1993 , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ economic exploitation . And as African American activist Assata Shakur reminds us that " after a while , people just think oppression is a normal state of things " . <p> " But to become free , you have to be acutely aware of being a slave . " <p> May Ken Sero-Wiwa 's legacy and spirit continue to remind us , that we must continue to fight until we are all free from all forms of oppression . <p> * Simamkele Dlakavu is an MA student at Wits and works for Oxfam in South Africa . <p> ** The views expressed here do not necessarily reflect those of Independent Media .
