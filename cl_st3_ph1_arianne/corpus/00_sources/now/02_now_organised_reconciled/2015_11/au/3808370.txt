<p> Hundreds of divers and snorkelers will take to the water in Victoria to monitor the state 's coastal ecology as part of the annual Great Victorian Fish Count . <p> Described as the biggest citizen science event on the marine calendar , divers will spend the next two weeks keeping an eye on local species , and looking out for any new and unusual sightings . <p> Organiser Mark Rodrigue said the event , which is now it its eleventh year , had made some surprising findings in the past . <p> " It 's a fantastic opportunity for people to discover what lies beneath the waves , " he said . <p> " In recent years there have been reports of sightings of some unusual fish that more historically have been found in other states and other parts of Australia , " he said . <p> " One of the things the fish count is quite proud of in its history , is it discovered a large boney fish in Victoria , that grows 1.4 metres . <p> " That 's the western blue groper , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ it was identified by communities on the west coast in some early fish counts and has been seen regularly , not only there but in some other locations as well . <p> " We 're interested in looking at fish on the move and what fish populations exist in Victoria and how those fish might be changing . " <p> Mr Rodrigue said climate change has resulted in a number of species moving from their traditional habitats . <p> " Already there are quite significant shifts in things like temperature , strengthening of the east Australian current , and this is what 's contributing to some of the species we 're interested it . " <p> Mr Rodrigue said he hoped those taking part would log their sightings using the Redmap project , which is run by the University of Tasmania .
