<p> Monday , November 2 , 2015 , 3:45 PM - Since 2001 , satellite measurements have suggested the bright surface that makes up Greenland 's Arctic ice is darkening , causing solar heat to be absorbed while accelerating surface melt -- a suggestion that has been backed up by numerous studies and anecdotal evidence . <p> Some papers suggested the " dark snow " is the result of forest fires or climate change -- but a new study led by researchers at Dartmouth shows that degrading satellite sensors aboard NASA 's MODIS satellites are behind the decline in reflectivity , not pollution . <p> The results suggest the ice sheet has n't lost as much reflectivity as previously thought . The findings also show carbon and dust concentrations in the area are n't causing the upper ice sheet to darken , and their presence in the atmosphere has n't increased significantly , as was previously thought . <p> For the study , lead author Chris Polashenski and his team analyzed dozens of snow samples from 2012-2014 across northern Greenland and compared them with samples from earlier years @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the quantity of black carbon deposited for the past 60 years or the quantity and mineralogical makeup of dust compared to the last 12,000 years , meaning that deposition of these light absorbing impurities is not a primary cause of reflectivity reduction or surface melting in the dry snow zone . Algae growth , which darkens ice , also was ruled out as a factor , " Dartmouth says in a press release . <p> " Instead , the findings suggest the apparent decline in the dry snow zone 's reflectivity is being caused by uncorrected degradation of sensors in NASA 's aging MODIS satellites and that the declining trend will likely disappear when new measurements are reprocessed.
