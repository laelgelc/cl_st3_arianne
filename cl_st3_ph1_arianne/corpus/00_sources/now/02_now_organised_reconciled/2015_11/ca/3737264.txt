<p> All of the SDGs are critically important , but number 17 is arguably the most important : " Strengthen the means of implementation and revitalize the global partnership for sustainable development . " <p> The first question we need to ask , then , is how to implement sustainable development ? <p> As the chief architect of the SDGs Jeffrey Sachs notes in his accompanying book The Age of Sustainable Development , the SDGs call for socially inclusive and environmentally sustainable economic growth . <p> To this standard definition Sachs , an economics professor at Columbia University , adds a fourth dimension : good governance . According to Sachs , good governance can not refer only to governments : " The world 's multinational companies are often the most powerful actors . Our wellbeing depends on these powerful companies obeying the law , respecting the natural environment , and helping the communities in which they operate .... Yet as with governments , reality is often the reverse . " <p> When we closely examine the governance of the natural resource extraction industries , we see what Canadian law scholars Penelope Simons and Audrey Macklin call a " governance gap . " In their book The Governance Gap : Extractive industries , human rights , and the home state advantage they note " the powerful influence of business actors on governments . Business objections that regulation will impede competitiveness , coupled with the threat of corporate flight in response to regulation , are persistent and often effective , whatever their empirical merits . " <p> The right question , then , is how to make governments responsive to the public 's long-term interest in sustainable development rather than corporations ' short-term interest in profit maximization ? <p> Sustainable development also calls for the integration of economic development , environmental protection and social inclusivity through good governance in the public interest .
