<p> Strategies to combat climate change by lowering Alberta 's greenhouse gas emissions are high on the provincial government 's agenda . <p> The climate change advisory panel under chairman Andrew Leach was announced even before the royalty review panel . Now it 's working under a tight deadline to provide advice to government in advance of international climate change talks starting soon in Paris . <p> The Canadian Home Builders ' Association-Alberta was co-convener of a technical session for the panel on ways to lower emissions from all types of buildings , including single and multi-family homes , as well as commercial , industrial and institutional structures . <p> We 'll have to wait to see the government 's full plan , but the new budget got things rolling by allocating $5 million annually to establish an energy retrofitting loan program aimed at families , farms and small businesses . <p> That 's encouraging , especially when the emissions picture is seen in context . Buildings are responsible for eight per cent of emissions -- that 's the lowest of the seven sectors the panel reviewed -- @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ comparison , the oilsands , conventional oil and gas , and electrical generation , produce more than 60 per cent of greenhouse gases . <p> Even without a new climate change strategy , energy efficiency in the residential sector is set to improve significantly over the next year as new national standards become requirements under the building codes . This is the next step in the long ramp up of standards that has made each generation of Canadian homes more efficient . <p> At the same time , Albertans should be proud of our innovative entrepreneurs who are finding ways to lower the costs of net-zero homes to within the range of typical home buyers , adapt designs to suit Alberta 's demanding climate , and solve the technical problems of connecting to the power grid . <p> While new homes will help curb emissions growth , they wo n't affect the existing housing stock . That includes about 1.5 million private dwellings whose ages reflect Alberta 's growth surge since the Second World War . <p> More than 60 per cent of all homes were built before 1995 . To put that in perspective , imagine that 20 per cent of the vehicles on the road today were gas-guzzlers from that era . <p> A CHBA study looked at how much energy was consumed by a popular style of home from the mid-1970s . CHBA updated the original construction specs and determined that the same home built today according to current codes would use 66 per cent less energy . New codes will widen the energy efficiency gap between old and new . <p> In our submission to the climate change panel , CHBA recommended the government consider ways to encourage energy retrofits . Besides shrinking the environmental footprint of the residential sector , retrofit programs in other jurisdictions have helped create jobs and generate tax revenues because black-market operators are cut out of the picture . <p> An energy efficiency labelling program for new and resale homes could also help raise the value of high-performing homes in the eyes of consumers .
