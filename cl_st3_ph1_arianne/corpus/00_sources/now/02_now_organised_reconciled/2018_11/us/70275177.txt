<p> I know what you 're thinking , but I 'm not referring to Thanksgiving or store-bought pie or even home-cooked pie created from canned brown stuff . I 'm talking about real , live pumpkins , the kind folks buy for Halloween Jack o'Lanterns . <p> About 20 years ago pumpkins in our garden shifted from an experimental crop to a must-grow crop . Whether due to improved soil after adding horse manure for 30 years or to climate change extending our growing season by a month , I do n't know . <p> Either way , most pumpkins picked in early to mid-September reliably prove mature enough to ripen indoors , and the last couple years have even seen a few of the big fruits maturing on their vines . Each year we harvest up to a dozen , storing them until time allows -- usually October and November -- for processing and freezing the results . <p> Pumpkins despise cold soil , so I grow ours in raised beds made from 55-gallon gas drums , cleaned and aged before being cut in half to form two vertical cylinders . With drain holes added and filled with @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ drainage , each one supports one or two plants . Two or three more sprawling plants thrive in a leaky old horse trough . <p> Frequent watering prevents premature yellowing of leaves , and a good twice-monthly boost of nitrogen-rich fertilizer ( we use potent and nose-pinching rotten-fish water ) encourages rapid growth . Floating row covers boost the temperature but should be removed when flowering begins to allow pollinators to do their job . <p> Usually slender-stemmed male flowers show first , followed by the chunky-based females that will actually produce the fruit . After early August I cut off any flowers or fruits that wo n't mature , to focus energy on maturing pumpkins . <p> I 've had good luck with early-season pumpkins such as Neon and Racer ( Veseys Seeds ) , which grow to 10 to 15 pounds ( occasionally bigger ) , as well as smaller 2- to 5-pound charmers like Small Sugar ( Denali Seed Co. ) and Cinnamon Girl PMR Fl(Johnny 's Selected Seeds ) . I harvest the fruits when frost threatens . Realistically , though , I just haul them in and leave them in a cool corner on the floor . There , they usually store well until January or longer . However , mine do suffer the occasional untimely death , melting away into a draining , mushy puddle with alarming suddenness . Consequently , I try to process them soon after the last vestiges of green ( or , in the case of Neon , yellow ) fully shift to the classic deep-orange pumpkin color . <p> Pumpkins can be processed in several ways , but my favorite is to bake them . After slicing in half with a strong knife , I gut them with a spoon . Sharpening the edges of the spoon 's bowl with a file or whetstone greatly speeds this job . Set the cut side down in a shallow pan and bake at 400 degrees until a fork slips in easily -- about an hour , more or less @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ stove oven . <p> Once cooked , the pulp can be mashed and devoured immediately with butter and a little brown sugar or maple syrup . Usually , though , I 'll put a cup of milk into the blender and then fill it half full with lumps of pumpkin scraped from the rind , blending until smooth . <p> The resulting thick soup stores well in the freezer for use throughout the year . We often simply eat it warmed with brown sugar and crushed walnuts . It can substitute in any recipe for canned pumpkin , taking the thinner consistency into consideration . <p> A luscious pumpkin pudding can be made by mixing 3 cups of pumpkin slurry with 4 eggs , 2 cups of evaporated milk , and sugar or brown sugar to taste , along with your choice of vanilla , cinnamon , allspice , ginger , nutmeg and cloves , and baked at 350-400 degrees for an hour or until the pudding stiffens .
