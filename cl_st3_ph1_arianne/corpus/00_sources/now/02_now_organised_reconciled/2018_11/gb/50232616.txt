<p> New research has disputed a longstanding view that early humans helped wipe out many of the large mammals that once roamed Africa . <p> Today , Africa broadly has five species of massive , plant-eating mammal ; but millions of years ago there were many more types of giant herbivore . <p> Why so many types vanished is not known , but many experts have blamed our tool-using , meat-eating ancestors . <p> Now , researchers say the mammal decline began long before humans appeared . <p> Writing in the journal Science , Tyler Faith , from the Natural History Museum of Utah , and colleagues argue that long-term environmental change drove the extinctions . <p> This mainly took the form of an expansion of grasslands , in response to falling atmospheric carbon dioxide ( CO2 ) levels . <p> " Despite decades of literature asserting that early hominins ( human relatives ) impacted ancient African faunas , there have been few attempts to actually test this scenario or to explore alternatives , " said Dr Faith . <p> A transition from eating mainly vegetables and fruit to predominantly eating meat may have driven the evolution of humans @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the development of stone tools , which would have allowed our ancestors to butcher the carcasses of animals ; either as scavengers or hunters . <p> Image copyrightSplImage caption Artwork : The extinct giraffe Sivatherium maurusium vanishes from the fossil record around 700,000 years ago <p> To investigate whether humans played a role , the researchers compiled a seven-million-year record of herbivore extinctions in eastern Africa .
