AFP PHOTO / JAMES COOK UNIVERSITY &amp; BIOPIXEL <p> SYDNEY , Australia -- Scientists have launched the largest-ever attempt to regenerate coral on the endangered Great Barrier Reef by harvesting millions of the creatures ' eggs and sperm during their annual spawning . <p> The researchers said Wednesday , November 28 , they plan to grow coral larvae from the harvested eggs and return these to areas of the reef which have been badly damaged by climate-related coral bleaching . <p> " This is the first time that the entire process of large scale larval rearing and settlement will be undertaken directly on reefs on the Great Barrier Reef , " said Peter Harrison @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <p> " Our team will be restoring hundreds of square meters with the goal of getting to square kilometers in the future , a scale not attempted previously , " he said in a statement . <p> The " Larval Restoration Project " launch was timed to coincide with the annual coral spawn on the reef , which began earlier this week and will last only about 48 to 72 hours . <p> Coral along large swathes of the 2,300-kilometer ( 1,400-mile ) reef have been killed by rising sea temperatures linked to climate change , leaving behind skeletal remains in a process known as coral bleaching . <p> The northern reaches of the reef suffered an unprecedented two successive years of severe bleaching in 2016 and 2017 , raising fears it may have suffered irreparable damage . <p> Harrison and his colleagues are hopeful their reseeding project can help reverse the trend , but he cautioned the effort will not be enough on its own to save the reef . <p> " Climate action is the only way to ensure coral reefs can survive into the future , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ restoration aims to buy time for coral populations to survive and evolve until emissions are capped and our climate stabilizes . " <p> The scientists hope that coral which have survived bleaching have a greater tolerance to rising temperatures so that a breeding population produced from this year 's spawn will grow into coral better able to survive future bleaching events . <p> The researchers , who also include experts from James Cook University and the University of Technology Sydney ( UTS ) , said a novelty of their reseeding project was to grow coral larvae together with microscopic algae .
