<h> Cultivating a taste for local rice varieties <p> Sitting at the eponymously named Ekdalia Road restaurant in south Calcutta last week , I was pleasantly surprised to see my yellowfin tuna in chimichurri sauce accompanied by a glistening mound of black rice . " I get it from local farmers , " said owner-chef Surojit Rout , who I discovered , is a passionate believer in showcasing Bengal produce . " I hope others will become aware of its superior flavour ! " <p> As usual the rest of the world knows this rice by the generic term " forbidden rice " because in China only royalty and nobility were allowed to eat it . India , unfortunately , has no such stories to buoy its allure , and hence black rice varieties have languished and been overshadowed by the lure of white rice , particularly basmati and its hybrid compatriots . Politics of colour even in cooking . <p> I eagerly tucked into the rice on my plate and was immediately struck by its flavour . It had a beguiling nuttiness , and was neither glutinous like some varieties nor earthily coarse like red rice . It @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ pulao or a sexy paella -- imagine coral-hued crustaceans and white fish standing out against that black rice -- or even a darkly desirable porcini risotto . <p> " Where can I buy some , " I asked Surojit excitedly . " Many stores have them now , though I get them from farmers , " came the reply . And that made me even happier . While the West Bengal government outlet Biswa Bangla ( my must-visit store at Kolkata Airport ! ) has been stocking several indigenous rice varieties for a while now , private stores stocking them means incipient demand . <p> Some digging around about this marvellous rice revealed that it is found all over Bengal , Jharkhand , Manipur and Odisha and its dark colour is due to antioxidants called anthocyanins . According to experts , black rice is not only rich in fibre and has a low glycemic index ( ideal for diabetics ) it also has more vitamins B and E , calcium , magnesium , zinc , niacin and iron than its white cousin . <p> It made me wonder whether the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the rise in diabetes among the bhaatloving Bengalis . Had they stuck to healthier , unpolished grains like their ancestors , " soogaar " may not have become the prime Bengali ailment . But it is never too late to return to older rice strains , especially as they are better for both human and soil health . <p> Surojit said some people in Bengal are going back to cultivating old rice varieties . And indeed , I found an article describing the efforts of the Forum for Indigenous Agricultural Movement , a group of 80-odd " modern traditional " farmers from 20 villages in Uttar and Dakshin Dinajpur districts who eschewed chemical-intensive , high-yield crops in favour of heirloom local rice varieties . <p> Heirloom rice including black ones are even more relevant to our times because climate change has led to new pests and diseases , making commercially grown rice very vulnerable . This leads to a vicious cycle of more pesticides etc . But indigenous rice varieties are not only more robust but are also able to withstand deficient water ( drought ) or excess ( flood @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ their names have a captivating local lilt , such as the flood-resistant , short-grained Shole , Hetomari and Narayankamini and drought-tolerant Pari , Kakri and Kelas , not to mention Gamrah that grows beside the Kosi in Bihar and can weather both flood and drought ! Surojit mentioned one he had just been told about called Radhunipagol , literally ' mad cook ' , and I ca n't wait to try it ! <p> Just their native feistiness somehow makes local rice more attractive to me than the laboratory-bolstered attractions of basmati . But for them to survive and thrive in a market driven by prices , attractiveness and cachet rather than health benefits , it has to gain attention and demand for their intrinsic goodness . Chefs and high profile opinion makers must lead the way , but all of us too must give them a try .
