Most notable is the split between Canada 's pure producers , who are being devastated by plummeting local prices , and the large , integrated energy companies that have been mostly unscathed . There 's also a rift between oilsands producers -- a target of climate-change activists around the world -- and the frackers and conventional drillers that have been suffering from the pipeline bottlenecks brought on by those environmental opponents . <p> An aerial view of a Chevron well site in Fox Creek , Alta . That split is hampering the sector 's ability to lobby the government with a consistent message . <p> " There is definitely a lot of concern around whether it 's really appropriate that one body is representing what has become a very complex industry with varied products and interests , " said Rafi Tahmazian , who helps manage about $1 billion in investments at Canoe Financial in Calgary . <p> The Canadian divisions bubbled to a head late last month , when top executives from 15 of the nation 's top oil producers met with Alberta Premier Rachel Notley and , instead of presenting a unified front and a list of demands , they were said to have sparred with each other in front of her .
