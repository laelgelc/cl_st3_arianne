<h> Meat consumption contributes to the murder of our planet <p> In a world where heatwaves will be longer , hotter , drier , and more frequent , now is the right time to start a serious conversation about animal agriculture , writes Paul Gilding . <p> In mid-July , as a brutal drought decimated crops , some European dairy and meat farmers were forced to cull their herds early to reduce the number of animals they needed to feed . <p> Such desperate decisions will become routine in a world where heatwaves will be longer , hotter , drier , and more frequent . That is why now is the right time to start a serious conversation about animal agriculture . <p> The animal agriculture industry is not only vulnerable to the observed and predicted effects of climate change ; it is also a key contributor to the problem . In fact , the farming of animals for meat and dairy products accounts for 16.5% of global greenhouse-gas emissions . <p> Moreover , if we treated climate change as the emergency it is and were serious about slowing the pace of warming over the next 20 years , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . This is because the animal sector is responsible for a third of all anthropogenic methane and two-thirds of nitrous oxide emissions -- both potent greenhouse gases that trap more heat than carbon dioxide . <p> Beyond climate pollution , a staggering 60% of all biodiversity loss is attributable to land-use changes from animal cultivation , and as much as 80% of all land in farming is devoted either to raising animals or to growing their feed . There is also the stubborn reality that eating so many animals is making us less healthy . <p> We can do better without great sacrifice . According to recent research by Chatham House , for example , people in developed countries would accept reducing their meat intake if convenient and tasty alternatives were available for about the same price . <p> To be clear , I am not suggesting that everyone should become vegetarian or vegan ( though it is indisputable that these diets are better for the environment , contribute less to climate change , and are healthier ) . By supporting polluting and inhumane practices , countries are literally paying companies to undermine the emissions targets set by the 2015 Paris climate agreement . <p> Instead , governments can -- and should -- shift subsidies to encourage the production of more climate-neutral , protein-rich crops , like pulses and other legumes . By planting more beans , peas , and lentils , farmers would help combat climate change by lowering greenhouse-gas emissions , and would benefit from crops that are more resilient to drier , warmer weather . <p> Government support can also be diverted from factory meat farms toward development of plant-based " meats " and other meat alternatives . In many countries , lobbyists have even successfully championed the prohibition of meat-based names for plant-based products . <p> For example , France recently banned terms like vegan " burger " and " steak " on the grounds that only animal meat could be either . <p> Such policies are a clear barrier to reducing meat consumption , and they contradict countries ' commitment to meet their Paris-agreement targets . <p> The meat sector 's support for them puts it in the same role as the fossil-fuel industry , focused on nothing more than its own bottom line . Or can producers engage as a positive force in the transition away from meat ? <p> When it comes to climate policy , food and agriculture should be treated more like energy and transportation , both of which have clear guidance on how to address climate change and reduce emissions . Policies to limit the planet-warming effects of agriculture and food production are long overdue . <p> We are two @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ under the Paris agreement . Countries will have to show what they have done and what they are doing to cut their emissions . But they will leave a huge part of the problem untouched if they fail to address the production and consumption of meat and dairy . <p> Meanwhile , European farmers will suffer through more droughts like the one they endured this summer , as will other food producers around the world . <p> It will become harder and harder to farm meat and dairy products , and early culls will become the new normal . <p> As with fossil fuels , our only choice is to overcome the industry 's defensive resistance and transform our food system so that it enables diets that are healthier , cleaner , and every bit as delicious . <p> Paul Gilding is co-founder of the Changing Markets Foundation , a fellow at the University of Cambridge Institute for Sustainability Leadership , a former CEO of Greenpeace International , and the author of The Great Disruption : How the Climate Crisis Will Transform the Global Economy
