<h> Andrew Coyne : Carbon pricing is cheapest way to reduce emissions but political cost is too high <p> The Liberal government will , we are told , ratify the Paris climate accord , together with its commitments for reductions in carbon emissions , in time for the next UN climate conference in Marrakesh , Morocco , the first week of November , though it has yet to reach an agreement with the provinces on how to implement it . <p> Perhaps this is not the obstacle it appears , as the minister of the environment seemed to suggest in an interview over the weekend : either the provinces agree to introduce some form of carbon pricing on their own , or the feds will impose it on them . They 'll make them a commitment they ca n't refuse . <p> Or perhaps the commitment is not what it appears . The new target to which the Liberals pledged themselves at Paris , we learned in the same interview , is the same as the old target set by the Conservative government : a reduction in emissions @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ In opposition , the Liberals had attacked this as inadequate ; in government they described it as merely a " floor " they intended to surpass . Now it is also the ceiling . <p> The difference , the minister insisted , is that the Harper target was a " fake " target it had no intention of delivering on . Whereas this is a " real " target . It 's still inadequate -- among the least ambitious of the major economies , who themselves have yet to commit to anything like the reductions most scientists believe necessary to achieve the UN 's goal of holding global warming to two degrees C above pre-industrial levels . But at least the Liberals intend to achieve it . <p> Now I know what you 're thinking . Did n't the previous Liberal government also commit to an emissions reductions target at Kyoto in 1997 -- six per cent from 1990 levels -- without the first clue of how to achieve it , a nullity amply reflected in the nine years of inaction that followed ? <p> But this time is different . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Harper Conservatives , the Trudeau government has a -- well no , they do n't really have a plan , do they ? They have a plan to make a plan . Of course , in the feds ' absence the biggest provinces have gone ahead with their own plans , whether the carbon tax that British Columbia has already implemented and Alberta says it will , or the cap-and-trade schemes that Ontario and Quebec are at various stages of introducing . <p> So the federal plan , so far as there is one , amounts for the most part to telling the provinces to do what they are already doing : different programs , with different coverage , different exceptions and offsets , and not as a substitute for all the other provincial and federal programs enacted over the years in the name of fighting climate change -- the subsidies for green technologies and conservation incentives and regulatory edicts of all kinds -- but layered on top of them . The new element would appear to be that the feds will set a floor under the provincial schemes : the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ tax or its cap-and-trade equivalent , will have to exceed some federally-set minimum . <p> Fair enough , as far as it goes . But what will that minimum be ? British Columbia has the nation 's highest carbon price , at $30 a tonne ; its government has frozen it there until the other provinces have caught up . Suppose the feds , overriding the strenuous objections of provinces like Saskatchewan , which wants no targets , or Quebec , which is all for targets unless they 're federal , drags everyone up to B.C. 's level ? <p> Then we will still be miles away from meeting our targets . Emissions of greenhouse gases are running at about 750 megatonnes annually , about the same as in 2005 ; on current trends they are expected to reach 768 MT by 2020 and 815 MT by 2030 . Which means that in the 14 years we have left , we have to reduce emissions by nearly 36 per cent , just to hit the original -- inadequate -- target of 524 MT . <p> What would it take to get @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ carbon pricing scheme would need to start at $30 a tonne today , rising to $200 by 2030 . This , in a country that goes berserk if the price of gas rises a nickel a litre at the pump . Jaccard figures the odds against any government taking on this sort of political risk are so prohibitive that we 'd be best advised to rely on regulatory fixes instead : the costs to the economy would be much greater -- carbon pricing is far the cheapest way to reduce emissions -- but since these costs would be largely disguised from the public , the political costs would be that much lower . <p> But this is perverse . Understand that absolutely nothing will happen if we do not hit our target , either to us or the planet -- we account for just 1.6 per cent of global emissions . The obligation is wholly moral : to do our share of the common work of averting a global calamity , even one that will mostly affect others . Selfless acts do n't deserve to be punished any more than necessary @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ are unwilling do what 's right by the planet , even in the cheapest way possible , that deserves to be exposed . To charge ourselves hundreds of dollars a tonne more just so we do n't have to see the bill -- to trick ourselves into doing the right thing -- would seem neither economic nor moral .
