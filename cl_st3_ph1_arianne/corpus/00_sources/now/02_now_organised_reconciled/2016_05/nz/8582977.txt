<h> Quantifying the risks <p> When our great-grandchildren look back on our stewardship of the planet , they may well struggle to understand why we left it so late , and did so little , to avert the dislocations they will have to live with . <p> But there is one group of people that will escape their judgement : the climate scientists who have done such a magnificent job over the past few decades of warning us of the risks we are running . <p> This month has seen the publication of a report from the Royal Society on Implications of Climate Change for New Zealand . <p> I imagine young minds reading this in a century 's time and staring in wonder that " they saw it coming and still they did n't stop it ' ' . <p> The report highlights six key risks . <p> For each it asks a few key questions : What is already happening ? <p> What might the future hold ? <p> What would be the implications for us if we manage to achieve a low-carbon future ? <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ knowledge gaps that we need to fill ? <p> ONE <p> Risk one is our exposure to coastal change : rising sea levels , increased frequency and severity of storm surges , flooding , and salination of water tables . <p> All of these will affect east coast communities such as Dunedin more sharply . <p> In a high-carbon world , sea levels would rise by up to a metre by the end of the century , and keep rising after that ; and today 's one-in-100-year high tides will occur " almost daily ' ' . <p> Considerable infrastructure , both public and private , will be affected and " landward migration ' ' will be the unwelcome result . <p> TWO <p> Risk two is flooding . <p> A warmer world is a wetter world and the volume of rainwater during storms will be beyond the capacity of current stormwater systems . <p> Saturated land will see runoff swell rivers and increase the frequency and magnitude of floods with " widespread impacts to property and assets ' ' . <p> THREE <p> Risk three is to fresh water . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ will be more frequent and more severe . <p> The risk of fires will rise . <p> Competition for water will increase for agriculture , town supply and hydro power . <p> Regimes of water management will have to change to allocate supply across competing needs . <p> For some regions , this may result in " transformational changes in farm and land management ' ' . <p> Water may increasingly be priced , and recycled . <p> FOUR <p> Risk four is to our surrounding ocean . <p> New Zealand has an exclusive economic zone of 4million square kilometres , the fourth largest in the world . <p> In a high carbon world , the oceans will become warmer and more acidic . <p> Fish species may migrate south from lower latitudes ( and with them , I would add , the fishing fleets that feed off them ) . <p> Paua , mussels and other marine organisms that form shells will struggle , including some types of plankton upon which extensive food chains rely . <p> FIVE <p> Risk five is to our ecosystems . <p> Some 50,000 species @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Earth . <p> The most severe immediate impacts of climate change on biodiversity are likely to come from human responses to the challenge : new diseases ; more weeds , pests , fires ; less water ; changes to land use and coastal land ; renewable energy systems ( hydro , biomass , wind and marine ) . <p> SIX <p> Risk six is international . Climate change , particularly in a high-carbon scenario , will have widespread and dramatic impacts on the world 's productive , economic , political and social systems . <p> Feeding a 2050 population of nine billion people will require a 70% increase in food production from a world in which the productive potential of many regions will be severely disrupted . <p> Migrations will increase in scale and frequency . <p> The economic viability of some nation states will be undermined . <p> The risks of violent conflicts will increase . <p> New Zealand will be less likely than most to experience these effects directly , but as a trading nation that has so much of what the rest of the world will increasingly need @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ pressures building up in the world outside will surely find their way to our shores also . <p> All of this sounds quite apocalyptic , and it is natural for us to be sceptical of doomsayers . <p> Few people alive today in New Zealand have experienced the collapse of a world they knew and kept them safe . <p> Radical change on the scale being described is perhaps just not something we can imagine . <p> But if we were to ask Dunedin 's newest residents just arrived from Syria , they would tell us how quickly human institutions can crumble under pressure . <p> Believe it or not , I am an optimist . <p> I think humanity will avoid the worst that climate change can do . <p> But only if we are brave enough to recognise the risks we run . <p> Just maybe , our great-grandchildren will be grateful . <p> What do you reckon ? <p> - Colin Campbell-Hunt is an emeritus professor at the CSAFE Centre for Sustainability , University of Otago .
