<h> ' Sprinter ' and ' sprummer ' : Why the old seasons do n't make sense anymore <p> CHRISTINE RUSH <p> Last updated 14:37 , June 1 2016 <p> Jo McCarroll <p> Some very confused fruit trees are already blossoming in Auckland . <p> It 's the 1st of June - officially the start of winter . Or is it ? <p> NZ Gardener magazine staff report that gardeners all over New Zealand have been telling them stories of muddled seasons , with blossom already in Auckland , blackbirds mating in Hawkes Bay and deciduous trees stubbornly staying green . <p> Warmer , extended autumn and abnormal rainfall can affect everything from bird migration to insect activity and of course plant growth . One gardener tells of harvesting green beans this week from seeds sown in early autumn . <p> In his book Sprinter and Sprummer , Melbourne botanist Professor Tim Entwisle argues that New Zealand and Australia should have five seasons because our weather and the way plants and animals respond to it , is markedly different to the Northern Hemisphere . <p> In many parts @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ confined to June and July . And because blossoms are appearing on trees earlier and earlier each year - Prof Entwisle calls this " spring creep " - he proposes August and September have their own season : " sprinter " . October and November are increasingly changeable too , so he 's coined the word " sprummer " to define these months . So the seasons would go : June and July ( winter ) ; August and September ( sprinter ) ; October and November ( sprummer ) ; December through March ( summer ) ; and April and May ( autumn ) . <p> " Bug Man " Ruud Kleinpaste has long argued that winter does n't truly begin until the winter solstice - " this year that 's 21 June at 10.34am ; and not a moment earlier " - because the earth is such a large mass it takes a long time to cool down . <p> " So there 's obvious lag , with late summer/autumn warmth that allows us gardeners to get a quick rise from seeds sown late in the season . " Expect more intense storms , more intense temperature swings between high and low , and gustier winds - in fact , we 've already seen this happening around the world . " <p> In New Zealand , the El Nino pattern that makes it drier on the east coast and wetter on the west will also happen under this climate disruption , he says . <p> " Already we are growing subtropical plants such as kiwifruit and citrus in southern latitudes . Another example is that we have mosquitoes that used to be found only in the North Island starting to get a foothold here in Christchurch . That 's the bad news ; on the upside though we 'll have more native insects down South . Everything is going to be confused . "
