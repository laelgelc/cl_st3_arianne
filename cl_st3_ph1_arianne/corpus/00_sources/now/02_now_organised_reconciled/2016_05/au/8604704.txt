<h> How to rank the world 's nations by risk <p> Many of Australia 's island neighbours are vulnerable to disaster , with foreign aid playing a ' key part ' in whether national infrastructure can stand up to the challenges posed by nature . RN Breakfast reports . <p> Measuring risk on the international scale is a fiendishly complex business , but according to Matthias Garschagen , author of a report by the United Nations University in Germany , it essentially boils down to two components . <p> On the one hand , he says , is a country 's exposure to natural hazards . ' How many people are exposed to floods , to typhoons , to earthquakes ? ' <p> Not all of the immediate risks and threats that we see can be simply blamed on climate change . <p> Matthias Garschagen , United Nations University <p> On the other is the society 's inherent vulnerability . ' In other words : is the society prepared for the disaster when it strikes ? ' <p> It 's this risk matrix that explains why a nation @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ flooding -- is not estimated to be ' high risk ' . <p> ' Japan is a good example that shows a large difference between those two components , ' Garschagen says . <p> ' If you just take the exposure side , Japan would rank as one of the highest countries globally -- it 's in the comparison between the two components when you see what really needs to be done about risk . ' <p> Risky business for the Asia-Pacific region <p> The problem for many of the island nations listed in the World Risk Index is that they score highly on both sides of the ledger . <p> ' On the one hand you have natural hazards , but at the same time you also have countries with major development difficulties , ' Garschagen says . ' Hence those turn into higher base-level vulnerabilities .
