<p> A little bit of context can go a long way to understanding budget figures and policy costings . What 's the timeframe ? Is this new or recurrent funding ? Is that graph accurate ? It 's time to make the maths a bit clearer , writes Matthew Bevan . <p> Numbers are really hard , you guys . <p> I 'm one of those people who waded into year 10 maths thinking , " I 'm never going to need any of this crap ! " then waded into adult life frustrated I had n't been listening properly . <p> This week , Australian voters are going to have to do a bit more maths than usual , while they process any changes to the budget tonight , followed by the numbers-based election promises of both major parties . <p> Of the 78 promises made by the Coalition before the 2013 election being tracked by the ABC 's Fact Check , almost all of them involved a significant financial aspect , whether it was new spending , new cuts , new taxation , or just the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ election campaign will be the same . Even the legalisation of same-sex marriage , which would cost the government almost nothing to implement , now has a price tag of at least $100 million due to the expense of a proposed plebiscite . <p> So it 's up to us , the voters , to process the numbers , do our own cost/benefit analysis and make a decision at the ballot box . <p> With that in mind , I 'd like to suggest some rules for politicians and journalists to make it easier for voters to understand policies and budget measures by providing a bit more context for their numbers . <h> 1 . Give timeframes <p> Please tell me consistently whether the number you 're giving is per year , over the next four years , or between now and 2050 . When politicians and reporters describe a budget blowout , for example , whoever is in government might say the budget will be over target by $4 billion , while the opposition might say it will be over by $21 billion . <p> Both of them may be @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ budget for the next financial year , while the other is describing the budget forecast over the next four years . This is confusing for your average punter who is only paying passing attention to the sound bite . <p> It can be even more confusing if you change the timeframe you refer to from one press conference to another - in one statement giving a per-annum figure and in another providing a figure projected across the forward estimates period . <p> Another issue is big projects . The $50 billion submarine build announced last week seems like a colossal amount of money . <p> It 's being touted as one of Australia 's biggest ever infrastructure projects , and one of the world 's biggest defence contracts . <p> The $50 billion might seem a little less , however , if you imagine it being spread out over a couple of decades . It only averages out at about $2.5 billion annually , assuming the last submarine comes off the slipway in 2036 . Compare this figure with the Grattan Institute 's figure on negative gearing - they say the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ policy . <h> 2 . Stop making big numbers sound small and vice versa <p> Tone really matters in statements . When you see a politician campaigning in his or her local seat , it 's amazing to see how they can make a $1.5 million spend on a local hospital sound like a bigger deal than a $1.5 billion cut to hospital funding . <p> While I 'm fully aware that at the local level a well-sold 20 grand on the roof of a leaky PCYC basketball centre can drown out coverage of a $20 million upgrade to a police station , that does n't mean journalists need to report them equally , even if one announcement comes with pictures of a minister sinking a three-point bank shot and the other comes with pictures of a boring press conference . <h> 3 . Stop the re-announcements <p> It happens all the time and both major parties do it - announcing a great funding initiative like it 's a brand new lump of spending , when it 's actually just a re-announcement of money that maybe did n't get the attention @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ can also be done by announcing a big funding initiative - a $300 million environment policy , for example , then waiting a month or two to announce a specific $20 million part of it as though it 's new money . <p> For modern journalists it can be difficult to find information on what has and has n't been announced . There is n't a central register of funding announcements ( maybe there should be ) and in the rush of turning a funding announcement into a news story it 's easy to miss the fact that something identical or very similar was announced 18 months ago . <h> 4 . Provide benchmarks <p> One billion dollars is pretty meaningless for an average voter . They will never be involved with the management of that much money ( whether in a personal or commercial sense ) and it 's difficult to compare large funding announcements like that to anything they experience in day-to-day life . <p> It may be helpful for voters to compare the cost of a new announcement to a service they 're already familiar with . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ TV channels , an online TV streaming service , four national broadcast radio networks , 60 local radio stations , a multitude of digital offerings , and the most trusted news service in the country . <p> The Department of Defence turns about $32 billion a year into a high tech Army , Navy and Air Force with about 58,000 active service personnel and a large amount of hardware . <p> You can pick whatever comparison point you want , but comparing new spending or tax increases to something the public can value may assist in their understanding of the importance of any announcements . <h> 5 . Do n't put out bad charts <p> This is the most egregious of all numerical sins . It 's very , very easy to draw a chart to argue whatever you want . <p> For example , it 's easy to " prove " global warming is n't happening by having a limited date-range on your X-axis . Look ! The temperatures on this chart are going down ! Never mind the fact that the chart only shows the last five years in @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ would be very easy to show a massive spike in unemployment by limiting the range of figures in your Y-axis . Look ! The line on the right is much higher than the line on the left ! Unemployment is outta control ! <p> These charts flood TV screens and Facebook timelines constantly , and they can be extremely misleading . <p> It is not always wrong to limit the range of your axes , but it is undoubtedly wrong to use the mere existence of charts to convince people of your point . <p> Now , I do n't actually expect politicians to follow any of these rules . I also doubt most of the media has the time or inclination to put announcements like these into context . <p> But when you listen to an announcement or look at a chart before making a decision as to who you 're going to vote for this year , it might be worth imagining an emotionless voice telling you about the announcement , then figure out a timeframe , try to remember if you 've heard the announcement before , and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ can understand . <p> Or just vote for the leader with the better voice/suits/DJ skills/zingers/hairstyle - it is a democracy after all . <p> Matthew Bevan is a producer , reporter and newsreader for RN Breakfast . <h> Comments ( 57 ) <h> Nova4avr : <p> Thank you for this article &amp; it hits the nail right on the head .
