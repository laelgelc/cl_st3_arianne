<h> New government seems willing to face big agricultural challenges <p> PETER MCDONALD <p> Last updated 13:27 , November 7 2017 <p> MARTIN DE RUYTER/STUFF <p> New Minister of Agriculture Damien O'Connor . <p> OPINION : The night Winston Peters announced that Labour would lead the new government the anguish for many may have been so great , that some may not have gone to bed for fear of not waking the next morning . <p> The anger was brief and has now given way to acceptance to some degree . What 's most encouraging is than there seems to be some " green shoots " emerging around a willingness to confront some big questions that the agricultural sector will face in the next decade or so . <p> Damien O'Connor , the new Minister for Primary Industries , has already highlighted some of these threats or opportunities depending on how we wish to approach them . The minister 's early intent is encouraging as we have been ambling along as a sector without effective co-ordination and collective leadership for some time . <p> This previous lack of governmental direction @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ many ways is reflective of the static nature of the farming leadership we now have . <p> I see three immediate challenges for our primary sector . All three , on face value , seem separate but the deeper you dig the linkages become apparent . <p> The environmental discussion which has had an intense airing has polarised many . Farmers especially have been caught unaware of how this issue has resonated with urban New Zealand . <p> With a greater farmer buy-in throughout the country , incremental improvements will build . I 'm confident that in time positive , as opposed to negative trends will be the norm . <p> In saying this , consequences will present themselves in a way that will stress our conventional methods of farming . Future land use systems will be designed specifically for sensitive catchments . The totally growth focused mantra of the conventionally dominated farming sector will be resigned to history . <p> Much has been written and talked about non ruminant based proteins of late . There seems to be some " Kodak thinking " going on with some farming leaders @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . Their speed of industrial advance as opposed to our slow genetic progress will be astonishing . <p> In their wish to establish themselves they will force our traditional customers to question our systems which may be confronting . Most importantly we must be able to clearly demonstrate the integrity of our farming models . <p> The third - and most likely our biggest challenge - is our reaction to climate change as a sector . <p> The front end is energy use ; on farm mechanisation has increased as we have intensified . Many conventional farms have complex high input systems that demand increasing amounts of energy to operate . <p> The back end literally is animal emissions . Solutions may be on the farm , possibly from farm forestry as an off set or de-stocking to balance any carbon budget . <p> Methane inhibitors in the form of a vaccine may help bridge the gap but I 'm doubtful if it would be seen as a long term solution . <p> The potential role of genetically engineered cultivars may provide the long term solutions that we seek to @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of GE could do irreversible damage to our high value markets . <p> The growth and production narrative that has been " thrashed " my entire farming career may be replaced with getting the same from less , or less from less to remain profitable . <p> The magnitude of these three challenges demands a new standard of farming leadership that is collective and strategic as opposed to adversarial . <p> Peter McDonald is a Dipton sheep and beef farmer and one-time chairman of Meat Industry Excellence .
