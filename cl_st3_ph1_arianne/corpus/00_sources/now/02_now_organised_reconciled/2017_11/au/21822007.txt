<h> Measuring the heat beneath the ice <p> A new map of Antarctica , showing the varying levels of heat flux below the surface of the ice . <p> Martos et al . <p> Scientists studying magnetic signatures in Antarctic rocks are peering beneath the frozen continent 's ice sheets to make a detailed map of the amount of heat seeping from the Earth 's interior at various locations beneath it . <p> In the process , they have not only bettered our picture of Antarctica 's geology , but improved knowledge of how the escaping heat might affect the ice sheets ' base layers , making them more or less slippery . That 's important in understanding how the ice may react to climate change . <p> The new map combines 50 years of magnetic measurements taken from aeroplanes , satellite observations , and data from marine research vessels , says Yasmina Martos , a geophysicist at NASA Goddard Space Flight Centre and the University of Maryland , USA , who was with the British Antarctic Survey when the research was conducted . <p> The goal , she says @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , but to measure the intrinsic magnetism of buried rocks . <p> Ferromagnetic minerals in these rocks hold magnetic signatures that produce measurable anomalies . But these minerals lose their magnetism when heated above a critical level , known as the Curie temperature , something that occurs a few tens of kilometres beneath the Earth 's surface . <p> By using these magnetic anomalies to determine the thickness of the magnetised layer , the scientists were able to distinguish between regions where the Curie depth occurred close to the surface and those in which it was as far as 60 kilometres down . From that , they were able to calculate -- and map -- the amount of heat escaping from the Earth 's interior . They mapped base of the ice to a resolution of about 15 kilometres , substantially better than in any prior study . <p> That 's important because the escaping heat melts the bottom layer of ice , in effect lubricating its flow . <p> " Geothermal heat is important to understanding conditions at the ice bed as it controls sliding processes , " says @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ unknown . Even a little melting at the base helps the ice sheet slide faster . " <p> Mapping the differences , she adds , helps scientists understand which parts of Antarctica are the most unstable , including " which parts are more probable to break , melt , or disappear . " <p> Conversely , pinpointing areas of low heat flux is important because these help stabilise the ice sheet . They are also important to scientists wanting to take core samples to study climate cycles from hundreds of thousands of years ago . " Such areas are important for the search for the oldest ice preserved on the planet , " Martos says . <p> Richard Alley , a geoscientist at Pennsylvania State University , University Park , in the US , likes the new study but cautions against misinterpreting it . <p> Heat flux from beneath the ice , he says , is not a direct contributor @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , barring a volcanic eruption ( which would only be a local effect , anyway ) , but the amount of heat is small . <p> The amounts of flux measured in the new study , for example , ranged from about 30 to 150 milliwatts per square metre . " Solar energy absorbed by the planet is about 240 watts per square metre , " Alley says . <p> But heat is important to the movement of the ice and is difficult to measure beneath several kilometres of ice . <p> " People sometimes have energy audits done on homes , looking for how much heat is leaving , " Alley says . " Measuring geothermal flux under Antarctica is a lot harder . " <p> The new research wo n't be the last word , he says , but is definitely an important advance . " I believe it will be influential and help us make better models of the ice sheet , " he says .
