<p> Lately we have been subjected to Prime-ministerial statements on energy policy that jump from Snowy 2.0 to propping up aged coal-fired power stations in NSW , to government support for a new " clean " coal power station in Queensland and back to pumped hydro in Tasmania . Long term strategy seems to have gone missing . <p> The latest announcement is a feasibility study of pumped hydro in Tasmania supported by ARENA . <p> Is investing in Tasmania as the ' battery of the nation ' likely to be a sensible idea ? <p> The mainland grid would certainly benefit from more large scale renewable generation backed by storage . Implementing this would require both a bipartisan consensus on closing down aged coal infrastructure and a long term policy in support of low emission renewable energy . <p> Sensible , bipartisan , long term planning does n't appear likely to break out any time soon . Even if it did , the next questions are what is the best form of storage and where should it be located ? <p> Pumped hydro is the most cost-effective form of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ climate , and in some locations , significant investment in transmission infrastructure . <p> Snowy 2.0 does have the advantage of being well connected to the NSW and Victorian grids . If the national battery is located in Tasmania it would require a billion dollar second interconnector to the mainland . <p> The sorts of big national project preferred by politicians are not the only solution . Our electricity system is rapidly moving from centralised energy generation to distributed generation and storage . <p> CSIRO and the Australian electricity network operators have developed one of the most credible scenarios for the future of the grid . <p> It anticipates that by 2050 , 30-45% of our electricity would come from customer owned generators . The plan identifies the need for incentives to ensure that customer battery systems provide benefits to the network as well as to customers . <p> A recent ANU study has identified 22,000 potential sites for off-river pumped storage around Australia in a range of sizes . Only a few of these are likely to provide viable but they offer possible advantages in being smaller investments that @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ need for network enhancements . <p> If there is a role for large scale pumped hydro storage , is Tasmania likely to be the most cost effective place to build it ? <p> As Everett Dirksen never actually said , " A billion here , a billion there , pretty soon , you 're talking real money " . At over $1bn for a second interconnector , $2bn for a 600 MW wind farm on King Island or over $1bn for the Robbins Island and Jim 's Plain wind farms , and Hydro Tasmania 's estimate of $5bn to build 2500 MW of pumped storage , we are talking ' real money ' . <p> And it is ultimately our money , whether the infrastructure is built as a regulated asset ( added to our electricity bill ) , by government grant ( our taxes ) or by private investment ( including our super ) . <p> Investments on this scale take the best part of a decade to plan , fund and build , and are paid for by users over a 40 year period or more . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ most cost effective way to meet our energy security in an electricity market where the significant trends are to increased energy efficiency , local generation and storage , and demand management . <p> The detailed analysis of pumped hydro funded by Hydro Tasmania and ARENA will be a welcome contribution to the public debate . But big schemes may well have had their day . <p> Hydro Tasmania dropped work on the King Island project and the Tamblyn report on the viability of a second interconnector was lukewarm on its viability to say the least . <p> My prediction is that the market will have provided decentralised solutions to the challenge of reliable , affordable clean electricity long before these big schemes see the light of day .
