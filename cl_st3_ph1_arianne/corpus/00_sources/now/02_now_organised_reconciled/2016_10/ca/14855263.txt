<p> For current and aspiring Alberta conservative politicians , the last two weeks appear to have bestowed an embarrassment of political riches . <p> Fresh on the heels of a poll showing two-thirds of Albertans oppose Premier Rachel Notley 's carbon tax , along comes Liberal Prime Minister Justin Trudeau to announce that Ottawa will impose its own carbon price -- a levy that will exceed Alberta 's by 2022 . <p> Jean declared that " Alberta needs to make it clear to Trudeau this will never be acceptable , " while Kenney expressed his disappointment " that Alberta 's NDP government wo n't join other provinces in fighting the job-killing Liberal carbon tax . " <p> While there 's likely an eager and considerable audience for such a message at the moment , it exposes both gentlemen to the obvious question that neither can seem to answer : what would you do differently ? <p> For some opponents of carbon taxes , the answer is simple : do nothing . And while it may be easy for politicians to pander to that crowd , it 's not @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ committed to addressing emissions , then carbon pricing would appear to be the simplest and most efficient way of doing so . <p> For one thing , both Jean and Kenney have conceded that climate change is an issue and that greenhouse gas emissions need to be addressed . So if not carbon pricing , then what ? And how are these alternatives preferable to carbon pricing ? <p> Kenney tweeted last week his belief that a " carbon pricing scheme means bigger government , not lower emissions . " Yet Kenney has stated his own preference for " a prudent regulatory approach to reduce the carbon intensity of Alberta oil and gas , " which would seem to entail both bigger government and not lower emissions . <p> Conservatives often claim to oppose carbon taxes , based in large part on arguments from the environmental far left : because a price on carbon would have to be massive to ensure any significant emissions reductions . However , they then turn around and suggest that emissions reductions can be had easily , through some vague regulatory tweaking . Either emissions reductions @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ will still entail costs , and certainly those costs will undoubtedly be passed onto consumers . There 's no free lunch here . <p> Jean 's own emissions plan is also quite vague , but he has at least suggested the rough outlines of what could be a reasonable approach . Jean told Postmedia that if Ottawa 's carbon tax is going to stand , he would introduce " dramatic tax cuts " to offset it . This , however , seems to be more of a last resort strategy after exhausting all other avenues to try and derail Ottawa 's plan . <p> Why not , though , propose to scrap the Notley plan -- including the oilsands emissions cap and the green energy subsidies -- and propose to let the federal carbon price prevail , and that it will be made revenue neutral through offsetting tax cuts ? That should be preferable to any other proposed emissions reduction scheme . <p> If governments are committed to addressing emissions , then carbon pricing would appear to be the simplest and most efficient way of doing so . There may be @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ but there 's nothing wrong with conservatives advocating a more restrained approach . <p> Conservatives would do well to take a cue from economist Ross McKitrick , who wrote a study on carbon pricing last month for the University of Calgary 's School of Public Policy . He notes how " carbon pricing works because it is a market-based policy ... but that means the policymaker needs to let the market play its role . " <p> In other words , price carbon , but steer clear of damaging regulations and other government interference . <p> The fight against carbon pricing is ultimately a futile one . We need voices to advocate that we do so in a manner that encourages revenue neutrality and competitiveness .
