<h> The P&amp;ID saga <p> The $9.6 billion judgment sum that a British Commercial Court ordered Nigeria last month to pay a rather obscure Irish firm , Process and Industrial Development ( P&amp;ID ) , may go down in history as the highest penalty inflicted on Nigeria by the itinerant impostors who ruled Nigeria for six months when the late President Umaru Musa Yar'Adua became critically ill in 2009 . <p> The jettisoned deal required P&amp;ID to build a gas processing plant in Calabar , Cross River state to refine wet gas from Nigeria 's oil fields and haul it to the nation 's power generating plants for use in generating electricity . <p> The federal government had the responsibility of building the gas pipelines . <p> The deal was jettisoned when then Vice-President Goodluck Jonathan who was schemed out of the transaction by the power usurpers eventually became acting president on February 9 , 2009 . He spoted gross irregularities in the deal and ordered renegotiation . By 2019 , interest on the award had pushed the sum to $9.6 billion . <p> The penalty which the arbitration panel engaged by P&amp;ID imposed on Nigeria was converted last month into a court judgment by Justice Butcher of a British Commercial Court who authorised P&amp;ID to impound Nigeria 's commercial assets worth that amount . The penalty is a crushing blow to Nigeria at a time of mounting debts and dwindling revenue . In terms of value , the sum is almost twice what is needed to build the illusive east-west rail line from Calabar to Lagos . <p> The sum awarded P&amp;ID is unacceptably high . In terms of damages , if Nigeria is compelled to pay the $9.6 billion , it would inflict devastating negative impacts on the economy . The first casualty would be the exchange rate of the naira . <p> The payment would deplete the nation 's foreign reserves to the extent that the Central Bank of Nigeria ( CBN ) would no longer be able to defend the naira effectively . That would automatically fuel inflation . <p> It would also inhibit the country 's ability to attract foreign investment . With the exchange rate of the naira plummeting and foreign reserves depleted , foreign investors ' perception would be that if they enter the economy they would lose money as the naira would no longer be stable . <p> The development would also affect Nigeria 's credit rating in the international money market . With a volatile oil market , lean foreign reserves and fluctuating exchange rate , Nigeria would only borrow at higher interest rate as high risk borrower . If it is borrowing now at seven per cent , the rate could be adjusted well above 10 per cent . <p> The Nigerian capital market would not be spared the negative impact of the dollar drought that the penalty would conjure .
