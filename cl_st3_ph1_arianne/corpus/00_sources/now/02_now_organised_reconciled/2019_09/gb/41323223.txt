<p> It 's no secret to residents living on the Yorkshire Coast that the landscape they know and love is disappearing fast . <p> Countless experts have warned that climate change threatens to displace thousands of residents from their homes in the coming years as coastal erosion speeds up to a faster rate than ever before . <h> Cliff erosion at Kilnsea Caravan Park in the East Riding of Yorkshire <p> Lost settlements <p> What fewer people realise , however , is that coastal erosion is no new problem in this region . <p> Since the Roman era , historians have documented dozens of hamlets and villages along the East Coast which have already disappeared , now lying beneath the waves of the North Sea . <p> The most complete account of these lost towns and villages was written by Thomas Shepphard in 1912 , who used old accounts and map drawings to figure out the rough locations of these settlements claimed by the sea . <h> A sign warning of coastal erosion in Hornsea , which has lost at least one village to the sea since the Roman period in Britain . <p> By the time of his @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ 30 or so settlements had been washed away , some of which had once played important roles in the fishing , farming and trade industries of the region . <p> Ravenser Odd and Ravenspurn <p> Undoubtedly the most significant of the settlements lost to the waves was Ravenser Odd and Ravenspurn . The latter even gets a mention in Shakespeare 's Richard II with the eponymous King speaking of a place called " Ravenspurg " . <p> It 's believed that the settlements lay a mile or so east from Spurn Point , on a sandbank on the mouth of the River Humber . <h> A lighthouse at Spurn Point . Ravenser Odd and Ravenspurn once lay a stone 's throw from this site before they were consumed by the sea . <p> Ravenser Odd was founded around 1235 and was the first port of entry to travellers coming up the Humber from the North Sea , making it an extremely important fishing , shipping and trading point . <p> As a result , the town grew quickly , establishing a court , chapel , prison , gallows , warehouses @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ ships and men for King Edward I 's wars with Scotland . <p> But by 1340 accounts show concerns about the impact of flooding on the town , and a few years later around 200 buildings had been lost . <p> Townfolk reported that the final destruction came when the chapel was attacked by the North Sea , upturning the bones of the dead buried there . Most fled to nearby settlements like Hull and the once-bustling town was claimed by the sea . <p> Auburn <p> Auburn or Aborn was a small village within the civil parish of Barmston and Fraisthorpe that was abandoned in the 16th century due to coastal erosion , with the village reduced to just one farm of around 200 acres and a cottage . <p> The church was dismantled in 1590 and rebuilt further inland , but was demolished finally in 1731 due to the sea once again encroaching . <p> Hornsea Beck <p> Another settlement lying close to the sea in Hornsea , Hornsea Beck was a small settlement recorded to exist as early as 1367 . <p> By 1747 however , it @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ estimated 38 houses falling into the sea . <p> An anonymous poem was said to have been found in Hornsea referencing the encroaching sea , reading : <p> " Hornsea steeple , when I built thee , <p> Thou was 10 miles off Burlington , <p> 10 miles off Beverley , and 10 miles off sea . " <p> Owthorne and Old Withernsea <p> Legend has it that the churches in Owthorne and the neighbouring town of Old Withernsea were built by two sisters who wanted to build a local church but could not agree on whether it should have a tower or steeple . <p> Both were eventually destroyed by the encroaching sea , with a huge storm in 1816 dealing the final blow to Owthorne church , destroying it along with large chunks of the town , and reportedly casting bodies from the graveyard into the sea . <p> Warning on rising sea levels <p> Along with dozens more , the stories of these lost settlements serve as a warning for the future of the Yorkshire Coast .
