<h> Drilling into the past to predict the future <p> Climate change is at the centre of a remarkable international drilling operation into Antarctica 's Ross Ice Shelf this summer . David Williams reports . <p> If your patient is seriously ill then making radiological scans , and analysing the resulting grainy images , can suggest the source of the problem . But there 's no substitute for surgeons actually opening them up and having a look . <p> The same applies to Antarctica 's Ross Ice Shelf , the world 's largest floating body of ice . <p> For several summers , scientists have been assessing what lies in front of the stalled Kamb Ice Stream , about 850 kilometres from Scott Base , near the " grounding line " , where the ice leaves land and starts floating . Radio waves have been fired into the ice and small explosives set off , creating " echoes " in black-and-white images much like those produced in hospitals . <p> The surveys near Kamb reveal a 600-metre-thick chunk of ice -- as deep as Wellington 's Mt Victoria tunnel is long . Collaborators include the United States ' space agency NASA , and China 's Jilin University . <p> There 's something for everyone -- engineers working the kilometre-long hot water drilling hose , as well as glaciologists , oceanographers , and paleoclimatologists . It 's a data treasure trove because the area is little explored . The ice shelf is about the size of France and the ocean cavity comparable to the North Sea . <p> There 's scant understanding of how the ocean water circulates because very few holes have been drilled through the shelf , says University of Otago associate professor Andrew Gorman , a marine seismologist who specialises in sea floor imaging .
