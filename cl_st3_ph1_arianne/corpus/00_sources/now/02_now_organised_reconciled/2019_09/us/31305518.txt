<h> Editorial : Climate fight of vital concern to Hampton Roads <p> A REPORT RELEASED one year ago by the U.N . Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change outlined the grave threat posed by global warming and the daunting steps needed at once to prevent an environmental catastrophe . <p> Scientists have long argued it is critical that an increase in average global temperatures not exceed 2 degrees Celsius above their pre-industrial ( circa 1850 ) levels . The purpose of the Paris climate agreement , drafted in 2015 and signed by 196 countries , was to limit warming to 1.5 degrees -- or just below that 2-degree threshold . <p> Advertisement <p> Extensive research conducted over decades concludes that represents the tipping point for the health of the global environment , a point at which the damage to fragile ecosystems may multiply and prove irreversible . <p> Many of the most calamitous effects of climate change -- widespread species extinction ; more destructive and frequent natural disasters ; diminished arable land and drinking water ; chaotic human migration -- are already happening . <p> And they are n't happening in distant lands , but here in Hampton Roads . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ systems forming in the Atlantic are more powerful and numerous , and less predictable . Heat waves are more punishing and inescapable to the most vulnerable . <p> At 2 degrees of warming , all of the above would be much worse . It is not hyperbole to say that it would compromise the viability of this region since the flooding from sea-level rise would render vast portions of Hampton Roads uninhabitable . <p> Preventing that fate will require an unprecedented undertaking by the international community to dramatically and rapidly cut CO2 emissions as well as other toxic pollutants that contribute to global warming . <p> The Paris agreement was meant to encourage that type of action , challenging each nation to set ambitious goals to cut emissions and establish firm plans to achieve them . Some nations -- Norway , Costa Rica and even India -- have done quite well . While others -- China , Russia and Saudi Arabia -- have fallen well short . <p> Then there is the United States . America should be a key player but instead will likely lurk along the sidelines . <p> Americans should not be satisfied with that . While the threat the world faces may seem insurmountable , there are changes to be made on the state level , in our communities and even in our homes and , collectively , we can make a difference . <p> Toward that end , a couple of developments in Virginia last week deserve mention . <p> On Tuesday , Gov . Ralph Northam signed an executive order to transform the commonwealth 's electric grid , reducing its dependency on fossil fuels and expanding the amount of energy from renewable sources . <p> Under that directive , the governor ordered the heads of several state agencies -- the Department of Mines , Minerals and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Department of Environmental Quality -- to develop a plan for Virginia to generate 30% of its energy from carbon-free sources by 2030 and 100% from renewable fuels by 2050 . <p> Northam also ordered that electricity purchased by the state increasingly come from carbon-neutral sources -- 30% by 2022 , up from 8% now -- and that government facilities be made more energy efficient to reduce consumption . <p> The governor 's executive order was followed days later by an announcement from Dominion Energy , which said it would build the nation 's largest offshore wind farm in the coming years -- expanding a two-turbine test project now under construction about 27 miles east of Virginia Beach . <p> Advertisement <p> When the full $7.8-billion project outlined last week is completed , the company says it will be able to generate about 2,600 megawatts of electricity by 2026 , sufficient to power 650,000 homes . Importantly , it complements Northam 's plan for the electric grid . <p> While both plans were generally well received , some activists criticized both the governor and Dominion for not going far enough . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ they complained , and Dominion 's determined push to construct new pipelines tarnishes any luster on its green-energy investment . <p> Those critics right point to the scope of the problem -- that IPCC report -- and conclude that even these leaps do n't go far enough to do what 's needed to save communities such as Hampton Roads from the climate calamity to come . <p> Still , this is progress . And given the federal government 's refusal to lead the charge against climate change , then leadership by state governments and even large energy corporations is arguably the best hope of producing results here . <p> Yes , that 's frustrating -- it 's infuriating , in truth , to see the White House and congressional leaders brush aside the scientific evidence before them -- but it would be a mistake to allow the perfect to be the enemy of the good here . <p> Similarly , it would be a mistake to believe that what the governor or Dominion or anyone else has announced or yet done will be enough to keep average global temperatures from @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ change effectively requires action every day , from making energy efficient decisions for your home to walking or biking more . It involves decisions about how to travel , what to eat , how to spend and , obviously , for whom to vote . <p> It 's dangerous and tempting to say it 's too much , that we 're too late . The numbers are certainly grim . But we must believe that the global population , working toward a common purpose , can do enough , not only to avoid catastrophe , but to build a more vibrant , sustainable world . <p> When the United Nations opens its climate conference on Monday , let it be imbued with that type of optimism and conclude with the hope needed to sustain the massive fight to come .
