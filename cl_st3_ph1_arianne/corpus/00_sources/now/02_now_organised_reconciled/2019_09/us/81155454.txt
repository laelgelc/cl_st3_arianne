The arguments vary , but a key conclusion is that the burdens of responding to climate change should be divided equitably -- not borne primarily by the poor . <p> This notion of " common , but differentiated responsibilities " is a fundamental principle of equity outlined in the 1992 United Nations climate change treaty , which laid the groundwork for the many international climate negotiations that have occurred since . <p> Philosophers like Henry Shue have laid out the reasons that wealthy countries like the United States are morally bound not just to significantly cut their own carbon emissions but also help other countries adapt to a changing climate . That includes contributing financially to the development of climate-friendly energy sources that meet the pressing and near-term basic needs of developing countries . <p> Historically , wealthy countries have contributed the most and benefited the most from fossil fuel emissions @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and institutional capacity to shift away from fossil fuels . <p> Meanwhile , poor countries are often most vulnerable to climate impacts like rising seas , more intense storms and eroding coastlines . <p> For these reasons , many environmental ethicists hold , wealthy high-emitting countries should lead the way on mitigation and finance international climate adaption . Some even argue that rich countries should compensate affected countries for the climate loss and damage . <p> Practical , not ethical <p> Political leaders tend to dodge questions of ethics in their policymaking and global debates on climate change . <p> According to Stephen Gardiner , a philosopher at University of Washington , climate policy often focuses on " practical " considerations like efficiency or political feasibility . <p> U.S. climate negotiators in particular have for decades pushed back against ethically grounded differentiated responsibilities and resisted top down mandatory emissions cuts , seeking a more politically palatable option : Voluntary emissions cuts determined by each country . <p> And some legal scholars say a climate policy based not on ethics but on self-interest might be more effective . <p> University of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ so far as to suggest , on efficiency grounds , that developing nations should pay wealthy countries to emit less , since poorer and more vulnerable nations have more to lose as a result of the climate crisis . <p> The kids are n't buying it <p> Young activists like Greta Thunberg are reversing the marginalization of ethics from climate conversations . <p> With their focus on challenging " systematic power and inequity " and respect and reciprocity , they recognize that virtually all decisions about how to respond to climate change are value judgments . <p> That includes inaction .
