<p> The concern of environmental justice has been rising in prominence for years , and it 's little wonder why . Across the United States , people of color are exposed to more air pollution than whites , according to Environmental Protection Agency scientists . <p> And that 's despite University of Minnesota research showing that white Americans , on average , consume more pollution-heavy goods and services than black Americans do , and yet tend to live in areas with less air pollution . White Americans experience about 17% less air pollution than their consumption causes . By comparison , black populations are exposed to around 56% more fine particulate matter pollution than is caused by their consumption . For Hispanics , that exposure jumps to 63% . <p> Further , extreme weather events like heat waves and hurricanes are made far worse by our planet 's warming climate . And black and brown communities are often most vulnerable . Many communities of color are in low-lying areas that are more prone to flooding and other dangers associated with hurricanes . <p> Counties with large black populations are already exposed to two to three more extreme heat days @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Concerned Scientists , compared to counties with smaller black populations . By midcentury , that number is expected to jump to 20 more extreme heat days . <p> What many do n't realize is that climate change also impacts the air we and our children breathe . As the climate changes , it increases the likelihood that ozone , particle and other air pollutants will get worse , according to the American Lung Association . Warmer air temperatures make conditions more likely for smog , an irritant the American Lung Association warns " acts like a sunburn on your lungs which may trigger an asthma attack . " And longer warm weather seasons will mean longer smog seasons . <p> That 's bad news for everyone , and especially bad news for children of color living with lung disease in the Dallas area . According to a study by the Nature Conservancy , children in North Texas have higher asthma rates than the rest of the country . And it should come as no surprise that those asthma rates get worse depending on the socioeconomic and racial makeup of a @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ where more than 17% of students have asthma , all but two were in the low-income , predominantly minority neighborhoods south of Interstate 30 . <p> Given the severity of this matter , we would expect national leaders to do everything in their power to respond to climate change for the tremendous danger it poses to our health and our communities . Unfortunately , that is not the case for the Trump administration . President Donald Trump 's appointees at the Environmental Protection Agency are rolling back key regulations that could intensify climate impacts . <p> Now is the time to demand presidential candidates prioritize climate change and climate equity in their platforms . Additionally , we must join the emergence of black voices leading the way on this issue and fighting for a healthier future . For example , Rep. Don McEachin , D-Va. , a respected member of the Congressional Black Caucus and the leading voice on environmental justice matters , has a plan that sets a national goal to achieve a clean energy economy by 2050 . This is the sort of bold action that could protect @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ worst impacts of climate change , health or otherwise . <p> Our country has a moral obligation to maintain environmental quality for everyone -- no matter their zip codes . Clean air and drinking water should not be a luxury . <p> Aubrey Hooper is president of the Dallas Branch of the NAACP and assistant secretary of the NAACP 's national board of directors . He wrote this column for The Dallas Morning News .
