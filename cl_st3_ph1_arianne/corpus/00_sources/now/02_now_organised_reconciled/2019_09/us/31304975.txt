<p> When it comes to the hurricane season , 2014 predictions promise a rather average season . But already NOAA is admitting that global warming or climate change do not play a large part in their hurricane predictions as of this moment . <p> Based upon the conference call given by NOAA today , the 2014 hurricane season for the entire Atlantic ocean , not just Florida , will include between eight and 13 named tropical storms , three to six hurricanes , and one to two major hurricanes that are category 3 or higher . The outlook also " calls for a 50 percent chance of a below-normal season , a 40 percent chance of a near-normal season , and only a 10 percent chance of an above-normal season . " <p> Of course , Kathryn Sullivan , Ph.D. , NOAA administrator , warns that if even one named storm hits a major city then everyone will remember that hurricane : <p> " Thanks to the environmental intelligence from NOAA 's network of earth observations , our scientists and meteorologists can provide life-saving products like our new storm surge threat map and our hurricane forecasts . " <p> The likelihood of this hurricane season 's predictions being accurate is 70 percent based upon the computer models . <p> So where does global warming fit into all this ? As it turns out , it really does not . Gerry Bell , NOAA 's lead hurricane forecaster , said , " We need climate models that handle both global warming and year-to-year changes , and we 're just not there yet . " The reason he says this is because previous predictions related to climate change science have been way off . For example , back in 2007 the IPCC said that there was a greater than 50 percent certainty that human activity was contributing to an observed intensification of hurricane activity . Instead of increased intensity , we 've been experiencing a marked , and greatly appreciated , gap of time where we have not seen too many deadly named storms . <p> It 's said that NOAA 's forecast modeling has improved @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ " forecast models have generally been more reliable in tracking the paths of storms while struggling to accurately predict their intensity . For example , climate researcher Kerry Emanuel of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology published a study last year in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences(PNAS) , and he claims the computer models still predict global warming should produce more hurricanes : <p> " It is important to emphasize that most studies suggest that the frequency of the highest category tropical cyclones ... should increase as the globe warms .
