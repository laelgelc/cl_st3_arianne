<h> You 're probably using disposable wipes wrong . How to avoid harming the planet , the sewers <p> Disposable wipes : Pull one out of the container , wipe down the entire kitchen and bathroom , then flush it down the toilet , right ? <p> While the wipes are a convenient and easy-to-use product that serve a variety of helpful purposes , many Americans may be using them incorrectly -- and could be hurting the sewers , the planet and our health , experts say . <p> Whether it 's flushing a nonflushable wipe or using antibacterial products to excess , misusing disposable wipes that can create costly " fatbergs " or cause potentially long-term damage to the environment . <p> Here 's a look at how not to use the wipes , and easy alternatives that can make them work better in your life : <h> Do n't flush wipes if the package says not to <p> If manufacturers deem a product unsafe to go down the drain , it more than likely will be labeled on the packing . <p> While there is debate over whether wipes labeled as " flushable " are truly safe @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ wipes and disinfectant wipes are for your trash can . <p> " All wipes will easily flush down your toilet , but that 's where the unseen problems begin , " said Mike Saia , a communications manager at Charleston Water System in South Carolina . <p> The utility has had multiple cases of massive clumps of the wipes clogging sewers and creating costly headaches for customers . <p> In October , a massive 12-foot long clog of disposable wipes cost Charleston Water more than $140,000 in damage and cleanup costs . And then in June , another clog disrupted the sewage system , costing about $60,000 . <p> A mound of disposable wipes clogged the sewers in Charleston , South Carolina , in October 2018 , disrupting service for days and costing the local water utility more than $140,000 in damage and cleanup costs . When those clumps reach a choke point , like a pump at a wet well in Charleston , they can completely disrupt the system . <p> The root of the problem is that some disposable wipe products are specifically designed not to break down , says Chuck White , vice president of regulatory affairs at the Plumbing-Heating-Cooling Contractors Association . <p> Dave Rousse , president of the Association of the Nonwoven Fabrics Industry , also known by an old acronym INDA , agrees that most disposable wipes should be thrown in the trash . <p> While he says products that meet INDA 's standard to be labeled as flushable are safe for drains , he agrees that nonflushable ones , which make up the majority of the consumer wipe market , need to be tossed in the trash . <h> Do n't use them excessively <p> A common concern from many environmentalists is that wipes , like other disposable products such as plastic bags or water bottles , are simply wasteful .
