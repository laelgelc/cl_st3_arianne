<h> Why we need a pan-Canadian student nutrition program <p> Kirsty Duncan is a Liberal member of parliament ( Etobicoke North ) and critic for the Environment . She has a Ph.D . in geography ( University of Edinburgh , 1992 ) and has taught meteorology , climatology , and climate change at the University of Windsor , corporate social responsibility and medical geography at the University of Toronto and global environmental processes at Royal Roads University . She served on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change , an organization that won the 2007 Nobel Prize with Al Gore and is the author of Hunting the 1918 Flu : One Scientist 's Search for a Killer Virus ( University of Toronto Press , 2003 ) , and Environment and Health : Protecting our Common Future ( 2008 ) . <p> Canadian children returned to school this month , and many of them returned hungry ; 40 percent of elementary students and 62 percent of secondary school students do not eat a nutritious breakfast . Poor nutrition status leads to poor health outcomes for children , and Canadian children from all @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ one in five Canadian children who lives below the poverty line . <p> School nutrition programs are highly effective in providing children with nutritious diets . Research confirms the importance of such programs for those who participate , including better grades and health , increased motivation , improved likelihood of graduation , and decreased absenteeism and violence . <p> Despite the clear benefits , Canada remains the only G8 country without a national nutrition program . The 10-15 percent of nutrition programs that do exist survive because administrators and volunteers who operate them are convinced of the benefits . <p> The Conservative government shows no sign of changing course and improving student nutrition . Minister of Health Leona Aglukkaq 's office has said " We see education very clearly as a provincial/territorial jurisdiction , so it 's nothing that 's being considered by our government at this point in time . " This is extremely disappointing considering the recognition by Dr. David Butler-Jones , Chief Public Health Officer of Canada , who has stressed : " As a result of being hungry at school ... children may not reach their @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ health impact throughout their entire lives " . <p> The Minister of Health 's spokesman 's comment is meant to mislead Canadians . Better eating is not only about education , but rather , improving nutrition , grades , and health . Schools are merely the delivery mechanism , as this is where children and youth congregate . This is precisely the reason why , for example , the federal government gives money to the provinces and territories to deliver vaccine programs through schools . <p> If the Minister of Health does not speak for the health of children and youth , who gives a voice to the voiceless , and ensures that every child matters ? This is why we so desperately need a Children 's Commissioner in Canada ( as MP Marc Garneau is advocating ) , who is independent , and who can speak for the most vulnerable in society . The 2009 OECD report , " Doing Better for Children " , provides data on how much each country spends from government funding on children from birth to age 18 . Why were no data available @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Article 12 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child states : " Children have the right to say what they think should happen when adults are making decisions that affect them , and to have their opinions taken into account . " Our children and youth want to be asked , listened to , heard and respected ; and they want security , including access to safe and healthy food . What is the government 's position in ensuring that children and youth are seriously engaged in making decisions about their lives , especially on something as basic as access to food ? <p> If the government refuses to listen to our young people , perhaps it will listen to the economic argument for a pan-Canadian nutrition program . Recent research from the Toronto District School Board shows that high-school students who eat breakfast before or at school are more likely to graduate than those who do not . <p> The Boston Consulting Group reports that , on average , each high-school graduate contributes an extra $75,000 to the economy . They earn higher salaries than @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ healthcare costs , and are less dependent on social assistance . <p> If providing food at school increases graduation rates by only three percent , a pan-Canadian school meals program in high schools at a cost of $1.25 a day could result in an annual net payback of more than $500 million . <p> The potential economic stimulus for Canadian agriculture is also considerable . Realistically , seventy percent of a pan-Canadian nutrition program could have domestic content with an annual return to Canadian producers of $1.5 billion . As a general rule , for each dollar spent in a community , an additional two to three dollars would be generated through processing , storage , trucking , etc .. <p> Outside North America , school meals are considered an investment rather than a cost . In Sweden , children aged six to 16 receive a hot meal each day by law ; and in Japan , children aged six to 15 receive school meals , with 50 percent of the meals made with local ingredients . <p> In closing , I believe we need concerted and effective advocacy for @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ adequate and safe food , which is a right of every individual . This means , first and foremost , listening to the voice of the child .
