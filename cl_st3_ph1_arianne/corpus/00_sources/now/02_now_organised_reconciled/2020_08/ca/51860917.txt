The foundations of that political construct were designed at the Quebec Conference in 1864 . In that era , 84 per cent of Canadians lived in rural regions , and livelihoods were largely gained from agriculture and natural resources . Today , 82 per cent of Canadians live in our cities @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . Yet the old construct that envisioned communities as merely distant cousins of the federal government and provinces lingers , leaving cities with the greatest population and economic responsibility but without enough of the tools to meet the challenges that have come with demographic change . <p> In large part , this nation 's talent , while functioning in our municipalities , is bottled up in a theatre of operation that ca n't afford to resource those energies . The thrust of our greatest challenges -- climate change , immigration , commerce , research and development , education , health care , citizenship , information , financial markets and democracy -- is throbbing in our population centres in confines that hardly contain the resources to deal with them effectively . <p> In a governing design that sees the federal government protecting its hold over provinces and provinces , in turn , safeguarding their influence over local communities , the regions that are most populated and vibrant -- cities -- are left with the least choices while bearing the greatest load . <h> Advertisement <p> Story continues below <p> This advertisement @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <h> Article content continued <p> It is a constitutional quagmire that has not adapted to the times , leaving us to the whims of deeply partisan politics at senior levels that hardly reflect the collaborative and familial strengths of those places most Canadians call home . <p> Put bluntly , it 's time for this country to modernize its governing architecture to keep pace with its burgeoning talent and potential .
