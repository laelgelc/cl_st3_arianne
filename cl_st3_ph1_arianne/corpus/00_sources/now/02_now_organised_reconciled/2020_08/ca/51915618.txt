<p> One familiar factor of late summer is the constant reporting of extreme weather in the United States and the Asian Pacific . Of course , it is hurricane season -- typhoon season , if you live in the Pacific Rim . Every year , a series of storms start out in the middle of the ocean and march , like rampaging armies , one after the other , toward landfall . <p> Most thunderstorm events do not develop into hurricanes , because high winds in the jet stream blow the tops off of the forming thunderclouds and do not give them a chance to form . This is the reason thunderclouds are shaped like a blacksmith 's anvil as the tops are sheared off by winds that can reach over 400 km-h in the stratosphere . In mid-ocean hurricane development , this effect is minimized . One of the most destructive , but least talked about effects of these violent weather events , is storm surge , which happens when the winds from a hurricane or typhoon blow water towards the shore . The highest storm surge events happen when there is a full moon . This allows the gravitational effects of the sun and moon to be added to the extra water the storm blew in , resulting in high storm surge events . <p> The driving force for this weather is the sun . In late summer , the sun beats down on the ocean heating the water . This water starts to evaporate and take the sun 's heat into the atmosphere . As the water vapour condenses into clouds , the heat carried by the water vapour heats the air . <p> This effect creates a low pressure area in the centre of the storm and the winds start to move to the low pressure area . This problem only will get worse as icecaps melt and sea level gets higher . <p> Of course , this cycle can not continue when the hurricane makes landfall . The water vapour that is driving the system can not be supplied to the air because the hurricane no longer has the warm water to supply the vapour .
