<h> Own it . Tax it . Share it . How Norway runs its oil boom . <p> Momentum is building across Canada on the need to develop a sustainable national energy strategy . <p> On this front , Canada and Alberta , our main petro-province , have much to learn from another major petroleum producing and exporting country , Norway . <p> Canada and Norway are advanced industrialized countries with highly developed political , bureaucratic and economic institutions . <p> Norway and Alberta have similar population sizes , similar production profiles , and similar levels of dependence on petroleum exports and government petro-revenues . <p> During my recent trip to Norway , I found they have taken very different paths , and with very different outcomes . <p> In Norway there was , from the outset , a societal consensus that government should play the dominant role in the petroleum industry , both as owner and regulator . <p> The Norwegian government owns 80 per cent of petroleum production , and retains roughly 85 per cent of the net petroleum revenues , mainly through a 78 per cent oil @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Alberta and Canada , ownership and control have been controversial issues . Right now , virtually the entire industry is owned by foreign and domestic private interests , which have taken the lion 's share of the petroleum wealth . <p> According to a Parkland Institute estimate , the Alberta government has averaged just 9 per cent of the economic rent from the oilsands over the last 15 years . And the federal government now takes ( after tax breaks ) a paltry 7 per cent of oil company revenues through the general corporate income tax . <p> The Norwegian government has been very effective in distributing the benefits of oil wealth both regionally and throughout its population , thanks to a generous social welfare system , a progressive tax system and an equitable labour relations system . <p> Strong unions ( 70 per cent of the workforce is covered by collective agreements ) are able to negotiate centralized wage settlements with employers ' organizations . And Canada 's super-rich are increasingly concentrated in Alberta . <p> Norway , which had very high taxes even before the discovery of oil , chose not to reduce them . Norway has maintained a steady tax level of 43 per cent of GDP , among the highest in the industrial world . <p> Alberta has lowered its non-petroleum taxes to the point where they are by far the lowest in Canada . This overdependence on fluctuating petro-revenue has created chronic fiscal volatility in Alberta . It also has fostered a tax-cut competition that has put pressure on other provinces to lower @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ public services . <p> Canada has lowered overall tax levels since the late 1990s from 36 per cent to 31 per cent of GDP , placing it in the bottom third of OECD countries . <p> If Canadian governments had the same overall tax/GDP ratio as Norway , they would have an additional $200 billion a year to invest . Just bringing it up to Canada 's mid-1990s level would give our governments an extra $90 billion . <p> Interprovincial disparities are growing in the wake of the petro-boom . Alberta 's fiscal capacity -- its ability to raise revenue -- will likely exceed 180 per cent of the national average over the next eight years , even as federal-provincial redistribution measures have been weakened . <p> Norway 's Petroleum Savings Fund has amassed over $664 billion in assets , all invested abroad , with only the long-term 4 per cent return ( now approximately $27 billion per year ) used for domestic spending . Canada , unlike Norway , has not been able to maintain a stable dollar during the petro-boom . The 60 per cent rise against the U.S. dollar during 2002-08 wreaked havoc with manufacturing and other non-commodity exports , resulting in a huge loss of output and jobs . <p> As two of the world 's largest petroleum producers and exporters , Norway and Canada-Alberta have a profound responsibility to reduce the greenhouse gas emissions that are threatening the planet . <p> Norway is a leader in carbon emissions reduction , both at home and internationally . Under the Copenhagen Accord , Norway 's carbon reduction targets are the most ambitious in the industrial world . <p> In contrast , Canada 's federal government and its counterpart in Alberta are climate laggards . Their actions show they do not view carbon emissions reduction as a high priority , especially when compared to the development of the oilsands . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ even meet its much weaker Copenhagen commitments . It refuses to put a price on carbon and its regulations are weak . <p> Notwithstanding the differences between the two countries -- notably that Canada is a decentralized federation with divided jurisdiction over petroleum-related activities -- lessons can be drawn from the Norwegian experience . <p> First and foremost , the federal government needs to take the lead , collaborating with provincial , territorial and First Nations governments , in building a public consensus around a national energy strategy . <p> We need a strategy that addresses concerns about economic development , energy security , inequality , interprovincial disparities , and climate change -- and outlines a plan for the transition to a low carbon economy . <p> Failure to do so will only heighten existing tensions within the Canadian federation . <p> Bruce Campbell is executive director of the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives .
