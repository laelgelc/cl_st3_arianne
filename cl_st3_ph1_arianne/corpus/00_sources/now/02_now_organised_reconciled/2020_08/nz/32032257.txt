<h> Pumped storage and the intricate dance of supply and demand <p> OPINION : There is a lot of excitement about the recent announcement by Energy Minister Megan Woods that the government is allocating $30 million to develop a detailed business case for pumped hydro to address New Zealand 's dry-year storage problem . The analysis will focus on Lake Onslow in Central Otago , but will also include the assessment of smaller potential pumped storage options in the North Island , as well as " other alternative technologies " . <p> If Lake Onslow stacks up , this will be New Zealand 's biggest infrastructure project since the Think Big projects of the 1980s . The project has drawn immediate euphoric reactions due to its potential to create local jobs and underpin a close-to-zero-carbon electricity system . But its cost ( at least $4 billion ) and the undoubted opposition it will face on ecological grounds mean that careful evaluation is needed . <p> What exactly is the problem is it expected to solve ? But an analogy might help . <p> Think of an electricity system as a dance in which two partners must mirror each other 's moves , with intricate footwork and pirouettes guided by subtle arm pressures and a knowledge of each other 's habitual moves . <p> One of the partners is Supply and the other is Demand , and their interactions are largely ( but not entirely ) guided by the market and some highly complex regulations . It there is an unexpected change in routine , and demand and supply get out of kilter , and a blackout may follow , so the stakes are high . <p> For years , our dance partners have faithfully followed each other with scarcely a falter , but now both demand and supply are developing individual new techniques , while together aspiring to win a new competition . Will they get there without a disastrous and expensive tumble ? <p> The new competition is called net zero carbon by 2050 , which reflects New Zealand 's commitment to the Paris Agreement . Hydro currently supplies around 57 per cent of New Zealand 's electricity but major new hydro dams on rivers are unlikely due to the environmental costs . It is an open question whether some fossil fuel-powered generation needs to be retained as a back-up for when demand overshoots supply . <p> The Interim Climate Change Commission ( ICCC ) concluded that New Zealand would get to 93 per cent renewable generation by 2035 under current market conditions . But let 's turn our attention for a minute to demand , the quiet , relatively unknown partner who may have more to offer than we think . <p> Demand is the electricity consumption of all of New Zealand 's businesses , organisations and households . For the purposes of a balanced grid , the supply dance partner needs to know how demand will fluctuate over the hour , day , week and year , to keep in step . <p> The most important piece of knowledge is the peak demand , so as to be able to have the generation capacity in place . Households and businesses are acquiring electric vehicles , which are charged from the grid . Farm irrigation is becoming widespread and creates new demand profiles in rural areas . Heat pumps are increasingly used for both heating and cooling . Such trends are creating new patterns of demand . <p> And households are n't just consuming power . Increasing numbers are installing solar generation and feeding any surplus back into the grid and/or storage batteries . New markets are developing whereby businesses can be paid to reduce their demand at times when supply is not keeping up , and it is only a matter of time before so-called " demand response " becomes commonplace amongst households too . <p> On the near horizon are housing collectives becoming virtual power plants , and electric vehicles feeding into the grid when supply is stressed . Demand is n't well understood and its potential is definitely under-appreciated when it comes to system planning . <p> So , the dance partners are changing . Supply will be becoming much bigger but more variable because of reliance on sun , wind and water . And demand is becoming more complex and yet potentially more able to help , through demand response , when supply ca n't keep up . <p> On the smaller time-cycles of minutes , hours and days the combination of flexible hydro and flexible demand can enable our future supply and demand to dance in tandem . The big problem lies with winter peaks when demand is at its highest , and dry years when supply is at its lowest -- especially when these coincide . <p> Which is why the minister is proposing a hydro storage dam that can be filled through electrical pumping when there is plenty of water in the river , and discharged back down through generators when the hydro dams are low . It essentially works like a big battery within the electricity system @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <p> It is easy to get caught up in the excitement of growing supply even further in this way . It is the role of the business case to assess whether something of the scale of Onslow is needed , given other developments such as the likely closure of the Tiwai Point aluminium smelter which will free up around 13 per cent of New Zealand 's electricity supply , potentially giving more flexibility for dealing with dry years . But might demand have some other options that could also be worth considering ? <p> What if we invested to reduce peak demand rather than increase supply ? Winter peaks are driven by household demand for heating and lighting . New Zealand houses , even Homestar-rated ones , are typically cold and yet waste a huge amount of heat due to poor insulation and/or inefficient heating . Inspiration can be taken from Europe where new buildings and building retrofits are required to meet near-zero energy building standards . <p> Efficient lighting is another under-explored solution , with recent research indicating that a gradual uptake of energy-efficient lighting could reduce the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ by at least 500 MW ( 9 per cent ) by 2029 , with co-benefits including lower power bills for households . <p> Solutions such as these are potentially much less costly than a single Think Big project , and bring multiple benefits for health and wellbeing as well as lowering householders ' power bills . Alongside the $30 million being put into investigating pumped hydro , I suggest it is timely to also develop a parallel business case on the contribution that demand-side solutions could make . <p> As we head into the next few decades , both demand and supply will need to learn new steps -- becoming more flexible and more responsive to each other 's changing profiles . Maybe it is demand 's turn to shine . <p> Dr Janet Stephenson is the director of the University of Otago 's Centre for Sustainability .
