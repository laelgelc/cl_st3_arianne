<h> Researchers unlock secrets of the past with new carbon dating standard <h> Researchers unlock secrets of the past with new carbon dating standard <p> Scientists have improved the technique for assessing the ages of historical objects , meaning radiocarbon dating is set to become more accurate than it has ever been . <p> Researchers at the Universities of Sheffield , Belfast , Bristol , Glasgow , Oxford , St Andrews and Historic England , plus international colleagues , used measurements from almost 15,000 samples from objects dating back as far as 60,000 years ago , as part of a seven-year project . <p> Using the measurements they created new international radiocarbon calibration ( IntCal ) curves , which are fundamental across the scientific spectrum for accurately dating artefacts and making predictions about the future . <p> Radiocarbon dating is vital to fields such as archaeology and geoscience to date everything from the oldest modern human bones to historic climate patterns . <p> With this information archaeologists can restore historic monuments or study the demise of the Neanderthals . <p> Geoscientists on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change ( IPCC ) rely upon the curves to find out what the climate was like in the past to better understand and prepare for future changes . <p> Professor Paula Reimer , from Queen 's University Belfast and head of the IntCal project , said : " Radiocarbon dating has revolutionised the field of archaeology and environmental science . <p> " As we improve the calibration curve , we learn more about our history . <p> " The IntCal calibration curves are key to helping answer big questions about the environment and our place within it . " <p> The researchers have developed three curves dependent upon where the object to be dated is found . <p> The new curves , published in Radiocarbon , are IntCal20 for the Northern Hemisphere , SHCal20 for the Southern Hemisphere , and Marine20 for the world 's oceans . <p> Previous radiocarbon calibration curves , which were developed over the past 50 years , relied heavily on measurements taken from chunks of wood covering 10 to 20 years big enough to be tested for radiocarbon . <p> But advances in the testing methods mean the updated curves instead use tiny samples , such as tree-rings covering just single years , that provide previously impossible precision and detail in the new calibration curves . <p> Improvements in understanding of the carbon cycle have meant the curves now extend all the way to the limit of the radiocarbon technique 55,000 years ago . <p> Radiocarbon dating is the most frequently used approach for dating the last 55,000 years and underpins archaeological and environmental science . <p> First developed in 1949 , it depends upon two isotopes of carbon called stable 12C and radioactive 14C . <p> When alive , a plant or animal takes in new carbon , so has the same ratio of these isotopes as the atmosphere at the time . <p> But once an organism dies it stops taking in new carbon -- the stable 12C remains , but the 14C decays at a known rate . <p> By measuring the ratio of 14C to 12C left in an object the date of its death can be estimated . <p> If the level of atmospheric 14C were constant , this would be easy . <p> However , it has fluctuated significantly throughout history , and in order to date organisms precisely scientists need a reliable historical record of its variation to accurately transform 14C measurements into calendar ages . <p> Researchers say the new IntCal curves provide this link . <p> Alex Bayliss , head of scientific dating at Historic England , said : " Accurate and high-precision radiocarbon dating underpins the public 's enjoyment of the historic environment and enables better preservation and protection . <p> " The new curves have internationally important implications for archaeological methodology , and for practices in conservation and understanding of wooden built heritage .
