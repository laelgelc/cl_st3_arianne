<p> The UK 's favourite seafood might need to change as a new study finds that cod could stop being sold in restaurants . <p> Cod and monkfish can not keep up with the rate at which sea temperatures are rising because of climate change . <p> But other seafood favourites - including Dover sole , red mullet and John Dory - may become more plentiful as seas around Britain continue to warm , according to the findings . <p> Researchers , who analysed the future effects of climate change on fish stocks for south-west UK fisheries , said families may have to change their diets to protect threatened fish species . <p> Families in the UK may have to change what fish they eat as study reveals cod may not keep up with the warming seas . The Celtic Sea , English Channel and southern North Sea have experienced significant warming over the past 40 years <p> Researchers , who analysed the future effects of climate change on fish stocks for south-west UK fisheries , said families may have to change their diets to protect threatened fish species . <p> The study , which generated future projections of climate impacts on fish in a rapidly warming sea region , forecasts major changes in the availability and catchability of commercially important Atlantic fish species . <p> The Celtic Sea , English Channel and southern North Sea have experienced significant warming over the past 40 years , and further increases in sea temperatures are expected over the coming decades . <p> The study involved researchers from the University of Exeter , the University of Bristol , the Centre for Environment , Fisheries and Aquaculture Science ( Cefas ) and the Met Office Hadley Centre . <p> The team used computer models to look at how fish numbers may alter by 2090 under a range of future climates . <h> RELATED ARTICLES <h> Share this article <p> Share <p> Projections suggest increases in abundance of warm-adapted species red mullet , Dover sole , John dory and lemon sole , and decreases in abundance of cold-adapted species Atlantic cod , monkfish and megrim . <p> The researchers said declining species may need further measures to reduce their vulnerability to further warming temperatures . <p> Their findings , published in the Journal of Applied Ecology , also indicate implications not only for the wider ecosystem , but that the fishing industry and management systems will likely have to adjust their operations to address the changes . <p> The team said families may also need to also adapt their diets in future to eat species that could benefit under future warming , such as the warm-water species red mullet , Dover sole , john dory and squid . <p> Study lead author Dr Katherine Maltby , who undertook the research while at Cefas , said : ' Our results show that climate change will continue to affect fish stocks within this sea region into the future , presenting both potential risks but some opportunities that fishers will likely have to adapt to . <p> ' Consumers can help fishers take advantage of these fishing opportunities by seeking out other fish species to eat and enjoy . ' <p> Co-author Louise Rutterford , of the University of Exeter , added : ' We know from working with fishers that warmer water species are appearing in catches more . <p> ' Bringing together their ' on-the-ground ' experiences with studies like ours will help inform future management decisions that enable sustainable exploitation while supporting fishers ' adaptation .
