But other investors and consumers are purely selfish . <p> Their model applies to any type of business that can do harm , but the authors use environmental concerns as their working example . In other words , it depends on demand . <p> Furthermore the authors suggest , in line with evidence from experimental economics , unless the pollution is extremely harmful , it is not in the interests of any shareholders to actually exit . <p> So most shareholders wo n't exit -- or at least not enough to get companies to " behave " . <h> Getting to vote @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the authors consider a scenario where shareholders get to vote on whether a company should be clean or dirty . <p> Basic economics says an individual shareholder 's vote only matters if it is pivotal ( i.e. it affects the outcome ) . In such cases a vote will be based on weighing the net social benefit from the clean technology , and the importance of others ' well-being , against their individual financial loss resulting from choosing the cleaner , costlier technology . <p> But here 's the key thing . If shareholders have diversified investments , a vote about one company will make a minor difference to their overall returns . So as long as the shareholder cares at all about the welfare of others , they will likely vote for the socially optimal goal -- in this case , clean technology . <h> Corporate reforms <p> All of this suggests that making sure shareholders get to express their voice is important to achieving socially optimal goals . <p> That might involve more pro-shareholder measures , such as the opportunity to vote on issues the board traditionally decides @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ ultimate power is voting out directors who do n't listen to them . <p> There is a catch to this in practice , though .
