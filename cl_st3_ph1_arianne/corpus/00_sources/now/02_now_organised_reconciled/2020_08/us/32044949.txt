<p> An unprecedented August lightning storm and we are back : fire season 2020 . Images of masked farmworkers harvesting berries in smoky fields and brave farmers discing firebreaks emerge again on social media , with the hashtags #countystrong , #farmersareresilient , #wefeedyou . <p> Farmers are resilient . We are determined and passionate about our difficult work , and we are dedicated to feeding our communities despite the challenges inherent in food production . And in many ways , that resilience is a problem . <h> First Person <p> This is First Person , where members of the Bay Area food community voice their perspectives . Scott Chang-Fleeman runs Shao Shan Farm , a Marin County organic farm that he recently had to evacuate due to wildfires . <p> Read More <p> As I write this , my 5.5-acre vegetable farm is 4 miles away from an uncontained wildfire in west Marin County . Our town has been on evacuation warning for the last three days . I have been tearing myself up over my decision to keep my employees away from the farm and to pause operations while @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ town to wait and see how the fire develops . <p> There 's a pit in my stomach of guilt . I 've run through this scenario a dozen times , and I always chose to stay and defend my crops , equipment and infrastructure . I have a plan of where to set up my irrigation pump and hoses to spray down the barn . I have N95 respirators so I can continue to plant and harvest even as the air quality worsens , and I will deliver these CSA boxes , goddamn it . <p> But as the images and posts of burnt-out barns and fields from the Santa Cruz fires began to roll in , the idea of staying felt wrong on a deep level . The pride I felt in my determination for strength and resilience revealed itself to me as martyrdom . I began to question : Is this food system I am a part of truly sustainable if it is reliant on an annual display of a farmer 's ability to forgo mental and physical well-being for their commitment to " their " land @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ production despite the odds " trickle down to exploit farmworkers ? <p> Farmers are no strangers to challenges and hardships , and for many of us , the struggles and subsequent triumphs are what make farming such a rewarding career and lifestyle . I catch myself frequently chanting in my head , " head down , power through " when confronted with a physically demanding task or cleaning up a mess after a windstorm . But this propensity for hard work is not resilience . A food system that is reliant on a farmer 's passion and ability to relinquish their own physical and mental well-being is extremely fragile . <p> Every bone in my body is telling me to get back to my farm and keep working , telling me I 'm weak for leaving when it is only an evacuation warning and the smoke is n't that bad . Toxically masculine culture on farms has led to people taking pride when they push the limits of safe physical exertion . Instead , we need to start praising and encouraging our farmers to stop and take a break in these times . <p> We have conflated resilience with sacrifice , and usually that sacrifice comes from our mostly Latinx workforce . We have let the values of toxic masculinity override the humanity of our people . A food system that falls apart without people breathing smoke to get strawberries on the table is not resilient . <p> The resilience I want to cultivate for myself is OK with missing a farmers ' market so I can make phone calls to friends who lost their homes . But the fact that any farm feels the financial pressure to operate through an annual emergency speaks to the fragility of our food system as a whole . <p> True resilience in our farming system would be having the ability , social safety nets and financial security to leave your farm in the face of fires without fear of financial ruin from loss of market or assets . <p> Instead of responding with hashtags of strength , what would our solutions look like if we responded to devastation with calls for softness ? Instead of Instagram posts that normalize exploitation by thanking farmworkers for putting food on our tables during poor air quality , what if we demanded protections and supported decentralized food systems that prevent them from having to be out there in the first place ? <p> Our food system is built on strength , on our ability to impose ourselves upon @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ it . We will never be stronger than the fires . We will never be stronger than nature . And that is important to recognize . Instead of standing our ground , holding strong , and rebuilding things the way they were , what if we open ourselves to weakness and recognize our vulnerability in the face of anthropogenic climate change ?
