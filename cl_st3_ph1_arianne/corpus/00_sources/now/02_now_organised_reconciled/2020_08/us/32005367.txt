<p> The paper showed that a doubling of atmospheric greenhouse gas ( GHG ) concentrations -- which at our present do-nothing pace , we 'll hit sometime in the next two generations -- would most likely push global average temperatures to a range of between about 2.5 degrees and 4.0 degrees Celsius . <p> As such , the fact that the midpoint of Sherwood 's team 's most-likely temperature range is well above the two-degree threshold is not good news if you or anyone you know enjoys eating three square meals a day . <p> Even though the use of renewable energy technology has increased rapidly over the last ten years , an extrapolation of even that rapid growth rate will not put us where we need to be in terms of holding temperatures to the lower end ( or below ! ) of the Sherwood team 's calculated range . <p> We need to make radical transformations to the way we produce and consume the energy , food , and industrial inputs to reduce both the flow of new GHGs into the atmosphere and the stock of GHGs already there . <p> Joe Madden , CEO of Xpansiv CBL Holding Group ( XCHG ) , makes a strong case that all the elements for a radical reduction -- and eventual reversal -- in the flow of new GHGs into the atmosphere already exists . <p> Joe Madden , CEO of Xpansiv and Xpansiv CBL Holding Group ( XCHG ) <p> Joe Madden <p> Madden thinks that by shifting the way we measure the value embedded in commodities , we can unleash a nearly unimaginable revolution that will enable us to cut atmospheric carbon levels and maintain a climate conducive to a 2,000-calorie per day diet . <p> All we have to do is rejigger our 19th century markets to align with 21st century technology and a modern understanding about environmental , social , and governance ( ESG ) factors . <p> The lynchpin of a market-led radical transformation is the trend toward ubiquitous digitalization and the vast and expanding pool of data that it creates . How can Big Data reduce GHG emissions ? By providing investors the information necessary to allow markets to function efficiently . <p> Case in point : a tank of gasoline . <p> When you fill up your tank , do you know where the oil that was refined into your gas was mined ? <p> Whether it was scraped out of an open pit strip mine in the Oil Sands of Alberta or pumped from the depths of the North Sea 's gray waters is completely opaque to you and to every other market participant on the face of the earth . <p> In a warming world , however , the lack of transparency about the carbon footprint of the commodity you are consuming is a big deal . <p> Gasoline refined from Oil Sands oil generates almost twice the level of GHG emissions as does North Sea oil . Because the distinction between the carbon intensity of different production methods and sources is opaque to the market , buyers and sellers have no way of showing an economic preference for one product over another . <p> Without information , markets can not do what they do best : allocate more resources to better products . <p> This particular example involves oil and gasoline , but the same can be said about literally all the commodity inputs we take as a matter of fact in our daily lives -- food , steel , lumber , electrical power , etc . <p> The frustrating fact is that much of the data that would allow for market participants to make crucial distinctions already exist thanks to Internet of Things ( IoT ) technology , Big Data , and the trends towards digitalization of industrial processes . <p> Oceans of real-time production data and other ancillary metrics are captured by producers and gathered by data aggregators . However , because the structure of commodity markets is set up to deal with standardized , undifferentiable goods ( e.g. , " 5,000 bushels of No. 2 Yellow Corn " ) , there is no way for them to accept and process multidimensional ESG factor data ( e.g. , carbon footprint of fertilizer used , energy needed to irrigate field , miles traveled to storage and processing facility , etc . ) . <p> Our 19th century , analog market structure ends up blocking buyers and sellers from seeing , assessing , and making economic decisions about the commodities they are trading . The Digital Feedstock acts as an immediate boost to producers ' bottom lines -- sort of a carbon tax in reverse . Rather than economically punishing inefficient producers with a carbon tax , the producer with the best Digital Feedstock generates highest profits . <p> For commodity producers and consumers in markets where carbon intensity is regulated ( e.g. , California or the European Union ) , the ability to generate and " retire " Digital Feedstock provides new ways to meet regulatory requirements , creating economic upside and reducing downside risk . <p> The gentle reader must be thinking by this point , " Okay -- great .
