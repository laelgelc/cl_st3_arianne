<h> Explainer : What do political databases know about you ? <p> American citizens are inundated with political messages -- on social networks , in their news feeds , through email , text messages , and phone calls . It 's not an accident that people get bombarded : political groups prefer a " multimodal " voter contact strategy , where they use many platforms and multiple attempts to persuade a citizen to engage with their cause or candidate . An ad is followed by an email , which is followed by a text message -- all designed to reinforce the message . <p> These strategies are employed by political campaigns , political action committees , advocacy groups , and nonprofits alike . These different groups are subject to very different rules and regulations , but they all rely on capturing and devouring data about millions of people in America . <h> Who is in these data sets ? <p> Almost everyone . Most campaigns get their voter information from a handful of data vendors , either nonpartisan or partisan . But voter data is very patchy and decentralized : each state holds its own database , and they often have different attributes . So vendors supplement it with other sources , like phone books and credit data . <p> It 's hard to get a full picture of everything that is fed into the vendors ' databases : the recipe each one uses is usually considered a trade secret . Experts say that most vendors provide hundreds of data points about each voter . <h> How accurate are these voter databases ? <p> It 's up for debate .
