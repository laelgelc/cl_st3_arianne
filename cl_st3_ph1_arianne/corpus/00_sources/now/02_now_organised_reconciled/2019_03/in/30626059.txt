What does this approach achieve ? <p> I had two main motivations . The first was to delve into the roots of the protracted , urgent crises of water that India and many other Asian countries face today , especially in the context of accelerating climate change . If we think about the range of options open to us as we confront these challenges , they are all shaped by history : our infrastructure , our policy models , our institutions , they all shape the sorts of future that we can imagine , and they are all the cumulative result of earlier generations ' dreams and fears of water . The second motivation was precisely to show that water is inextricable from politics and society . To write a history of water is to write a history of politics , of social struggles , of inequality . I wanted to make that clear . <p> You write of big water management and hydro-engineering projects in colonial and newly independent India . How did that past create our present vulnerability ? This is something the historian Rohan D'Souza has also written about very eloquently . The current river-linking project in India quite explicitly traces a lineage back to the Englishman Arthur Cotton . But there is also a global dimension to this . The enthusiasm for large dams was widely shared in the mid-20th century , and American water engineers played an especially central role in bringing those models to India , albeit models that were always adapted to and transformed by India 's own needs . That past has contributed to our present vulnerability in a number of ways : it constrains our imagination in the sense that it leads , again and again , to large-scale engineering solutions . The risks are serious ones . There is an ongoing social crisis that comes from the sheer number of people in India displaced by large dams . There is an ecological crisis that comes from the dams ' environmental impact . One is of course global warming , which India has historically contributed very little to . But scientific research suggests that the monsoon 's patterns are also being altered by aerosol emissions in South Asia and China , as well as by changes in land use , including urbanisation and intensive irrigation .
