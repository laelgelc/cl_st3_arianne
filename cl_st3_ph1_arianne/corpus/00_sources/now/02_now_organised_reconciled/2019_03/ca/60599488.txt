<p> Researchers at UBC Okanagan in Kelowna have discovered a surprising new source of carbon dioxide emissions -- bicarbonates hidden in the lake water used to irrigate local orchards . <p> " We have been studying the carbon content of soil for some time , " said Melanie Jones , professor of biology and study lead author . <p> During photosynthesis , plants remove CO2 from the atmosphere and convert it into plant tissue such as roots , leaves , fruit or bark . However , the decomposition of dead plant tissue produces CO2 and release it back into the atmosphere . <p> Some CO2 that was removed from the atmosphere by plants can be converted into soil organic matter , where it can remain in the soil for hundreds of years , said Kirsten Hannam , an agroecologist with Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada and a co-author on the study . <p> " So major research efforts are underway to figure out how to increase soil organic matter content , " Hannam said . <p> Hannam says greater organic matter in soil has the benefit of sequestering greater atmospheric CO2 , which helps combat climate change while also improving @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Jones , Hannam and fellow UBCO soil scientist Andrew Midwood have been analyzing the chemical forms of CO2 that leave the soil surface during irrigation . <p> Working in a drip-irrigated apple orchard , tests were repeated with different water supplies , using irrigation water or de-ionized water , and the results were remarkably different . <p> " It turns out that some of the CO2 released after irrigation comes from the natural salts -- bicarbonates -- dissolved in water from Okanagan Lake as it is applied to the soil , " said Midwood .
