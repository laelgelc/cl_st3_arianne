( David Herbst ) <p> Long ago , naturalist John Muir extended an open invitation to harken to the rugged beauty and wonder of the high mountains . <p> Listen to the sounds of gushing streams as they roll through wild canyons , amid gardens of rocky meadows . Wander , surrounded by the forest and snowy peaks of the Sierra Nevada . <p> Today , however , these places are in peril . That 's according to a new study out of UCSB 's Sierra Nevada Aquatic Research Lab in Mammoth Lakes , part of the university 's Natural Reserve System . <p> The paper shows the risk of drought , in particular , may threaten the aquatic life that is integral to headwaters and the rivers into which they drain . The results appear in the journal Freshwater Biology . <p> " The high elevation zone you love to hike around in , and streams that you love to go fishing in , and places that you look to as your refuge for solace are changing , " said lead author David @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Institute . <p> " And climate forecasts suggest they could be disappearing , " he said . <p> To understand how these montane streams respond to drought , Herbst and collaborators tracked the range and diversity of aquatic invertebrates from 2002 through 2015 . <p> Scientists divide streams into roughly two types of habitats : riffles and pools . Riffles are shallow , rocky areas with relatively strong currents . Pools are the deeper areas that alternate with riffles . <p> Different species live in these different habitats , with the most sensitive creatures preferring the swifter waters of the riffles , Herbst said . <p> But as streams dry up , riffles disappear and pools can become disconnected . This reduces the richness of the stream community and results in a simpler food web . <p> For instance , large algae-eating and filter-feeding invertebrates , which are prime food for larger animals , like birds and fish , tend to disappear . <p> Mountain streams can bounce back , according to the scientists . When flows do return , they increasingly come back in excess , in what climatologists call a whiplash . <p> " We 're swinging from one extreme of drought to another extreme of flooding , and how this affects stream communities is not well understood , " Herbst said . These wide variations in flow may actually do more harm to the ecosystems than good . <p> Normally , perennial mountain streams may become intermittent , with disconnected stretches persisting for long periods . This is standard in coastal creeks , and the wildlife there has adapted to these conditions . <p> There 's a whole community of insects , and even some amphibians and fish , in coastal watersheds that can survive these intermittent flow regimes . But this is not common in the high Sierra Nevada . <p> And California is starting to see more precipitation fall as rain than snow , which can be just as devastating to mountain streams as the overall drought . Snow lingers , providing a longer , slower discharge that peaks in the late spring , rather than in winter . Snow also recharges groundwater far better than rain . <p> Ultimately , these differences set the conditions for what types of communities live in the streams , Herbst explained . <p> What 's more , mountain streams play an outsized role in their watersheds . A small pond or creek at the headwaters of a river can be more critical to the resiliency of the ecosystem than miles of downstream habitat . <p> It 's far easier for biodiversity to flow back downstream after a disturbance than to creep its way up the watershed . And if these mountain refuges run dry , there 's nothing farther upstream to reinhabit them . <p> " This scenario points out what you want to search for in making conservation decisions about what to protect in the mountains , " said Herbst .
