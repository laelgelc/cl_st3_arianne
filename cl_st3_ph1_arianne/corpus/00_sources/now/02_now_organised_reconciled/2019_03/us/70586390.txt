<p> Science I write about the ocean , climate change , and the future of our planet . <p> A mosaic of animals observed in central/northern California during the 2014-2016 marine heatwave . <p> Jacqueline Sones <p> Rising temperatures as a result of climate change are bringing many flora and fauna closer towards the poles and to higher elevations . Nearly 50% of species on this planet are already moving to cooler habitats with temperature ranges that do not extend beyond their comfort zones . On land , such migrations may influence pollination patterns and the transmission of vector-borne diseases . And in the oceans , where heatwaves are becoming increasingly common , ocean currents are changing directions and redistributing species around the globe . <p> Between the years 2014 and 2016 , the northern Pacific Ocean endured one of the longest marine heatwaves ever recorded . During this time , two back-to-back oceanographic events - a mass of warm water known as " the blob " and an El Ni ? o event - caused seawater temperatures in northern California to resemble warm temperatures usually seen near Baja California , Mexico . A new study documents dozens of warm-water species seen north of San Francisco during this prolonged period of warming . <p> Of the 67 warm-water species the researchers observed near the Point Reyes National Seashore , over half of them had never been seen this far north before . The unusually warm seawater coupled with the reversal of ocean currents near Monterey Bay allowed these species to travel so far away from their typical home range . Some of these newly observed species included the violet sea snail , blue buoy barnacle , and several kinds of sea slugs . The pelagic red crab was another newcomer from Baja California that was spotted not only in central California but also near Newport , Oregon .
