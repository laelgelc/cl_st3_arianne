<h> Galway scientist exploring the depths of the Indian Ocean <h> There are still blank spots on the maps , and Prof Louise Allcock from NUI Galway is tackling that on an expedition to explore the depths of the Indian Ocean . <p> The Nekton ' First Descent ' Mission to explore the deepest parts of the Indian Ocean began last week and will continue until April 17 . <p> Professor Allcock is second in command of this expedition of 33 scientists surveying underwater life around the Seychelles . <p> Researchers will be making dives themselves to look at plants and animals that live at depths of up to 30m ( 98ft ) . <p> For deeper trips below the surface they will be making use of two crewed submarines and a remote operated submersible to study life and habitats at depths of up to 500m , while sensors will offer a glimpse into life at 2000m . <p> Prof Allcock is Head of Zoology at the Ryan Institute in NUI Galway ; she said taking part in this expedition is an incredible opportunity . <p> " It 's very exciting to be deploying so many different pieces of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . " <p> At least 50 ' first descents ' are planned on this expedition where Nekton will be working on behalf of the Seychelles Government and partners . <p> Nexton an independent , non-profit research institute that works with the University of Oxford to increase scientific understanding of the oceans . <p> For this expedition to the Seychelles it has chartered the Ocean Zephyr , a Danish-flagged supply ship . <p> The Seychelles is a collection of islands roughly 1,500km off the east coast of Africa . <p> At least fifty descents are planned on this expedition alone , and over the next four years Nexton is planning similar missions in six different regions of the Indian Ocean . <p> The data they gather will provide important data which the Seychelles government will use to help implement its Marine Spatial Plan on ocean conservation , climate change , and fishing . <p> The Seychelles has set itself an ambitious target of protecting 30% of its marine territory by 2020 , an area roughly twice the size of the UK.
