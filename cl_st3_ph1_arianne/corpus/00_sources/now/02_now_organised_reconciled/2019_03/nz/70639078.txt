<h> Time to get used to drought <p> OPINION : Waikato is not as drought prone as other parts of the country , particularly east coast regions , but that could change over the next few years as the effects of accelerated climate change kick in . <p> Although scattered showers are forecast for some parts of Waikato over the next week , the rain is not expected to be significant enough to lift soil moisture levels and promote pasture growth before winter frosts arrive . Autumn rains , which have usually arrived well before the end of March , have not materialised and many farmers are now feeding out supplements held for winter . <p> According to the NIWA Climate Group there have been consistent climate change projections for more than a decade , predicting drier conditions in eastern New Zealand and an increase in drought occurrence and severity . That does not necessarily mean the frequency of very heavy rainfall and floods will decrease . Previous research suggests the frequency of very heavy rainfall may in fact increase in many parts of New Zealand , even in those areas where the annual rainfall decreases on average . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to be increasing challenged by the lack of rainfall at crucial times and by heavy rain events at others . Some of the effects of accelerated climate change are already being felt and can be expected to increase with time . Pasture and crop farming could face a double jeopardy with heavy rain inhibiting seed strike in spring and harvest in autumn and dry periods preventing growth , both at crucial times . <p> One solution to the drought issue would appear to be water farming , using dams and ponds to collect surplus surface water during heavy rain events and irrigation during dry periods . That may not be as straight forward as it seems given the cost of existing water storage schemes and the resistance to harvesting flood waters by conservation groups . Extraction of water from aquifers will also likely be even more difficult and restricted . <p> NIWA scientists have also indicated that the expansion of irrigation through increased water extraction is coming to an end and that the future is about making better use of available water . What we can take from these reports @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ aquifers and surface water systems for agricultural irrigation will come under increasing restriction as regional councils seek to reduce the impact of nutrient run off on natural water bodies , reduce the related nutrient emissions which arise from increased irrigation and correct historic over allocations in some waterways . <p> There is also the issue of the cost of irrigation both in the establishment phase and in the ongoing operating costs as well as the individual on-farm infrastructure costs . There is also the serious issue of protecting the quantity and quality of fresh water used for reticulated domestic use . <p> Any increase in irrigation will bring a predictable increase in association nutrient emissions which will have the potential to impact on potable water sources . While the supply of potable water has priority over all other uses , the current timeframes for meaningful reductions in nutrient emissions are probably too long if we have regular severe droughts in the next five to ten years . We probably also need to have as near to zero emissions as possible before the predicted droughts occur . <p> As with the @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ flood events we should rely on planning solutions rather than engineering solutions , if indeed we need to be looking for solutions at all . Planning for flood events and seal level rises is relatively logical but planning for drought events has few options . <p> Farmers in some regions have already accepted a change in operations and a move away from reliance on pasture alone , a significant reduction in production expectations and increased reliance on irrigation . It is obvious that farming will be different in the future and we may not have a lot of time to prepare for increased incidents and severity of drought . <p> We clearly need strategies to assist communities in drought prone regions while at the same time avoiding the negative impacts of irrigation in the form of nutrient emissions and correcting over allocation of some waterways . <p> Regional councils and farmer groups could consider asking central government to overcome the initial prohibitive establishment costs of irrigation with a government interest free loan over 50 to 80 years with ongoing operating costs funded through a targeted regional council rate against those @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ infrastructure costs to be met by farmers . The quid pro quo would be zero nutrient emissions within a few short years from the beginning of operations . <p> It could be done if short term profitability was replaced with long term sustainability as some farmers have already embraced .
