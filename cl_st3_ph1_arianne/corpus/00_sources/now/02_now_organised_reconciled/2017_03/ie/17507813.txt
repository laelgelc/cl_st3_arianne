<p> Off the coast of South Africa , as many as 200 humpback whales -- typically solitary creatures -- have been swarming and researchers are n't exactly sure why . <p> When a usually solitary creature like the humpback whale suddenly decides to go against its nature and swarm in huge groups , marine biologists are unsurprisingly eager to investigate . <p> According to National Geographic , a team led by Ken Findlay from Cape Peninsula University of Technology , Cape Town , began observing the peculiar behaviour during three different expeditions over the course of a four-year period . <p> Typically , humpback whales will form into groups of as few as three or four , but in these instances the team was observing as many as 200 or more swarming together . <p> To make the mystery even more perplexing , the swarms were occurring during spring . Typically , humpback whales visit the area during the winter months to feed on migrating fish . <p> While global events like climate change could be seen as an easy answer to solving this mystery -- due to radical changes @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ by the team suggest it could be something else . <p> In a paper published in PLOS One , Findlay and his team believe one answer could lie in a change in humpback feeding patterns , as the swarms have been observed hunting for prey . <p> Whale supergroup off the coast of South Africa . Image : Jean Tresfon <h> Species in midst of a boom <p> This could mean either the availability of prey has changed or the behaviour of the whales has changed . <p> A second possible explanation -- and a more positive one -- is that our estimates of the number of humpback whales may be too low and that this recent change is a sign that the species is booming in numbers not seen since the whaling era of the 19th and 20th centuries . <p> Unlike a number of other marine species , the humpback whale is categorised by the International Union for Conservation of Nature as one of least concern , with over 60,000 of the animals known to researchers .
