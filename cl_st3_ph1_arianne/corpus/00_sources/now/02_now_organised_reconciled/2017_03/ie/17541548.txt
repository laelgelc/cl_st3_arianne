<h> Recycling wastewater would ease global water shortages while protecting the environment <p> Updated / Wednesday , 22 Mar 2017 03:46 <p> A wastewater treatment plant inundated by floodwaters in Mississippi <p> Recycling the world 's wastewater , almost all of which goes untreated , would ease global water shortages while protecting the environment , according to a major United Nations report published today , World Water Day . <p> For decades , people have been using fresh water faster than Nature can replace it , contributing in some regions to hunger , disease , conflict and migration . <p> Two-thirds of humanity currently live in zones that experience water scarcity at least one month a year - half of those people are in China and India . <p> On current trends , the UN Environment Programme forecasts that water demand -- for industry , energy and an extra billion people -- will increase 50% by 2030 . <p> Global warming has already deepened droughts in many areas , and the planet will continue to heat up over the course of the century , even under optimistic scenarios . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ bed to find water <p> " There is an absolute necessity to increase water security in order to overcome the challenges brought on by climate change and human influence , " said Benedito Braga , head of the World Water Council , an umbrella grouping of governments , associations and research bodies . <p> Wastewater - runoff from agriculture , industry and expanding cities , especially in developing nations - is a major part of the problem . That is especially true in poor countries where very little , if any , wastewater is treated or recycled . <p> High-income nations treat about 70% of the wastewater they generate , a figure that drops to 38% for upper middle-income countries . In low-income nations , only 8% of industrial and municipal wastewater undergoes treatment of any kind . <p> More than 800,000 people die every year because of contaminated drinking water , and not being able to properly wash their hands . <p> Water-related diseases claim nearly 3.5 million lives annually in Africa , Asia and Latin America - more than the global death toll from AIDS and car crashes @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ create dead-zones in rivers , lakes and coastal waters , and seep into aquifers . <p> The 200-page World Water Development Report details a four-pronged strategy for transforming wastewater from a problem to a solution , said lead author Richard Connor of UNESCO 's World Water Assessment Programme . <p> " Up to now , decision makers have mainly focused on supplying clean water rather than managing it after it has been used , " Connor told journalists . " The two aspects are inextricably linked . " <p> Water can be used over and over , he added , pointing to the fact that water from several major rivers in the United States is recycled up to 20 times before reaching the ocean . <p> The potential for reusing liquid waste is perhaps best illustrated by astronauts on the International Space Station who drink recycled urine and use it to wash up . <p> On a larger scale , there are many nations where necessity has spawned innovative technologies . <p> In Singapore and the southern California coastal city of San Diego , residents already drink recycled water . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ agricultural water , respectively , has been recovered for reuse , according to the report . <p> Besides being recycled , wastewater " can also be a rich source of nutrients , minerals and energy - all of which can be cost-effectively extracted , " said Guy Ryder , chair of UN-Water and head of the International Labour Organization . These are the same elements , he added , that cause terrible damage when untreated wastewater is released into the environment . <p> Already in use in Australia , China and Japan , these systems can be easily scaled up . <p> More than a fifth of global phosphorus demand worldwide could be met by recycled human urine and feces , according to a recent study . <p> Waste can also be converted into fuel . <p> A 2015 law in Japan requires sewage operators to use biosolids as a carbon-neutral form of energy .
