<h> ' Solid , liquid waste may have overtaken fertilisers as the biggest source of N2O emissions ' <h> Published : Thursday 15 October 2020 <p> Rising global nitrous oxide ( N2O ) emissions paint a worrisome picture : Human emissions of this greenhouse gas -- 300 times more potent than carbon dioxide ( CO2 ) -- increased 30 per cent in the last four decades , according to a research published in Nature recently . <p> The research was conducted through an international collaboration between the International Nitrogen Initiative ( INI ) and the Global Carbon Project of Future Earth , a partner of the World Climate Research Programme . It involved 57 scientists from 48 institutions in 14 countries . <p> While countries have been reporting their N2O emissions since 1990 under the United Nations Framework Convention for Climate Change , these inventories do not show the complete picture for they do not account for natural sources of emissions . <p> A major proportion of these emissions come from agriculture , which warrants an integrated approach to solve the problems of global food security and push for climate @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Raghuram , chair of INI and professor of biotechnology at Guru Gobind Singh Indraprastha University , New Delhi , to understand the significance of the current research , its implications and future solutions . <p> Akshit Sangomla : What makes the current assessment significant in terms of collected data and its analysis ? <p> Nanduala Raghuram : This is the most recent ( data up to 2016 analysed ) and comprehensive analysis that includes both natural and man-made emissions ( only some of the latter are reported by many countries towards Sustainable Development Goals ) . <p> AS : How will this research help in making better sense of N2O emissions and its possible mitigation ? <p> NR : Identifying the geographical break-up of emissions will alert countries of each region to address this concern at the national and regional level . <p> Identifying the sectors responsible for most emissions ( agriculture in this case ) can be helpful to make sector-specific policies to reduce emissions . Basmati has the lowest N-use efficiency among rice , but farmers of Punjab and Haryana use all the water and urea they can get to produce it , mainly for export . <p> There is plenty of scope for reduction of urea consumption in all cereals and horticultural crops . There is evidence to show that at least 50 per cent of the nitrogen fertilisers ( urea in case of India ) can be replaced with organic wastes such as manure , wastewater , compost , biochar , etc , without any loss of yield . <p> This also has another benefit of reducing waste pollution and emissions from it .
