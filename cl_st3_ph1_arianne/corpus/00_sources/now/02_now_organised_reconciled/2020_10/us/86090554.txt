<p> WASHINGTON , D.C. - The federal government is about to spend $15 million on a new push to restore the Cuyahoga River by adding more twists and turns to its sections that traverse Cuyahoga Valley National Park . <p> The river - whose name derives from a Native American word that means " crooked " - will have between 3/4 of a mile and 1 1/2 miles of curves added to its length under a new U.S. Army Corps of Engineers project funded by the Great Lakes Restoration Initiative . <p> Adding the extra curves to the river will help it better absorb extra water that 's been funneled into the river in the form of runoff from impervious surfaces from nearby developments , as well as added rainfall in the region due to climate change . Army Corps of Engineers officials say the Cuyahoga River basin gets 7 inches more rain each year than it did in the 1960s , with an average increase of .12 inches each year . <p> The upcoming work at the Boston Mills ski area will also include planting native trees such as @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ pin oaks , willows and dogwoods to absorb moisture and reduce the amount of silt that comes into the river . That will help reduce the need to dredge the river , and improve its ecosystem for insects , fish , birds and other wildlife . <p> According to U.S. Army Corps of Engineers project manager Russell Brandenburg , redirecting the river 's flow through the park is among 16 projects that multiple organizations are performing along the Cuyahoga River with the goal of removing it from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 's " area of concern " list by 2024 . <p> " It is a big group effort , where we are all coming together for the common good of the Cuyahoga River as a whole , " said Brandenburg . <p> This map supplied by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers shows where it plans to add more curves to the Cuyahoga River as part of a restoration project .
