<h> Individual companies are more likely to be in the crosshairs than entire sectors as sustainability goes mainstream <h> Rochelle Toplensky <p> The campaign to divest fossil-fuel stocks has picked up speed in recent years and hit investors where it hurts . The next targets are n't so easy to identify . <p> More than 1,200 institutions managing over $14 trillion have now signed divestment commitments with gofossilfree.org . Movements to target tobacco producers and companies in Apartheid-era South Africa inspired the 2010 initiative to pressure investors to dump oil and gas stocks over climate change concerns . <p> The campaign is one of many factors that have caused big energy stocks to underperform spectacularly this year . Investors need to consider who might be next in the firing line , but few industries are as broadly tainted by environmental or social concerns as tobacco or oil . The kind of detailed analysis usually applied to companies ' financial data may be the only answer . <p> This summer Nordea Asset Management sold its $47 million stake in Brazilian beef giant JBS over deforestation concerns . Chemicals , fast fashion and industries like steel and cement all emit lots of greenhouse gases . Even technology -- a popular sector for green-minded investors -- has massive power-hungry data centers . Mounting concerns about biodiversity point to four troublesome commodities : beef , soya , palm oil and timber , says Katherine Ogundiya , sustainable and thematic investing analyst at Barclays . In each industry there are climate leaders and laggards , and many of the big publicly listed companies sell a range of products . <p> To understand the risks to which a stock exposes them , investors need information from companies themselves on carbon emissions , decarbonization plans and other measures of sustainability . For now , a menu of voluntary standards currently allows companies to pick and choose what to report , but this is changing .
