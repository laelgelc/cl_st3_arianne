Photo provided by Jen Pierce . <p> Drylands , informally known as rangelands , occupy about 40 percent of the Earth 's surface . Dryland is a terrain familiar to many Idahoans . It 's found along the expansive Snake River Plain across Southern Idaho where there 's typically not enough water for trees to grow , except along the river banks . Drylands may appear rugged , but are fragile and sensitive to human activity . <p> Jen Pierce , an associate professor in the Department of Geosciences , has received a grant of more than $500,000 from the National Science Foundation to advance first-of-its-kind , critical zone research of drylands ecosystems . Two of five study sites are in Idaho . <p> " The project will improve knowledge of soil and water resources in natural and agricultural fields locally with implications for drylands globally , " said Pierce . <h> The research <p> In dryland soil , carbon is stored not in its organic form -- think of the healthy black soil prized by gardeners -- but in its inorganic form , calcium carbonate , that creates soil known as caliche , or hardpan . This kind of soil represents long-term carbon storage . <p> " The more carbon we can store in the soil , the less carbon is released ( in the form of carbon dioxide ) into our atmosphere and warming planet , " said Pierce . <p> What scientists do n't yet know , she said , is how activities like irrigation or development that transform sagebrush into green lawns affect drylands .
