In their version of the model , they included dynamics for both migration and remittance , cash being exchanged between people in the two countries . The researchers measured " exposure " to climate change by quantifying how people might be affected by it , where they are and where they might go , as well as how much money they might have . <p> First , they tested the accuracy of the model by looking at different border policies , making them both easier and more difficult to cross than they are today . They also investigated the effects of these border policies on different income levels and on people 's ability to relocate . <p> Then , using actual migration flows compiled by past researchers and derived from the World Bank , they made projections up until the year 2100 . Using what scientists call a " gravity model , " they took into account economics , demography , migration and income differences between places to determine the number of people moving . <p> They found that exposure and vulnerability to impacts of climate change tend to be higher in developing countries . When people are allowed to move freely , they tend to send more money " back home , " which provides an important source of income for the origin country . This income could also be used to reduce vulnerability to climate change . <p> Researchers at SPIA have been studying the relationship of climate change to migration for more than a decade using a variety of modeling approaches .
