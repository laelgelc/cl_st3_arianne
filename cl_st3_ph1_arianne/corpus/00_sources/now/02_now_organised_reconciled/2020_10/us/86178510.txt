<p> Just after researchers wrapped up fieldwork for the season in the turquoise waters between the Virgin Islands and Puerto Rico in 2017 , back-to-back major hurricanes tore through their study sites and laboratories . It was a serious setback in the team 's work with little-known parasites called gnathiids , whose young latch onto saltwater fish and feed on their blood . <p> But the misfortune also presented a rare opportunity to study how catastrophic hurricanes impact marine animal populations , says molecular ecologist Juan Andr ? s Pag ? n , a graduate student at the University of Porto in Portugal and lead author on a recent study in Scientific Reports . Every year since 2013 , the team -- which calls itself Gnathiid Gnation -- had collected a species it named for Bob Marley ( Gnathia marleyi ) , found in coral reefs . After the hurricanes the researchers returned to collect more specimens . " We were able to pretty clearly establish that , prior to the hurricanes , these populations were stable , " Pag ? n says , meaning the parasites remained in the same isolated areas year after year . <p> After the storms , things changed . <p> Gnathiids are bad long-distance swimmers . Under normal conditions they stick to small areas , similar to other invertebrates such as crustaceans and mollusks . But G. marleyi rode the 2017 hurricanes over unusually long distances , with some apparently traveling more than 250 kilometers . The researchers found that this displacement led to a " mixing of the pool " and , in turn , a more genetically similar gnathiid population , says team leader Paul Sikkel , an Arkansas State University marine ecologist and study co-author . " The hurricanes homogenized all that , " Sikkel says . " At any one site the diversity became higher . But overall the populations became more even because things were being moved around . If hurricanes increase in intensity as predicted , Aronson says , the study suggests there may be more genetic homogenization among some marine animals that would otherwise remain in isolated populations . This would make peripheral populations less likely to cleave off and create new species , he adds . <p> The researchers are also investigating whether the parasites spread blood-borne pathogens that could harm fish populations .
