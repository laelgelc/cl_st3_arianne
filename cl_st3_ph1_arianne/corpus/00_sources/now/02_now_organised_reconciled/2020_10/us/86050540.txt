And it could quadruple over the next 80 years if greenhouse gas emissions do n't decline dramatically in the coming decades . <p> Led by Jason Briner of the University at Buffalo , State University of New York , the study is among the first to compare the possible future of the ice sheet with its ancient past . <p> " Now we 're really able to put into perspective just how anomalous our current change is and future changes might be , " said Josh Cuzzone , a co-author @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ California , Irvine . <p> The researchers used models , informed by data from ancient ice samples drilled out from the ice sheet , to reconstruct a history of Greenland spanning the past 12,000 years . They also used models to predict how the ice sheet might change under different climate scenarios -- assuming both higher and lower levels of greenhouse gases -- through the rest of this century . <p> The findings were concerning . <p> Before the industrial era , the highest rates of Greenland ice loss in 12,000 years were around 6 trillion tons of ice in a single century . That 's similar to the rate at which ice is melting in Greenland today . <p> As the climate continues to warm , those rates are expected to increase . How much depends on how fast the climate warms . <p> The researchers examined two possible future climate scenarios . If that happens , the models suggest the ice sheet could lose 14 trillion to 36 trillion tons of ice over the course of this century . <p> Researchers are now " increasingly certain that we are about to experience unprecedented rates of ice loss from Greenland , unless greenhouse-gas emissions are substantially reduced , " Andy Aschwanden , a researcher at the University of Alaska , Fairbanks , said in a commentary on the research also published yesterday in Nature . <p> The amount of future ice loss could make a big difference to communities around the world through its effect on sea levels . <p> Greenland is already the biggest contributor to global sea-level rise .
