Infections continue to occur in communities around the country . Viral resurgence has forced multiple states to pause or reverse overall reopening plans . Public health leaders caution that we may not experience " real normality " until next year , and even that may be optimistic . The pandemic has not gone away . <p> If the pandemic remains a major threat , why are some people increasingly apathetic about behaviors that mitigate it ? <p> One explanation is that humans are poorly-equipped to handle threats like Covid-19 . Over time , humans have developed systems for responding to immediate dangers , like oncoming cars at a crosswalk . Cognitively , these threats can tap into what some have described as our system 1 thinking : fast , intuitive , and almost automatic ways of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ play . Imminent threats can trigger reflexive " fight or flight " responses -- quickened breath , flushed skin , trembling muscles -- that rapidly prepare us to fight or flee . <p> We do n't have this wiring for distant threats . Humans are famously bad at assessing risk , particularly in the long-term . As a species , we can also be prone to hyperbolic discounting , the concept that describes our tendency to prefer smaller , immediate rewards over larger , deferred ones . <p> These forces can create apathy about serious , but slower-moving , threats . Consider the issue of climate change -- as serious and existential a threat as we 've ever faced . Despite compelling evidence and many calls to action , the global community has been slow to address the problem . <p> One reason for this paralysis : our cognitive wiring allows our concern about short-term threats , like lost business profits or economic development opportunities , to drown out long-term threats , like food insecurity and poor air quality . The task is harder when threats are enshrouded in uncertainty . This problem looms large for issues like climate change , where ambiguity about specifics -- the exact magnitude of temperature increases , the extent of damage to biodiversity , the specific areas and communities that will be most affected -- can hinder action . <p> Covid-19 may create similar inertia . Despite all that the scientific community has learned about the virus , much still remains unknown . If it can linger in the air via aerosols , what are the exact dangers of airborne versus close contact infection ? If poor ventilation increases transmission risk , what defines safe ventilation for airplanes , as compared to restaurants or other settings ? In particular , public officials can fund studies to evaluate infection risks in specific settings , while business leaders can partner with researchers by providing technology for those studies or encouraging employees to participate in them . <p> Second , leaders should maintain communication strategies for sharing Covid-related information with constituents and employees . This work may not seem like a top priority given how prominent Covid-19 remains in the national news cycle . But communication is now as critical as ever given the sheer amount of information -- and misinformation -- that exists about the pandemic . <p> Of course , behavioral tendencies are not the only drivers of inaction or unsafe Covid-19 behaviors . Addressing cognitive biases also does not obviate the need for coordinated public health surveillance , vaccine development , and other measures . Nonetheless , human behavior is the final common pathway to all interventions , and therefore plays a key in ultimately overcoming the pandemic .
