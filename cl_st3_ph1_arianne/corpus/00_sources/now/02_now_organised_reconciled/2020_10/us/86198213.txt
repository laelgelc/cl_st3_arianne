<h> Coastal permafrost more susceptible to climate change than previously thought <p> IMAGE : Micaela Pedrazas ( left ) and Cansu Demir , both graduate students at The University of Texas at Austin Jackson School of Geosciences , examining an exposed side of an ice wedge polygon , which ... view more <p> Credit : Bayani Cardenas <p> If you flew from the sea towards the land in the north slope of Alaska , you would cross from the water , over a narrow beach , and then to the tundra . From the air , that tundra would look like a landscape of room-sized polygonal shapes . Those shapes are the surface manifestations of the ice in the frozen ground below , a solidified earth known as permafrost . <p> Scientists long believed the solid permafrost extended offshore : from the tundra , below that narrow beach and below the seafloor declining at a gentle slope . They viewed that permafrost like solid brick , locking the subsurface--and the vast amounts of carbon it holds--in place . <p> But new research led by Micaela Pedrazas , who earned her @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of Geosciences working with Professor Bayani Cardenas , has upended that paradigm . They found permafrost to be mostly absent throughout the shallow seafloor along a coastal field site in northeastern Alaska . That means carbon can be released from coastline sources much more easily than previously thought . <p> The study was published in Science Advances on Oct. 23 with coauthors from the Jackson School and UT 's Marine Science Institute . <p> Using a geophysical technique called electrical resistivity imaging , the researchers mapped the subsurface beneath Kaktovik Lagoon along the northeastern coast of Alaska over the course of three years . <p> The results were unexpected . The beach and seafloor were entirely ice-free down to at least 65 feet . On the tundra itself , ice-rich permafrost was detected in the top 16 feet , but below that , the subsurface their imaging mapped was also ice-free . <p> " This leads to a new conceptual model , " Pedrazas said . <p> Permafrost is found in cold climates that remain frozen during the course of the year . Scientists have been tracking the impact of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ permafrost releases its stores of frozen carbon into the atmosphere as methane and carbon dioxide , contributing to climate change . <p> Permafrost studies have almost exclusively focused on the region beneath the tundra . Because it 's not easy to work in such remote locations and under harsh weather conditions , the transition from sea to shore has been largely ignored . <p> " This study tells us that the coastline is much more complicated than we thought , " said co-author Jim McClelland from UT 's Marine Science Institute . " It opens up the possibility for routes of water exchange that we were n't thinking about . " <p> Besides global considerations , the work has local impacts . The communities along the coast , most of whom are Inupiat , live on the permafrost . As the permafrost thaws , it accelerates coastal erosion , which carves away at the land on which homes and infrastructure stand . In the Kaktovik region , erosion can be as great as 13 feet per year . <p> " Their cultural heritage and their welfare is integrated and intricately @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ There 's an immediate need to understand what 's happening in these lagoons . " <p> The new paradigm requires reimagining the coastal Arctic ecosystem as well . Liquid groundwater means that carbon and nutrients can move between the tundra and the lagoon . It also means that saltwater can move beneath the tundra , potentially affecting freshwater sources . <p> Paul Overduin , who was n't involved in the research , but who studies permafrost at Alfred Wegener Institute Helmholtz Centre for Polar and Marine Research , said that this work is the first step in understanding permafrost 's transition from sea to shore . <p> " As is often the case , when we start looking at something people do n't know much about , you open up a whole bunch of questions that needed to be looked at , " he said . " That 's what 's really exciting here .
