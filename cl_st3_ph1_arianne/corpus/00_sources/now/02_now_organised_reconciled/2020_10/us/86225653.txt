<h> When Is It Time For A " Manhattan Project " ? <p> This past weekend , I was invited to be part of the " Science Foo Camp , " an annual gathering of science-y folks hosted by Google and O'Reilly Media . This is usually held as an in-person gathering at the Google headquarters in California , but under the circumstances , they shifted this year 's meeting to a virtual format . So , alas , I did n't get to go anywhere more exotic than my office on campus , where I spent many hours participating in sessions via Google Meet . <p> The SciFoo meeting is an " unconference " with the program developed on the spot from suggestions by the attendees . When looking at the list of participants , I was struck by the fact that it was a fairly eclectic mix of people working in very applied technology and people whose work was on much more " basic research " sorts of fields : foundations of physics , cosmology , and so on . That 's a little bit unusual in scientific @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ wondered whether people there might have thoughts about how best to allocate efforts between those categories . Both basic and applied research are essential -- applied research takes results from basic research and turns them into technologies that change the world -- but exactly how much time and money should be devoted to each is an interesting question . <p> This eventually led to me proposing and moderating a discussion with the title " When Is It Time For A ' Manhattan Project ' ? " The framing comes out of a comment made by another physicist when I first floated the idea , questioning how " real " the division between basic and applied is . As he noted , there are a good number of famous physicists like Robert Oppenheimer and Richard Feynman who are primarily known for their theoretical work on very " basic research " problems who also did exceptional work with the extremely applied Manhattan Project . <p> I thought that was a good observation , and one that really sharpened the question . That is , the Manhattan Project is an example where people @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ the war temporarily put that aside , and devoted themselves to very applied work . In the process , they discovered or developed great abilities in running huge applied projects , but after the war they mostly returned to the more basic topics they had been known for before . <p> That kind of clicked with another of my motivations for considering the question of allocation of effort to basic vs. applied research , namely the regular calls for " more science " to address some urgent crisis -- usually climate change , more recently Covid-19 . While I am all in favor of " more science " as a general matter , on a more personal level a lot of these end up making me feel left out . My background is in experimental cold-atom physics , which is very much a " basic research " kind of area without a lot of direct relevance to developing useful green technology or medical therapies to address an acute crisis . They were people who put aside basic research for a time , because they felt the need to contribute to a more immediate applied problem . <p> In that context , the question sort of becomes " At what point is the sort of all-hands effort made by the physics community during WWII appropriate or necessary ? " Or , to put it in a more specific @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ climate change demand that we ask whoever the modern equivalent of Schwinger is to stop working on string theory and cosmology and join in a crash program to develop better batteries or fusion reactors ? <p> In the course of the discussion , one of the participants objected to the use of " Manhattan Project " as an example , citing the ethical issues of a weapons program , and also the inherently nationalistic nature of the project ( which would also disqualify the radar effort ) . We tried to come up with a less militaristic example , with the primary candidates being the Apollo " Moon shot " ( as a crash program to achieve a specific goal in a short period ) and the development of CERN ( as an example of international collaboration ) . I do n't think either of those quite works , though , because they both mostly involve scientists doing the same kinds of things they were already doing , just with a much bigger budget . That is , the Apollo program took people who were already rocket scientists ( and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ gave them truckloads of cash with which to do rocket science . CERN brought together a pan-European collection of scientists to build a huge accelerator , but it did that basically by hiring a lot of people who had already built small accelerators , and pooling their efforts and budgets . <p> The thing about the Manhattan Project and the parallel radar effort ( which does n't have as iconic a code name ) that seems unique and striking ( at least to me ) was that they shifted people from one area to another on a temporary , emergency basis . Feynman stopped thinking about basic quantum mechanics , and instead spent a few years developing algorithms to calculate bomb properties using punch-card computers . Schwinger stopped his fundamental quantum research , and instead spent his nights solving problems in applied classical electromagnetism . After the wartime projects wrapped up , they went back to what they 'd been doing before it , and by 1948 each had revolutionary theories of quantum electrodynamics . Neither of them did much research after that that could be considered " applied " @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Whenever something seems unique like that , it 's worth asking what were the conditions that enabled it . One feature that was pointed out during the conversation is that there was a strikingly direct connection between the kinds of basic physics that were hot topics in the 1930 's -- when physicists were first able to start calculating properties of nuclei in a useful way -- and the work needed to build a bomb . Similarly , the radar effort largely involved complicated calculations using reasonably well-established mathematical apparatus -- at some level , it 's just solving Maxwell 's Equations . It thus was n't that dramatic a transition for at least some of the scientists involved . <p> The other major feature characterizing the wartime physics efforts is that they had very specific goals and a sense of urgency . There was an active shooting war going on , so their work was going to save lives in a very immediate way . And they also had fairly specific and well-defined goals they were trying to reach : making a fission bomb , or making a radar @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ That specificity is one of the biggest difference between those wartime efforts and the current crisis : while climate change is a very big and urgent threat , the potential solutions remain kind of diffuse . There are lots of different things that could make a difference , but not much clarity about which is the best place to throw a huge amount of brainpower and money . <p> There was also an institutional element to the war efforts that 's missing today , which might be the biggest single obstacle to any kind of practical shifting of efforts . That is , during the war , there was an obvious central authority that could recruit scientists , set broad goals , and ensure the necessary logistical support . The physics community of that era was also a whole lot smaller , so it was somewhat easier to identify and pull in everybody good . These days , the scientific community is a much larger collection of cats to be herded toward whatever goal might be set : vastly larger numbers of individual scientists , but also a much larger @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ to be begged and cajoled into mounting any kind of coordinated effort . <p> And , of course , there 's a nearly complete lack of leadership around the major crises facing us today . This is most starkly visible in the United States , whose government has spent the last four years not only failing to step up but actively running away from any kind of concerted effort . Most of the rest of the world is n't doing all that much better , though , and of course there 's an utter lack of any kind of international authority that might coordinate a global effort . <p> ( There was an intriguing suggestion during the discussion that it might be worthwhile to develop ways to split time between basic and applied projects . That is , as one participant said , it would be good to be able to spend , say , one day a week or one week a month contributing to a project that worked toward solving climate change , or on ways to contain the Covid pandemic .
