<h> Why the climate-change crisis is more than a question of feeling <h> By Peter Christoff <p> October 2 , 2020 -- 4.00pm <p> CLIMATEHow to Talk about Climate Change in a Way that Makes a DifferenceRebecca HuntleyMurdoch Books , $32.99 <p> The COVID pandemic has shown how fiercely we in Australia can respond to anything that threatens our family and the world as we know it . It has provoked rapid political intervention and we have been prepared , for the most part , to endure profound inconvenience for the common good . <p> With climate change , we also face an emergency , the dimensions of which are clarifying as once-distant projections become immediate realities , as our forests burn and the Reef dies . Stopping global heating now requires a shockingly fast transformation of the fossil fuel economies upon which we depend . <p> Yet overwhelming scientific understanding and evidence and the mounting toll of unnatural natural catastrophes have instead led to paralysis , delay , denial , hostility and conflict . These reactions roll from dinner-table debates on through the social media and into the arid @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Climate change is an existential threat and therefore an extreme emotional provocation . From guilt to shame , from anger to denial , from rage to grief , and from love to loss , the accompanying emotions are sometimes felt by the same person , occasionally simultaneously . No surprises here , given the problem has stared us in the face , with little done to avert its impacts , for three wasted decades . <p> Credit : <p> Huntley offers a compassionate and erudite exploration of the feelings that climate change evokes and the blockages they cause . Her engaging discussion is sometimes personal , reflecting on her own conversion to climate activism . She aims to penetrate what , in Australia , has too often been a dialogue of the deaf between ' ' believers ' ' and ' ' sceptics ' ' , by speaking to those on both sides who might be susceptible to examining their reactions reflectively . <p> Yet if Huntley 's greatest strength is her careful interrogation of the emotions , this also presents us with paradoxes . For some of us , reason and facts first flick that switch . For others , it is the configurations of anger and grief at a future stolen from us and our children . Too often , however , in this book reason is relegated : beliefs and emotions precede analysis . <p> The book is also intimately but frustratingly individualistic . Huntley seeks individual reflection in the hope that by confronting the full range of emotions -- but especially fear -- person by person , the shifting massed weight of individual opinion will propel progressive climate politics and policy . Of course the personal is political . But Huntley 's implicit ' ' theory of change ' ' is unfocused and remains strangely apolitical . She might well respond that without that first personal step , nothing else follows . Yet not all individuals are the same , as the cliche goes . Some have positions of great power . The difference between personal beliefs and public roles and responsibilities is critical . Few care about the private beliefs of a CEO if they meet the demands of their professional role without harming the enterprise or the public . Similarly , Prime Ministers and Premiers are n't elected in a charisma contest -- though they now too often think they are -- but to govern for public benefit . <p> Huntley 's orientation towards the personal and private perhaps arises from her focus on American social research . While understandable given apparent similarities between US and Australian publics , our collective obsession with the US means we fail to scrutinise and learn from positive examples of how climate policy has been normalised , institutionalised , and successful elsewhere -- in Europe , and particularly in Britain and Germany . <p> Why have many European political systems relied upon and been guided so effectively by scientific and expert advice in the realms of climate and energy @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ gravity of the climate challenge ? How has public accountability been so successfully fashioned and enforced as to enable and drive responsible leadership ? <p> Answers to these questions would illuminate the contrasting , damaging failures of our own political system . And how do we deal with a post-truth public sphere in which the opportunity for genuine dialogue is denied by individuals armed with fake facts engineered by merchants of doubt ? <p> These are not the questions that Huntley set herself . But by concentrating on personal transformation and failing to consider the political institutions that channel and obstruct action , we may end up talking more about climate change -- but not in a way that makes a difference . <p> Peter Christoff teaches about climate politics at the University of Melbourne .
