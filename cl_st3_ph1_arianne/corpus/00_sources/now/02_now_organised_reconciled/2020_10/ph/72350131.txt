<h> Science Solitaire The gifts of quarantine <p> If journeys were " midwives of thought , " as philosopher Alain de Botton wrote in The Art of Travel , I think the days and nights of quarantine are the forceps . While we are forced to isolate as much as we can to stay safe , we also have invisible prongs connected to a virtual world . We are a species adapting to the conditions of our wired isolation . <p> It is yet too early to know the permanent dictates of this kind of experience on our human nature but for sure , the duration , quality , and reach of experiences now are very different from our lives before this . And so too are the reflections that they afford us . <p> Like many I know and know of , I seem to be working a lot more now -- not in the narrow sense of simply ticking to-do lists that come with a job , but discovering and cultivating new streams of living and learning . At the time of this writing , I found that those two seemingly disparate experiences were jointly teaching me a lesson about something so essential about being human . <p> In the days that I prepared for my climate crisis lecture to a class on the other side of the world , I struggled NOT with the scientific data . The science is very robust on climate change . But what was nagging me was a part of myself asking , why should a bunch of privileged students so far away in a comfortable autumnal afternoon care about the climate crisis experience in countries like the Philippines ? <p> So while I laid out the scientific terrain on which our experience of the climate crisis lay , I focused on why they should care . I talked about recycled air that we all breathe from long ago , from Shakespeare , Magellan , and even Lapu-Lapu , and from near and also far away , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ we all share . I talked about the march of human species across natural history , before countries came to be , before borders required visas , before citizenship framed our sphere of what we should care about or fear for and up to where . Should the accident of our birth limit the reach of what our life should reach for and care about ? I talked about the fact that just as the unrivalled might of the dinosaurs defined the Jurassic age , our power to conquer as humans defined this era as the Anthropocene . And being the Anthropocene , I challenged them to see if this is what we humans ultimately have to show for for defining an era -- a planet laid waste ? <p> I got feedback from the students and their professor that the slant of the lecture I delivered brought them to places in their own minds that they had never been to . I had to steer it so the students would realize for themselves that who they are as human beings is defined not so much by what they were born with or what they have , but what they reach out to as they live their lives . <p> Ultimately , who we become is how far and deep we reach outside ourselves . Young people everywhere are on a metaphorical train to a future passing us by . We have got to take the time to flash neon signs to signal to them that what they care about is essentially who they become , and how the only planet that holds all our past and present , will be . <p> Conversely , what we " fall " for is a how we lose who we are . That is , I think , one of the main insights we cultivated from the webinar with psychologists Dr Richie Rosales-Parr and Dr Gina Hechanova-Alampay , on the points raised by Netflix film The Social Dilemma .
