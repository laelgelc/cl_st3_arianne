Globally , the gas contributes 28 percent of human-induced climate warming . But it 's a far more intense issue for us . Almost half our greenhouse gas emissions are generated by our agriculture . Of those , 10.3 percent are from nitrous oxide , a long-lived and very potent climate changer , and 35.5 percent from methane , a short-lived but also very powerful climate force . Of that farm methane , 68 percent comes from ruminant animals , overwhelmingly in the dairy and red meat sectors . <p> When , how and by how much we reduce methane will have far ranging impacts on climate and the economy . Simplistically , if we make good decisions , we 'll meet our climate goals , and our agricultural scientists and farmers will contribute to the global challenge of making meat and dairy foods more climate compatible . His current roles include an adjunct professorship at the Institute . <p> Frame , Macey and their colleagues argued that the conventional way of measuring methane 's climate impact was flawed , and that they had devised a better way . Using this new metric , they argued that because methane was short-lived , so was its impact on climate change . By about 12 years , about one-third of the methane has broken down , reducing the climate impact of the gas . By 20 years or so little of the gas remains , leading Frame and colleagues to argue that its climate impact is negligible . <p> Thus , if farmers keep emissions static , as they have over the past decade or so , or reduce them slightly as their farming efficiency continues , then the steady flow of methane is not contributing to additional climate change , Frame and colleagues argue .
