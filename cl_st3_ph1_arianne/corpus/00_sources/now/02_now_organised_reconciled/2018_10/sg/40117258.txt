" <p> The meeting in Incheon is aimed at finalising the report - a summary for policymakers - ready for an official release on Monday . <p> The IPCC , the world 's definitive scientific body when it comes to climate change , was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize a decade ago and has been given what may rank as its hardest task yet . <p> It must not only tell governments what we know about climate change - but also how close they have brought us to the edge . And by implication , how much those governments are failing to live up to their goals for the planet , set in the 2015 Paris climate agreement . <p> Limiting warming to 1.5 deg C is the most stringent and ambitious goal in that agreement , originally put there at the behest of small island nations and other highly vulnerable countries . <p> But it is increasingly being regarded by all as a key guardrail , as severe climate change effects have been felt in just @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ little bit more warming would bring . <p> " Half a degree does n't sound like much till you put it in the right context , " said Mr Durwood Zaelke , president of the Institute for Governance and Sustainable Development . " It 's 50 per cent more than we have now . " <p> Parts of the planet , like the Arctic , have already warmed beyond 1.5 deg C and are seeing alarming changes . Antarctica and Greenland , containing many metres of sea-level rise , are wobbling . <p> Major die-offs have hit coral reefs around the globe , suggesting that an irreplaceable planetary feature could soon be lost . <p> It is universally recognised that the pledges to cut greenhouse gas emissions made in Paris would lead to a warming far beyond 1.5 deg C - more like 2.5 or 3 deg C , or even more . <p> " The pledges countries made during the Paris climate accord do n't get us anywhere close to what we have to do , " said Dr Drew Shindell , a climate expert at Duke University and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ They have n't really followed through with actions to reduce their emissions in any way commensurate with what they profess to be aiming for . " <p> At issue is what scientists call the " carbon budget " . Heat-trapping carbon dioxide is the main greenhouse gas - burning fossil fuels , deforestation and wildfires are major sources - and CO2 reached record levels in the atmosphere last year . <p> Because the gas lives in the atmosphere for so long , it steadily accumulates because forests , oceans and soils can not absorb all that extra CO2 that mankind is creating . <p> So there is only a limited amount of CO2 that can be emitted before it becomes impossible to avoid a given temperature , like 1.5 deg C. Unless emissions start to decline - which gives us more time . <p> This is why scenarios for holding warming to 1.5 deg C require rapid and deep changes to how we get energy . <p> The window may now be as narrow as around 15 years of current emissions , but since we do not know for sure @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ how much of a margin of error we are willing to give ourselves . <p> " It would be an enormous challenge to keep warming below a threshold " of 1.5 deg C , said Dr Shindell , bluntly . " This would be a really enormous lift . " <p> So enormous , he said , that it would require a monumental shift towards decarbonisation . <p> By 2030 - barely a decade away - the world 's emissions would need to drop by about 40 per cent . By the middle of the century , societies would need to have zero net emissions . <p> What might that look like ? In part , it would include things such as no more petrol or diesel-powered vehicles , a phase-out of coal-fired power plants and airplanes running on biofuels , he said . <p> " It 's a drastic change , " he said . " These are huge , huge shifts ... This would really be an unprecedented rate and magnitude of change .
