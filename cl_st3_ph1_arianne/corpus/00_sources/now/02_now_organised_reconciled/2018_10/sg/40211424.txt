<p> PARIS ( AFP ) - The World Wide Fund for Nature ( WWF ) and its partners have tracked population changes in Earth 's animal species for decades . News from the latest " Living Planet " report , released on Tuesday ( Oct 30 ) , is more grim than ever . <p> Here are key findings : <h> Populations crashing <p> From 1970 to 2014 , the number of vertebrates or animals with a backbone - birds , reptiles , amphibians , mammals and fish - plummeted across the globe , on average , by about 60 per cent . <p> For freshwater vertebrates , losses topped 80 per cent . Geographically , South and Central America have been hit hardest , with 89 per cent less wildlife in 2014 than in 1970 . <p> The WWF Living Planet Index tracks more than 4,000 species spread across nearly 17,000 populations . <h> Species disappearing <p> The index of extinction risk for five major groups - birds , mammals , amphibians , corals and an ancient family of plants called cycads - shows an accelerating slide towards oblivion . <p> Depending on which categories are included , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ 100 to 1,000 times greater than only a few centuries ago , when human activity began to alter the planet 's biology and chemistry in earnest . <p> By definition , this means that Earth has entered a mass extinction event , only the sixth in half a billion years . <h> Boundaries breached <p> In 2009 , scientists weighed the impact of humanity 's expanding appetites on nine processes - known as Earth systems - within nature .
