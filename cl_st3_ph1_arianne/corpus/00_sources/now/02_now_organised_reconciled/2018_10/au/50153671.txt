<h> Wentworth , Gillard and the national apology conjunction <h> By John Warhurst <p> 24 October 2018 -- 10:00pm <p> There was a stark contrast in the past week between the politics of the Wentworth byelection and the national apology to the victims and survivors of child sexual abuse by and within institutions last Monday . At a glance it showed the worst and the best of our political system . <p> This showed in several ways . The national apology had our two senior political leaders united in a single cause . They addressed all Australians at length in the House of Representatives chamber and then they spoke more briefly in the Great Hall to a large group of victims , survivors and their advocates . Both Scott Morrison , the Prime Minister , and Bill Shorten , the leader of the opposition , spoke very well and from the heart . These were well-crafted professional speeches like many others , of course , but whatever their origins they came across as authentic and personal expressions . <p> Julia Gillard received the greatest acclamation and the deepest recognition by victims and survivors of institutional child sexual abuse.Credit:Alex Ellinghausen @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Liberal and Labor leaders as they recognised the contributions that all recent prime ministers had made to the Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse set up by Julia Gillard in 2012 . She received the greatest acclamation and the deepest recognition by victims and survivors . For someone who has been the most gracious of our recent ex-leaders such spontaneous popular acclaim must have been a deeply moving contrast to the painful treatment meted out to her by her political opponents in parliament when she was prime minister . <p> Monday was an all too rare exercise in putting partisanship aside . Admittedly to do anything else on that day would have revealed supreme insensitivity and been totally inappropriate , but it was still a welcome return to decency and civility . Our leaders were seen at their best in an atmosphere of humility and positivity while recounting awful stories of criminality and abuse of vulnerable children . Both men rose above their reputations . Shorten is not widely popular but spoke well , hitting all the right notes . All elections are about competition rather than consensus , of course , and to expect anything different would be na ? ve . But electoral competition brings out the worst in our politicians . Party leaders play the macho role they think is expected of them and that means running down your opponents and playing up the dangers that they pose to our nation . Differences between candidates and parties are real enough , but they are exaggerated in the cause of competition , whereas on an occasion like Monday in parliament they are put aside just for the moment . <p> The Wentworth result , meaning the huge swing against the governing party rather than the ultimate result , was a repudiation of major party politics . Credit:AAP <p> That result has returned Australia briefly to minority government once again , a situation in which the government must reach towards the sensible centre as it rethinks its legislative program for the next six months . Minority government , in which the government lacks a clear majority of votes on the floor of the House of Representatives , was last experienced for three years during the Gillard government . How ironic that the Gillard period has emerged as the common element linking Wentworth and the apology . <p> While minority government is feared by the major parties and lambasted by some influential commentators it can produce many positive outcomes . On this occasion it may lead , through the concerted push of the House of Representatives cross bench , to more positive action on climate change . <p> More immediately it may also lead , with Phelps as one of its champions , to the removal of children from Nauru to the greater safety of the Australian mainland . While neither political leader recognised a connection between the sexual abuse of institutionalised children and the treatment of asylum-seeking children in detention several interjectors in the parliament and the Great Hall clearly did and made their voices heard . Has Australia repeated a pattern of neglect and abuse of children ? <p> The connection may turn out to be not just symbolic because it may be that the change to the parliamentary balance caused by the Wentworth result will be just the push that is needed to resolve the issue of the remaining children on Nauru . <p> If that is the case then this week may be remembered not for the contrast between two types of politics , negative and positive , but as one in which Australia promised to do better and showed that it can . <p> John Warhurst is an Emeritus Professor of Political Science at the Australian National University
