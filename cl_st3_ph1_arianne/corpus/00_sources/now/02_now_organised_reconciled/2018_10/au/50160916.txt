<h> Troppo troppo <p> The tropics in Australian literature runs the gamut , from bountiful home of an ' amiable and extrovert race ' , to a place steeped in slavery and indigenous suffering . Author Suzanne Falkiner traces the lineaments of an Australian tropical literature , forever tinged with uneasiness . <p> The people living in the Australian tropics have always tended to make a virtue of their difference , seeing themselves as less conformist and more insouciant than their southern neighbours . The tropical regions of Australia were the last that the British settlers made their own . With no resistance to tropical diseases such as malaria and dysentery , the crews of early visiting ships were often decimated . In return , the sailors and settlers introduced alcohol and venereal diseases to the local populations . <p> The nation 's northernmost tropical settlement , Darwin , or Palmerston , as it was until 1911 , was destroyed three times in its short history . After the failure of a shifting string of settlements in the early 19th century which were attempted largely to thwart any ambitions the French might have had in the region , a port was permanently established in 1869 . <p> Initial British interactions with the local inhabitants were undeniably brutal , going a long way to justifying the description of the colonists as invaders rather than settlers . The settlements themselves were largely built on Chinese labour , followed in Darwin by hardier Mediterranean races such as the Greeks . <p> This was an environment hardly conducive to great literature . Most early accounts were non-fiction reports composed by visiting government officials , travel writers and journalists .
