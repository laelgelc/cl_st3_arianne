<p> Petaluma , CA--Restoring forests has become a world-wide strategy for simultaneously addressing the challenges of climate change and biodiversity conservation . In a new study , scientists at Point Blue Conservation Science assessed how successful restoration efforts in California 's Central Valley were at these two goals . Key among the findings was the conclusion that , in some cases , optimizing for carbon storage may come at the expense of biodiversity . <p> Forests store tremendous amounts of carbon in the trees and soil , and they can provide valuable habitat for wildlife . But reforestations designed to maximize carbon storage may not be as successful at conserving biodiversity . Researchers found that areas with the highest densities of trees had more carbon stored in trees ( as expected ) , but that bird density and diversity were lower . So , decisions about planting density , thinning , burning , and other actions may increase one benefit at the expense of the other . <p> " Going into the study , we understood that restored forests of any sort are generally better for carbon storage and biodiversity than agricultural fields or cleared land . They conducted surveys in 4 study areas ranging in size from 70 - 370 acres . These included a remnant forest at least 80 years old , a 30-year old planted forest , a forest that is naturally regenerating after levee breaches 22 and 32 years ago , and an area currently undergoing restoration . <p> The study found that after 20 - 30 years , restored riparian forests were similar to the remnant forest , storing 170 - 285 more tons of carbon per acre in the trees than the youngest study area . They also stored twice as much soil carbon and provided habitat to 4 times as many birds .
