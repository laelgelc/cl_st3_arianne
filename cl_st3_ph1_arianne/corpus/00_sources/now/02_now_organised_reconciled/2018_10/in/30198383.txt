<h> The Kedarnath Wildlife Sanctuary is a treasure trove of moth species that act as a vital indicator of biodiversity <p> As a 14-year-old , my first trekking experience was going up to the Kedarnath Temple from Gaurikund in Uttarakhand . Little did I realize then that an untiring inquisitiveness for moths would bring me back to this landscape many years later . The Kedarnath Wildlife Sanctuary ( KWS ) , spread over 975 sq . km , houses the most fascinating biodiversity as well as medieval temples . With over 200 different species of rhododendrons ( locally known as the buransh ) , the state flower of Uttarakhand , the area is resplendent in several shades of red and pink . Vast swathes of lush green oak ( banj ) forests cover the gentle slopes of the mountains . Spread over Chamoli and Rudraprayag districts of the state , the sanctuary is one of the most prominent protected areas in the western Himalayas , and home to the endangered musk deer . <p> The region was previously unexplored for its moth fauna , and the complex and dynamic nature of the insect 's ecosystem intrigued me . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ are often described best by connecting them to their popular cousins , the day-tripping butterflies . <p> A Himalayan Monal . The 14km trek to the shrine , starting from the roadhead at Gaurikund , passes through the sanctuary . The other medieval Shiva shrines in the sanctuary area that attract devotees are those of Mandani , Madhyamaheshwar , Tungnath , Ansuya Devi and Rudranath . <p> Apart from the pilgrims , many birders and wildlife photographers also throng the area to get the desired " eye-level shot " of the Himalayan monal , the koklass pheasant , as well as the musk deer and the Himlayan tahr , among other high-altitude fauna . I have been studying this in the western Himalayas for the past four years , but this area had remained unexplored . I was keen to understand moths in a scenario where humans and nature are at crossroads . <p> Looking out for moths was an engrossing experience in this landscape , with its varying habitats , ranging from temperate , coniferous and alpine forests to alpine meadows ( bugyals ) . The sanctuary also possesses a diverse climate and topography , with dense forests of pine , rhododendron , birch , and banj oak . It was a normal light-trapping session within an oak-dominated forest , when right towards the end of the sampling hours we went to check the trap and Prabhat Singh Bisht , my field assistant , exclaimed " Itna bada moth aaya trap pe ( such a huge moth is at the light trap ) . These markings serve as a defence mechanism to confuse echolocating bats which prey upon them . Another night , just when I was wrapping up my trap , with startling sambar and barking deer calls piercing through the silence of the forest , a peach blossom moth ( Thyatira batis ) settled on the wire attached to the light . This behaviour has been reported in the wood tiger moth ( Arctia plantaginis ) and several other tiger moths ( Family Erebidae , subfamily Arctiinae ) . The sanctuary region is moist and oak dominated , so there 's an abundance of the gypsy moth group ( genus Lymantria ) , where the males are dark brown and smaller compared to the females with their white wings and black markings . Mountains have always been considered centres of spectacular biological diversity and the Himalayas are certainly so . But human activities are threatening this important landscape . Moths have been proven to act as indicators of forest health , and this is extremely important in the present situation of habitat degradation and climate change . Studying their ecology would be helpful to understand the changes in the ecological hierarchy . But , very often the conservation efforts for moths would be directed towards the Himalayas as a unit , but because of the inherent diverse nature of this mountain system , area-specific conservation should be targeted .
