<h> Alarm as China eases 25-year ban on rhino horn and tiger parts <p> ANIMAL conservationists are alarmed over China 's decision to partially reverse a ban on the trade of rhino horn and tiger bones . <p> Rhinos and tigers are both endangered in the wild and China prohibited their trade in 1993 . <p> But on Monday , the China State Council said parts from captive animals would be authorised for scientific , medical and cultural use . <p> The World Wildlife Fund ( WWF ) is among those who have expressed worry that this will increase demand for the animals and jeopardise efforts to protect them . <p> Rhino and tiger parts are highly valued in traditional Chinese medicine . They are prescribed to treat a large variety of ailments including fever , gout , insomnia and meningitis , though any benefits have not been proven . <p> In its statement announcing the replacement of the 25-year old ban , the council said powdered forms of rhino horn and bones from dead tigers could be used in " qualified hospitals by qualified doctors " . <p> The animal products can only be obtained from farms , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ " antiques " could be used in cultural exchanges if approved by the cultural authorities , the statement adds . <p> The WWF said in a statement that the move would have " devastating consequences " and be an " enormous setback " to efforts to protect the animals in the wild . <p> " Even if restricted to antiques and use in hospitals , this trade would increase confusion by consumers and law enforcers as to which products are and are not legal , and would likely expand the markets for other tiger and rhino products , " WWF said . <p> Rachel Nuwer , author of the book ' Poached : Inside The Dark World of Wildlife Trafficking ' , said on Twitter : " This could spell game over for the world 's 30,000 remaining rhinos and 4,000 remaining tigers . " <p> She argued that trade in legal endangered species products provides cover for parts from animals that have been illegally killed . <p> Meanwhile , a new WWF report says " exploding human consumption " has caused a massive drop in the global wildlife population @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ losses in vertebrate species - mammals , fish , birds , amphibians and reptiles - averaged 60% between 1970 and 2014 . <p> " Earth is losing biodiversity at a rate seen only during mass extinctions , " the WWF 's Living Planet Report said . <p> It urged policy makers to set new targets for sustainable development . <p> The Living Planet Report , published every two years , aims to assess the state of the world 's wildlife . <p> The 2018 edition says only a quarter of the world 's land area is now free from the impact of human activity and the proportion will have fallen to just a 10th by 2050 . <p> The change is being driven by ever-rising food production and increased demand for energy , land and water , it says . <p> Although forest loss has been slowed by reforestation in some regions in recent decades , the loss has " accelerated in tropical forests that contain some of the highest levels of biodiversity on Earth " , the report notes . <p> Marine freshwater species are particularly at risk , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ lakes , rivers and wetlands - have seen an 83% decline in numbers since 1970 , according to the report . <p> The WWF calls for " a new global deal for nature and people " similar to the 2015 Paris agreement to tackle climate change by reducing greenhouse gas emissions . <p> " Decision makers at every level need to make the right political , financial and consumer choices to achieve the vision that humanity and nature thrive in harmony on our only planet , " the report says . <p> The data , gathered from peer-reviewed studies , covers more than 16,700 populations belonging to 4,000 species around the world .
