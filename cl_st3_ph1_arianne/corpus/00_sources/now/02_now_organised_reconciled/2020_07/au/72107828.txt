<h> Opinion <h> God save the Australian Republican Movement <p> I 've just realised that the Governor-General of Australia works for the Queen of England . He 's her representative , apparently . I know you 'll think me a bit dim , but it was something I knew but did n't really know . <p> Then I read the so called Palace Letters -- the belatedly released correspondence between the Governor-General and Buckingham Palace around the dismissal of the Whitlam government . <p> I 'm now on the verge of writing to Peter FitzSimons to sign up to the republicans . <p> Gough Whitlam on November 11 , 1975 - the day he was dismissed.Credit:Hulton Archive <p> Fitz has never managed , thus far , to draw me into the ranks . I viewed such constitutional reform as a second-rank issue , well behind climate change and Indigenous recognition . The Australian Governor-General worked for the Australian people , I thought ; the connection with London was a quaint historical leftover , made more charming by the excellent qualities of the current Queen . <p> Advertisement <p> Then I read the letters . <p> Ever since they were @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ what they reveal -- either " nothing " or " plenty " -- each side waving dramatic quotes . Kerr , it emerges , acts like an ambassador for a foreign power , whose seat of power is London and whom he serves with obsequious loyalty . <p> Like an ambassador , he constantly briefs head office with all possible manner of local news . At one point , when Kerr fears he is writing too often , he is reassured : " keep at it " . <p> Remarkably , it took a lengthy court battle , waged by the indomitable Jenny Hocking , to see the letters released -- 45 years after the events they describe . Some of us , after all , are old enough to remember that day . <p> On November 11 , 1975 , I was a schoolboy living two suburbs away from Parliament House and rushed down to see the drama . I missed the famous moment -- " nothing will save the governor-general " -- but , by the time I arrived , I saw crowds that were growing in size and in anger . <p> We all assumed there 'd be protests , a repeat of the marches that helped end Australia 's involvement @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . It was exciting and terrifying and baffling all at once . <p> The crowd stayed late into the night , at which point the affable Fred Daly , legendary Labor MP , walked out onto the steps , gave a long , passionate , occasionally funny speech , and told us all to go home . <p> Then ... nothing happened . The people failed to rise up . There were arguments in every pub , and in front of every TV set , but that was as far as it got . No protests of any substance . No strikes . <p> Stranger still , a large group of Australians , previous supporters of Whitlam , switched sides in the election of December 1975 . <p> According to Horne , the thinking went this way : for the Queen 's representative to sack him , Whitlam really must have done something wrong .
