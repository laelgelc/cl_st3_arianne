<h> ' Weather whiplash ' is affecting water quality in Lake Simcoe watershed <p> Dana Eldon , outreach co-ordinator with the Lake Simcoe Regional Conservation Authority , with climate-change materials available at the Bradford West Gwillimbury Public Library . Miriam King/Bradford Today <p> The climate is changing . <p> According to Environment Canada 's senior climatologist Dave Phillips , we are experiencing " weather whiplash " -- warmer summer temperatures extending into the fall , periods of drought , more intense storms resulting in heavy downpours , and more unpredictability in the weather . <p> In the Lake Simcoe watershed , the impact of climate change is complicated and magnified by rapid development and population growth , said Dana Eldon , education outreach co-ordinator with the Lake Simcoe Region Conservation Authority . <p> Eldon was at the Bradford West Gwillimbury Public Library earlier this week , outlining the complex picture of the effect of climate change on Lake Simcoe and its watershed , meaning an area of land where all the surface water drains into the same place . <p> The key is water quality , she said . <p> It is the water that sustains the diversity of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ estimated $200 million per year to local economies , she said . <p> There are also nine municipalities within the watershed that rely on the lake for their drinking water supply . <p> And now , said Eldon , with changing conditions , " there are places in the lake where young fish ca n't survive anymore . " <p> Warming water temperatures put cold-water fisheries at risk and reduce the amount of oxygen in the water , she said . <p> Runoff spikes in urban areas <p> The watershed population is expected to double within 30 years to more than 800,000 residents , Eldon said . <p> That means more rooftops , more roads , driveways , parking lots and shopping malls , and less opportunity for rainwater to soak into the ground . <p> Instead , the water runs into the storm-sewer system , carrying fertilizers , pesticides , oil , gas , and salt and sand from roads and parking lots . <p> The use of the term " storm sewer " is misleading , Eldon said , noting it suggests there is some kind of treatment @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <p> " For the most part , storm drains go out into the nearest stream , " or into stormwater management ponds , which are designed to manage the volume of water , not its quality , she said . <p> Everything dumped or flushed into storm sewers ends up in nearby rivers , and eventually , in Lake Simcoe . <p> To paint a picture of this , Eldon said 10-20 per cent of rainwater in a natural environment will become runoff , with 80-90 per cent soaking into the soil , eventually reaching groundwater . <p> In a subdivision , however , the split between runoff and absorption is 50/50 , and the runoff increases to 90-100 per cent of rainwater in a fully urban environment , she said . <p> That becomes a problem when an area receives " almost a month 's rain in one or two storm events , " Eldon said .
