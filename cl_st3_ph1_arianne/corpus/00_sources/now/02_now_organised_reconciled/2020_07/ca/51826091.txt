<p> It 's no surprise the Halifax Board of Police Commissioners had a jam-packed agenda for its meeting last week . What was surprising , and disappointing , was watching the board get bogged down in an absurd definition of " defunding the police " and fail to tackle the ambitious and substantive items on the agenda . <p> The board provides civilian governance and oversight for the Halifax Regional Police and also acts in an advisory capacity for the RCMP . The board has seven members , three of whom are municipal councillors ( Lisa Blackburn , Tony Mancini and Lindell Smith ) . <p> Under the Police Act , the board is required to " act as a conduit between the community and the police service providers " and " ensure that police services are delivered in a manner consistent with community values , needs and expectations . " <p> But is the board living up to its mandate ? <p> The community needs the board to exercise its oversight role now more than ever . There is growing evidence that the COVID-19 fines being handed out by police have disproportionately fallen on the most marginalized , @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ 's hunger for revolutionary change in policing . <p> What 's more , members of the public continue to be denied the opportunity to speak at the board 's meetings , ostensibly because the board has n't figured out how to arrange this over Microsoft Teams . <p> When the board met last week , the results were not promising . We refer , in particular , to a last-minute addition to the board 's agenda : a motion to adopt a definition of " defunding the police " that would include " police performing policing functions . " <p> This illogical and circular definition was concerning to those calling for a critical examination of where the public money devoted to police might be better spent to keep our communities safe and well . <p> Given that it was billed as a " staff motion , " it also remains unclear who drafted the definition , raising significant accountability concerns . <p> It was n't even necessary for the board to come up with its own definition . Common themes appear in every legitimate definition of " defunding the police @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ conduct , like small-scale drug possession , on the recognition that criminalization has historically targeted Black , Indigenous , and poor communities ; <p> an end to " broken windows " models of policing , which have been debunked as ineffective ; <p> the demilitarization and disarmament of police units to prevent the use of force that has been increasingly levied on Black and Indigenous peoples in Canada ; and <p> the redirection of funds currently used to support policing into initiatives that support community safety , provide safe and secure housing for all , and offer mental health and addictions services . <p> Calls to defund the police are not new -- even in Halifax . In January , El Jones gave a presentation to the board where she identified the worrying trend of rising police budgets across Canada and called for a freeze to the HRP budget until such time as the force could demonstrate that it had ended all practices of racial profiling . <p> The commissioners had another common-sense definition before them at their most recent meeting . In a presentation to the board , HRM @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ DeRico Symonds defined " defunding the police " as " reallocating money from police and reinvesting into : mental health supports , education , social services , housing , prevention programming , anti-racist education , food security and alternatives to policing . " <p> Rather than engaging with the substance of what " defunding the police " could actually look like in Halifax , the board discussed the ill-fated definition motion -- which did n't even end up making it to the floor . The board ultimately added a similar motion to the agenda for its next meeting on July 20 . This kind of procedural wrangling risks jeopardizing community confidence in the board . <p> One way for the board to fulfill its responsibilities to the community would be to work together with HRM Regional Council to hold public consultations on systemic racism and the future of policing in Halifax . A useful precedent might be the community consultations held by Dr. Scot Wortley and the Nova Scotia Human Rights Commission , leading up to the Wortley Report on street checks . <p> The recent efforts of the Edmonton @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ council held five days of virtual hearings on racism and policing in the city , with 142 Edmontonians testifying . Many spoke to experiences of victimization at the hands of police . Others provided a myriad of proposals for reforming police practices , improving accountability and redistributing funds . <p> The council ultimately approved 20 actions to reform policing , including reallocating $11 million from the police budget to grants for supportive housing to end homelessness and creating a citizen-led task force to recommend a new community safety plan for the city . <p> HRM has already demonstrated its ability to engage in bold and ambitious planning on significant , inter-jurisdictional issues such as climate change , so there 's no reason why we ca n't replicate Edmonton 's success in this respect . <p> If the board wants to meet its statutory obligations to the community , listening to the community would be a good place to start . <p> Harry Critchley is a law student at Dalhousie University and a member of the steering committee for the Nova Scotia Policing Policy Working Group ( NS PPWG ) . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ of the NS PPWG.
