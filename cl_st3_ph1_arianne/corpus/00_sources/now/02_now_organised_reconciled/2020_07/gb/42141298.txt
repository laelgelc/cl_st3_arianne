<h> California dreaming : A plan to phase out petrol and diesel cars that might actually work <p> The idea based on successes in California -- the home of Tesla ( via Getty Images ) <p> Transport is now the UK 's biggest source of climate-warming greenhouse gases . <p> While other sectors slash their carbon footprint , our cars alone continue to produce 15 per cent of annual emissions -- and the figure is still rising . <p> To tackle this , earlier this year the government announced plans to bring forward a ban on new petrol and diesel cars to 2035 . The response was an outcry from carmakers , who accused ministers of having a " date without a plan " . <p> Right now , they have a point . Britain 's car industry makes 1.3m cars annually , while Brits buy 2.3m per year , 247,000 of which are British-made . Transforming the fundamentals of such a huge industry in just 15 years is no small task . <p> The industry needs a chance to adapt , to retrain workforces and to retool supply chains . The first , pioneered by Norway , could be called the " kitchen sink " approach : a smorgasbord of tax reliefs and special privileges for owners of zero-emission vehicles . It 's expensive , but it works . Electric vehicles make up 61 per cent of new car sales in Norway , and it aims for 100 per cent by 2025 . <p> The problem , of course , is that the post-pandemic world wo n't have a lot of money to spend . Furthermore , the myriad interventions of the kitchen sink approach can distort the market and have unintended consequences . <p> Our new Policy Exchange report , published this week , argues that there is another way , based on successes in California -- the home of Tesla . This approach , known as a Zero-Emission Vehicle ( ZEV ) Mandate , can be brilliantly effective -- and much cheaper . <p> A ZEV Mandate works like this . Those which can not produce enough ZEVs can buy credits from those which do . This makes ZEVs relatively cheaper to make and buy , which also makes Britain more attractive to ZEV producers like Tesla . <p> The percentage starts low , but increases each year . Eventually it reaches 100 per cent , so that petrol or diesel cars are no longer sold . Manufacturers can see what 's coming -- they need to start making ZEVs , increase their prices or make way for ZEV manufacturers which can meet demand . <p> The trajectory is clear , flexible , and relatively technology-neutral . <p> Crucially , it allows Britain 's car industry to start now without massive subsidies .
