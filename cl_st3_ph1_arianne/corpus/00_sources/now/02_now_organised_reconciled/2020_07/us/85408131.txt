<p> Christine Lagarde told the Financial Times last week she wants the European Central Bank she leads " to look at all the business lines and the operations in which we are engaged in order to tackle climate change . " That could include shifting ECB bond purchases under its quantitative easing ( QE ) programs to favor debt issued by environmentally correct companies . Governor Andrew Bailey suggested this month that the Bank of England might also weigh green concerns more heavily in its QE corporate-bond portfolio . <p> The appeal to climate activists is obvious . Central banks are among the most powerful economic institutions , with cash to burn and minimal accountability to voters who often are skeptical of expensive green follies . <p> Environmentalists also fret that as central banks wade deeper into corporate debt markets , monetary authorities tilt the scales in favor of fossil fuels . Traditional energy companies issue large quantities of widely traded debt for central banks to buy on the secondary market , and they usually have the credit rating required to be eligible for purchase . Green-tech firms often strike out on both counts . <p> This distortion is @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ corporate debt , but instead officials increasingly want to tilt the scales in the other direction . But the costs will be enormous , and the biggest will be the blow to central-bank independence . <p> Ms. Lagarde , Mr. Bailey and their global peers have undermined their institutions ' hard-won independence enough with existing QE programs . The more engaged central banks become in pricing and allocating capital , the more exposed they are to political pressure when some leader or other thinks they 're getting it wrong . Ms. Lagarde faces criticism that her pandemic policies are a subsidy for Italian government debt .
