<h> St. Pete has big decisions to make regarding climate change and development Editorial <p> How much density is too much in the most threatened areas ? <p> What should St. Petersburg look like in 40 years , when sea level rise becomes less of a coming threat and more of a reality ? That 's the question that City Council members will need to debate at their committee meeting on Thursday . Will the city 's leaders declare any area threatened by coastal flooding and sea level rise to be untenable for re-development ? Or can appropriate safeguards be put in place to cautiously and thoughtfully increase density in threatened areas ? It 's not an easy decision , as both sides have merit . But at this point in the process , an unconsidered " no " to all development in those areas is the wrong approach . <p> Roughly 40 percent of St. Petersburg is inside the Coastal High Hazard area , a designation that reflects whether storm surge from a Category 1 hurricane could cause flooding . The zone is geographically diverse and stretches from the Carillon area south through Gandy Boulevard to the University @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Marina district . Current city rules bar increasing the maximum amount of residential dwelling units allowed in the Coastal High Hazard area . But city staff began to look for options as the zone grew due to updates to flooding projections and parts of St. Petersburg became increasingly developed . <p> The proposed Coastal High Hazard development areas for St. Petersburg . The policy does not mean that single-family homes can suddenly be turned into towering structures that house dozens of people . And the increased density would only be allowed in 30 percent of the Coastal High Hazard zone classified as high-traffic areas like activity centers or busy corridors , including downtown and parts of Fourth Street N. The new policy would also require all new development in the area -- not just projects seeking an increase in density -- to adhere to stricter building standards than in other parts of the city . But banning any increase in density in large swaths of a growing city could have widespread economic impacts . For one , it could make increasing the stock of affordable housing much more difficult . A continued ban on increasing density also makes it harder to attract re-development in areas that desperately need it . The key will be to find the right balance , which ca n't be done if the council rejects the idea without thoughtful consideration . <p> City planners do n't have a great answer for every question , including how they will grapple with more people evacuating during a hurricane . Their answer so far : Each development will have to have a hurricane evacuation and re-entry plan for residents before it can be built . " It wo n't be easy for a development to make it to the end of the application process , " said DeLisle , who added that it took an " enormous amount of time " for city staff to come up with the Coastal High Hazard proposal . " There 's nothing knee-jerk about it . " <p> St. Petersburg has tough decisions to make when it comes to where environmental and economic concerns meet . Council members should n't be too quick to take sides or to rule out the proposed plan before it gets a full airing . Sounds bites and tired tropes wo n't do anymore . It 's time for the hard conversations , the ones that will have lasting impact on the city .
