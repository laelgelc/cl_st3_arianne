<h> Sanctions in the era of pandemic <p> US President Donald Trump is seen as he delivers a statement about Iran in Washington , US on January 8 , 2020 Kevin Lamarque/Reuters <p> Millions of people in the United States have long known what it is like not to be able to buy food , or soap , or toilet paper . Some because they came here from much poorer places , others because they are among the 140 million poor or low-income people living in the country today . <p> Now , millions more of us are experiencing this scarcity for ourselves due to the COVID-19 crisis , as panic-buying cleans out supermarket shelves , healthcare workers plead for masks and gloves , and hospitals and states are forced to bid against each other for scant supplies of ventilators . <p> We are learning what it feels like not to be able to get what we need . The difference is that economic sanctions are designed to make people 's lives unbearable . The goal of US sanctions - in Iran , in Venezuela , and beyond - is precisely to destroy the lives of ordinary people , in hopes they will rise up in favour of whatever regime change Washington is looking for . <p> Long before the COVID-19 pandemic , and long before Washington cancelled its funding of the World Health Organization ( WHO ) , these US sanctions were already violating human rights and international law . The UN Human Rights Council ( UNHRC ) determined that economic sanctions " can have far-reaching implications for human rights , " including " on the right to life , the rights to health and @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ and the right to an adequate standard of living , food , education , work and housing . " UNHRC noted , in particular , its alarm at " the disproportionate and indiscriminate human costs of unilateral sanctions and their negative effects on the civilian population , in particular women and children . " <p> Along with violating international law , economic sanctions do not even work for the purpose they are ostensibly imposed . There is no historical example of US economic sanctions persuading a local population to rise up and overthrow their government in response . <p> Not in Iraq , where 12 years of crippling US-led sanctions led to the deaths of more than 500,000 children in between Washington 's two Iraq wars . Not in North Korea , where decades of US sanctions and other pressures helped keep the country isolated and impoverished . Certainly not in Cuba , where decades of blockade have not prevented Cuban medical missions from travelling the world to assist in crisis response work , while providing some of the best healthcare in the hemisphere for its own people . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ continues to impose sanctions that destroy the lives of tens of millions - even as the coronavirus wreaks new levels of havoc on the lives of the poorest and most disenfranchised among them . <h> Pressuring Iran <p> US sanctions are the key weapon in Trump 's " maximum pressure " campaign against Iran . They have been tightened repeatedly . <p> Those sanctions have been in place for decades . Before the Obama administration signed the Iran nuclear deal in 2015 , they had already caused great suffering among ordinary people . In 2012 , then-UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon reported that " the sanctions imposed on the Islamic Republic of Iran have had significant effects on the general population , " including food and medicine shortages . <p> The nuclear deal led to the lifting of some of those sanctions . But when Trump withdrew from the deal in 2018 , they were slapped back on again , along with devastating new sanctions since . <p> Officially , humanitarian items like medicine are exempted .
