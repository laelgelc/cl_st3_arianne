<h> Life in the shallows becomes a trap for baby sharks <p> Scientists can now explain how baby reef sharks tolerate living in the sometimes-extreme environments of their nurseries -- but , they also say these habitats face an uncertain future which may leave newborn sharks ' trapped ' . <p> The lead author of the study is Ian Bouyoucos , a Ph.D . student at the ARC Centre of Excellence for Coral Reef Studies at James Cook University ( Coral CoE at JCU ) . <p> Though the word ' nursery ' conjures images of soft , nurturing environments , these shallows are anything but . The habitats can be ' extreme ' , with dramatic changes in temperature and oxygen levels . <p> " It 's not a nice place really , in terms of environmental conditions , " said co-author Associate Professor Jodie Rummer , also from Coral CoE at JCU . The extremes of the shallows can mean high temperatures and low oxygen levels , which can be a strain at best . <p> " But we found the growth rate and metabolism of baby sharks is resilient to the temperature changes they currently face @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ <p> " We also found the sharks with a greater tolerance for higher temperatures had a greater tolerance for low oxygen levels , which is really promising . " <p> Dr. Rummer said mother blacktip reef sharks usually give birth close to shore . There may be , at most , only four pups born at a time with perhaps only one surviving . <p> " Essentially from the day they are born , these sharks have to be pretty tough in how their bodies work in order to tolerate these harsh environmental conditions , " Dr. Rummer said . <p> The research was conducted within the largest shark sanctuary in the world -- the French Polynesian shark sanctuary . The top threat to sharks worldwide is overfishing , but sharks are protected within the 4.8 million square kilometre sanctuary . <p> Though the young sharks appear resistant to extreme changes the authors warn , as waters get warmer with climate change , future populations are threatened . If they remain within the safe , shallow nurseries , they suffer the effects of warming waters and decreasing oxygen levels . A trap , indeed . " <p> Mr Bouyoucos says while these sharks can cope with these challenging conditions now , they are approaching their limits . <p> " There are already extreme fluctuations and extreme highs going on in the shallows -- and the conditions are only getting worse , " he said . <p> " We have to ask , will reef sharks continue to adapt and evolve over generations at a pace that 's fast enough to keep up with climate change ?
