<h> The coronavirus-climate-air conditioning nexus <p> This summer , America will be hemmed in by a climate emergency on one hand and a continuing deadly pandemic on the other . Meanwhile , humming away in the background , aggravating our plight , will be that longtime summer friend : air conditioning . <p> Climatic models are projecting that heat waves will be more frequent and more intense than usual across the United States this summer . As pandemic-induced restrictions are relaxed , severe heat likely will drive most social gatherings and group activities into the air-conditioned indoor world . There , the risk of coronavirus spread will be much higher than it is in a park or on a porch . <p> These days , it 's always riskier to gather indoors than outdoors . Research says that indoor spaces can be made somewhat safer simply by opening windows for ample ventilation . That wo n't work in air-conditioned buildings , which must be zipped up tight . <p> ADVERTISEMENT <p> Air conditioning raises the risk further by lowering the indoor relative humidity . When humidity is high , virus particles are carried inside bulky saliva droplets that fall to the floor or other surfaces within seconds . But with low humidity , they are in much smaller droplets called aerosols that stay airborne far longer , ready to be inhaled . <p> Some types of cooling systems also serve to circulate droplets very efficiently and potentially could infect large numbers of people . A widely cited case study found that in January , one customer at a restaurant in Guangzhou , China , infected nine other diners sitting at three different tables with COVID-19 . The breeze from an air conditioner near one of the tables had efficiently distributed virus-laden droplets along a 20-foot-long path . <p> There are numerous cases in which air conditioning has caused health problems by spreading bacteria and mold spores , which can be associated with so-called " sick building syndrome . It accounts for 17 percent of year-round home electricity consumption and the resulting emissions ; furthermore , the Energy Information Agency predicts that U.S. energy use for air conditioning will grow faster than any other use of energy in buildings of all kinds in coming decades . <p> The chain of causation forms a perfect circle . Greenhouse emissions from past decades -- including billions of tons of carbon dioxide emitted by air conditioning , aircraft and other technologies that also happen to be implicated in the pandemic -- may make this summer the hottest ever , prompting even more air conditioning use , which will further increase greenhouse emissions .
