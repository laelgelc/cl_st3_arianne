Bedrock rivers set rates and patterns of erosion in most landscapes , but constraints on river response to late Cenozoic climate change remain elusive . Here , we determine cosmogenic isotope and luminescence ages of well-preserved bedrock terraces along the Fortymile River ( Yukon River basin ) to reconstruct an 5 ? Myr history of fluvial adjustment to late Cenozoic climate and Yukon River headwater capture at 2.6 ? Ma . Post-capture Yukon River downcutting lowered the Fortymile River outlet , forcing subsequent bedrock incision throughout the Fortymile basin in two pulses , from 2.4 to 1.8 ? Ma and at @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ intervals of slow river channel sedimentation under near-consistent climate forcing from 4.8 to 2.4 ? Ma and from 1.8 to 1 ? Ma . The Fortymile River delivers sediment to the Bering Sea , where provenance and accumulation rate changes since 4.3 ? Ma match observed variations in incision . Our results link alluviation and incision to late Cenozoic climate steadiness and change , respectively , and support the hypothesis that climate-forced changes in precipitation and runoff fundamentally control the pace of river incision and landscape erosion . <h> Main <p> Bedrock river incision sets the pace and pattern of landscape evolution over most of Earth 's surface and is thought to tend toward a steady state of equilibrium with rock uplift rate over long ( 106 ? yr ) timescales1,2 . Strath terraces cut into bedrock preserve river channel remnants above the active channel and permit measurement of long-timescale histories of bedrock incision and concomitant uplift3,4,5 . Terraces also record the fundamental unsteadiness of river erosion over time in response to externally imposed base-level lowering ( for example , by tectonic uplift4,5 or drainage reorganization6,7 ) , because strath @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ vertical incision into bedrock3,8,9,10 . Although incision hiatuses may be stochastically triggered ( for example , by mass wasting events ) 9 , forcing by late Cenozoic climate change is believed to exert a primary control on the timing of terrace formation4,8,10 , the incision of bedrock channels1,2 and hence erosion of terrain within the drainage network11,12,13,14 . <p> Whether and how climatic change may affect the related processes of erosion , bedrock incision and terrace formation remain uncertain12,13,14,15,16 . Changes in late Cenozoic climate include evolution of orbitally paced glacial -- interglacial cycles and increases in the dominant period and amplitude of these cycles at 2.6 and 1 Ma17,18 . These late Cenozoic climate cycles may have forced periodic fluctuations in continental precipitation , for example , 19 such that concomitant variations in discharge link climatic forcing to erosional landscape response20,21 . Climate-forced variations in the ratio of discharge to fluvial sediment flux are thought to drive alternation between intervals of terrace-forming alluviation and bedrock incision across either individual climate cycles5,8 or increases in precipitation1 commensurate with the period and amplitude of forcing12,13,20 . Our data show profound synchronous shifts in fluvial process ( that is , alluviation to incision ) , rate and sediment export24 that occur across key frequency changes in late Cenozoic climate ( Fig. 3 ) . We sampled four shallow pits ( sample horizons 2 -- 5 ? m depth ) , one deep ( 22 ? m ) quarry in the high Fortymile River terrace gravels and three shallow pits in the middle Fortymile River terrace gravel ( Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1 -- 7 ) . We prepared samples at the University of Vermont44 and measured 26Al/27Al and 10Be/9Be ratios at the Purdue Rare Isotope Measurement Laboratory ( Supplementary Data 2 and 3 ) 45,46 . We corrected each isotope measurement for backgrounds by subtracting the average of all blank measurements from each sample ( Supplementary Data 4 and 5 and Supplementary Fig. 8 ) and propagated the standard deviation of the blank measurements into sample uncertainties ( Supplementary Data 2 and 3 ) . Resulting concentrations are equivalent within 1.5% to concentrations based on batch-specific blank corrections in place of the overall average blank ( Supplementary Fig. 9 ) . @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ concentrations and analytical uncertainties , with 10Be and 26Al on the x and y axes , respectively37,38 . Samples with a common pre-burial history contain 10Be and 26Al concentrations that reflect post-burial decay away from the surface production 26Al/10Be ratio ; a line thusly fit to these concentrations can be used to both ( 1 ) quantify the duration of post-burial decay and ( 2 ) identify and omit samples with dissimilar pre-burial history ( that is , outliers ) . We use a MATLAB implementation of the isochron approach38 that applies a linearization factor to correct post-burial production among the 10Be concentrations ; the y intercept reflects post-burial isotope production , but the linearization of 10Be preserves the slope associated with decay of the inherited pre-burial concentrations . We identified 2 of 47 total isotope concentration pairs as outliers ( &gt;2s ) below the regression and omitted these from the final linear fits to sample suites 17ALR306 and 16ALR233 ( Fig. 1d , i ) . We quantified basin-averaged erosion rates using 10Be concentrations measured in quartz from modern river sand ( 250 -- 850 ? ? m ) @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ Data 1 ) . The 10Be accumulates in quartz at Earth 's surface to depths commensurate with cosmic ray e-folding length ( 0.6 ? m in rock ) , at rates determined largely by latitude and altitude and inversely proportional to erosion rate40,41 . Dividing the 10Be production depth by a given measured erosion rate thus estimates the duration of erosion at the measured rate . Given similar rock type and durability throughout the Fortymile River basin and minimal lithologic variation within the sampled tributary catchments32,33,34 , we assume that quartz sampled at the 250- to 850- ? m grain-size range is well mixed and represents erosion throughout the tributary catchment upstream . We use a MATLAB likelihood estimation routine to compute the most likely elevation and latitude from pixel values within each sample catchment and use the CRONUS online calculator ( https : **26;170;TOOLONG ) to assess erosion rates based on 10Be concentrations measured in quartz sand from each outlet ( Supplementary Data 6 and 7 ) . Consequently , we assume minimal snow and topographic shielding effects on 10Be production rate and report uncertainties computed by CRONUS that reflect these assumptions ( that is , shielding factor = 1 ; Supplementary Data 6 and 7 ) . We used opaque metal pipes to collect luminescence samples from cutbank exposures of the modern floodplain , within 5-m elevation of the active channel , at three locations ( Supplementary Data 1 ) on the West Fork Fortymile River ( Supplementary Figs. 10 -- 12 ) . Mineralogy of the samples proved best suited for fading-corrected IRSL dating48 . IRSL sample processing and analysis was conducted at the Utah State University Luminescence Lab following standard procedures49,50,51,52,53 ( Supplementary Data 8 ) .
