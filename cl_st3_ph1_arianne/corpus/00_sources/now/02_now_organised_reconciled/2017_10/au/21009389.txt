<h> Thai hill products to be sold in IKEA <p> A social enterprise that employs hill tribe communities in Thailand has been boosted by a new partnership with IKEA . <p> Thin Lei Win <p> ReutersOctober 10 , 201712:01pm <p> Since leaving behind conflict and poverty in their native countries , Kam Kampun and Nittaya Soponprakobkit have not ventured beyond the forested hills in northern Thailand they now call home . <p> Without Thai ID cards , their movement is restricted . Their handmade creations , however , are set to travel the world . <p> Kampun , 60 , makes beautiful paper from mulberry bark and Nittaya , 32 , has woven striking black , white and grey textiles which are now on sale in IKEA stores across Europe . <p> The pair work for the Doi Tung Development Project , one of Thailand 's oldest and most established social enterprises , which employs hill tribe communities , many of whom are stateless . <p> From this month , their products can be bought at IKEA stores in six European countries as part of a limited-edition collection called EFTERTANKE @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ biggest home furnishings retailer . <p> This is the sixth and largest collection Doi Tung has produced for IKEA in a decade-long partnership . <p> Some 200,000 products - made of textiles , pottery and paper - have been crafted by about 330 artisans , three quarters of them women . <p> " I can not go anywhere ( in ) or outside the country , but my products can be sold in other countries , " Nittaya , a native of Yunnan in China who started helping her family farm opium aged six , told the Thomson Reuters Foundation . <p> In Thailand , a middle-income country of 66 million people , about 7 million live in poverty and one-third of 15-year-olds are illiterate , according to the World Bank . <p> There are nearly half a million stateless people , who are not recognised as nationals of any country , government figures show . <p> Nittaya 's family struggled to survive from opium farming , and migrated to Myanmar in search of work when she was about 11 .
