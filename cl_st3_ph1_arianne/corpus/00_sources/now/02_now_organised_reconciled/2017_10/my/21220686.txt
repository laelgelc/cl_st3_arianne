<p> THE International Monetary Fund ( IMF ) and climate change do not often appear in the same headline together . Indeed , environmental issues have been , at most , peripheral to IMF 's core functions . <p> But now , economists inside and outside the IMF are beginning to understand that climate change has significant implications for national and regional economies , and so , it 's worth reconsidering the fund 's role in addressing climate challenge . <p> To her credit , IMF head Christine Lagarde has boldly injected IMF 's voice into the global debate on policy responses to climate change and has identified a number of roles the fund can play . <p> IMF has conducted valuable work on how carbon emissions can be reduced through market prices that reflect the negative externalities of those emissions . In particular , it has become a leading voice for quantifying and streamlining , or eliminating fossil fuel subsidies , as well as for introducing carbon-pricing mechanisms . <p> What is still missing , however , is a bigger role for the IMF in enabling countries to prepare @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ are three things it could do , building on its current efforts , that would make a big difference : <p> DEEPEN research on macroeconomic and financial impacts of climate change . <p> In a climate change debate that has become heavily politicised , IMF 's technical and nonpartisan voice is uniquely valuable . Few questions are as important as understanding the possible effects of a changing climate on the world 's economies , especially the most vulnerable ones . <p> IMF has recently started to make important contributions in this area . In a paper published last year , it started to look into the implications of climate change on so-called " small states " . And , earlier this month , IMF devoted , for the first time , a whole chapter of its flagship World Economic Outlook to the impacts of weather shocks on economic activity . <p> Building on these foundations , IMF should focus its research capabilities on a key question , namely , whether climate change is having a " level effect " or " growth effect " on per capita income . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ a given amount of income over time ( think of damaged bridges and buildings ) , but not affect the capacity of the economy itself to grow . If the latter , then climate change is also harming the drivers of growth themselves , such as the productivity and availability of workers , the productivity of agriculture , and flow of investment . <p> The economy 's growth rate will slow as a result , and losses will compound year after year , leaving an economy significantly worse off than if only level effects applied . <p> Getting better answers to this question is essential for policymakers making decisions about how much to spend today to avoid damage tomorrow . <p> FORMALLY incorporate climate change into policy dialogue . <p> One of IMF 's core functions is macroeconomic surveillance . This function brings IMF staff into regular policy dialogues ( called Article IV consultations ) with financial authorities in virtually every country in the world . <p> Financial authorities have a key role to play in preparing for climate change , as they are charged with budget planning and managing @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ risk into the dialogue as a formal part of its consultations , not just with small states , but with a much larger set of vulnerable countries as well , including systemically-significant ones . <p> This year , in collaboration with the World Bank , IMF launched the first Climate Change Policy Assessment ( CCPA ) during the Article IV consultations for the Seychelles . <p> The assessment focused on policy options to reduce vulnerability to climate change ; the Seychelle authorities found it to be very useful . More CCPAs are planned -- a small handful per year -- but this is simply not fast enough given the urgency and gravity of the challenge . <p> IMF should formalise CCPAs as a routine part of Article IV consultations for a broad swathe of vulnerable , low-income countries . <p> This will require investing in staff capacity and training , including in its Monetary and Capital Markets Department , which can help countries identify how climate risks and opportunities could affect their financial systems . Maximising synergies with the World Bank on the CCPAs will also be necessary . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ facing a balance-of-payments crisis often draw on IMF resources and enter into a programme relationship with it . <p> One of the trickiest elements when negotiating such a programme is how to treat different categories of spending and where to cut to restore fiscal balance . <p> These include , for example , deposits into a national reserve fund , premium payments on sovereign insurance against natural disasters , or the costs of issuing catastrophe ( " cat " ) bonds . <p> Protecting some of these expenditures from programme-mandated cuts is fully appropriate , as they are designed to provide a measure of fiscal protection to the government in the aftermath of an extreme weather event . <p> For instance , IMF might treat cat bond issuance costs and insurance premiums as investments with potential upside , rather than as expenditures , thereby exempting them from cuts . <p> Lagarde has positioned IMF as an important and credible voice in the debate about climate change . Now it 's time for the fund to expand and institutionalise this new role , helping poor and vulnerable countries understand and confront the macroeconomic and financial risks of climate change .
