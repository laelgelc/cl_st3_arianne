<h> Annamarie Hatcher : Mating season begins <p> September marks the beginning of the rutting season for moose ( in Mi'kmaq : tia 'm ) in the Bras d'Or Lake Biosphere . This time is exciting for the amorous moose and the hunters . <p> The noisy antics of the excitable males may be one of the reasons that the September moon is called Wikumkewiku 's , or mate-calling time , in Mi'kmaw culture . <p> Another important ungulate ( hoofed animal ) that may have played a part in the naming of the September moon was the reindeer , or caribou ( in Mi'kmaq : qalipu ) . Although we have none in Cape Breton now , they played a significant role here in the past . <p> The caribou is one of the main characters in a story that includes the closely-related moose and white-tailed deer . <p> When Europeans first joined the Mi'kmaq in Cape Breton ( in Mi'kmaq : Unama'ki ) , moose were the largest members of the deer family here . However , the last caribou was seen at about the time that a new species , the white-tailed deer ( in Mi'kmaq : lentuk ) , became established on the island . Timing is everything , is n't it ? <p> A successful release of white-tailed deer in the Digby and Halifax areas during the mid 1890s was documented in the book " Deer of Nova Scotia ' by Benson and Dodds ( N.S. Dept. of Lands and Forests , 1977 ) . The herd thrived and was reported in all mainland counties by 1904 , and it first reached Cape Breton around 1911 . <p> The healthy deer population was rapidly growing just as the caribou population disappeared from the island . It may be coincidental . However , scientists propose that moose , caribou and white-tailed deer ca n't co-exist in close quarters because of a tiny brain worm with the scientific name of Parelaphostrongylus tenuis . <p> This parasite is common in deer and they @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ is fatal for many other ungulates such as caribou and moose . <p> Caribou are now out of the picture in Cape Breton and the moose seem to be keeping a healthy distance between their populations and those of the colonizing white-tailed deer . Currently we seem to be at an impasse in the Biosphere . <p> So , why is the tiny brain worm tolerated by white-tailed deer and not moose and caribou ? And , why has it been more of a problem in recent times ? Well , white-tailed deer and P. tenuis have co-existed for thousands of years and evolution has enabled the deer to adapt to this parasite . For the moose and caribou it is a relatively new threat and they have n't had time to adapt . <p> Brain worm fatalities in moose and caribou are becoming more of a problem as the climate warms . Warmer winters allow the populations of white-tailed deer and their tiny passengers to move further north across North America , invading traditional habitats of moose and caribou . <p> Another factor related to climate change is increased @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . The lifecycle of P. tenuis depends on the presence of certain species of snails and slugs , which are the intermediate hosts in the brain worm 's lifecycle . <p> So , moist ecosystems with warmer winters have enabled the white-tailed deer and their tiny brain worms to thrive and that may have contributed to the loss of the Biosphere 's caribou herds . What will happen in the future in the snail and worm-mediated impasse between the deer and the moose ? <p> Although we may place an educated guess , it is hard to predict . In nature , everything is connected and the nature of those connections is often very complex ! <p> Dr. Annamarie Hatcher is an adjunct professor at Unama'ki College , Cape Breton University , and a board member of the Bras d'Or Lake Biosphere Reserve Association .
