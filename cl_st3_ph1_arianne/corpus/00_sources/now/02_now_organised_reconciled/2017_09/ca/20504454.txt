<h> Since 1956 our GDPs and emissions have mostly increased apace . If we are to meet our 2030 emissions-reduction target , they must be " decoupled . " <p> For much of the post-Second World War period , the growth of many national economies was closely correlated with an increase in greenhouse gas ( GHG ) emissions . The strong coupling of economic growth and GHG emissions has been a major contributor to human-induced climate change . <p> During this period , Canada was no exception . Between 1956 and 1995 , Canada 's economy ( represented by gross domestic product , or GDP ) and GHG emissions exhibited a strong coupling , increasing by over 300 percent and 160 percent , respectively , according to Statistics Canada data . <p> In November 2016 , the federal government ratified the Paris Agreement on Climate Change , thereby pledging to meet Canada 's Intended Nationally Determined Contribution ( INDC ) , a 30 percent reduction in GHG emissions , from 735 megatonnes ( Mt ) in 2005 to 515 Mt in 2030 . <p> For Canada to achieve its INDC @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ 30 percent from the level in 2005 , by 2030 . The provinces will also have to decouple their economies from their emissions , since no province wants to sacrifice its economy to achieve its emissions target . <p> Ideally , a province will want to achieve a perfect decoupling , growing its GDP at a rate at least equal to the rate at which its emissions are declining . This implies that by 2030 not only should each province 's emissions be 30 percent below 2005 levels , its GDP should also be at least 30 percent higher . <p> Despite the wide range of emissions-reduction policies and regulations presently in place at the provincial level , the latest numbers from Canada 's 2017 National Inventory Report ( NIR ) indicate that , as of 2015 , Canada is not on track to either meet its INDC or to decouple its emissions from the economy .
