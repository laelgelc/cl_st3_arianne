<p> Once upon a time , between 5 and 6 million years ago , much of the Mediterranean Sea disappeared in what came to be known as the Messinian Salinity Crisis ( MSL ) , named after the age it took place in . Weirdly , as a new Nature Geoscience study reveals , it likely triggered a series of powerful volcanic eruptions too . <p> It may seem odd that the two dramatic events had any connection -- so in order to understand what it may be , let 's go back to when the crisis began . <p> The first half of this geological epic is well-known to scientists . Around 6 million years ago , the Strait of Gibraltar -- the gap between Spain and Morocco that connects the Mediterranean Sea with the Atlantic Ocean -- was closed off rather suddenly at the same time the area was experiencing a dry , warm climate . These factors meant that much of the water that evaporated off the sea was never replenished . Any pools of water that remained were so salty that they proved to be deadly to most types of life . <p> Around 5.3 million years ago , the crisis ended when the Strait of Gibraltar opened up again , triggering an Atlantic Ocean-based multi-step waterfall that was over a kilometer ( 0.62 miles ) high and had an outflow of around 1,000 times that of the modern-day Amazon River . Shortly afterwards , the Mediterranean Sea had returned . <p> An international team led by the University of Geneva suspected that this was n't all there was to the story . They suspected that such a massive loss of water would have had a rather bizarre secondary effect -- namely , it would have triggered volcanic eruptions nearby . <p> The premise is n't a new one . With all that water gone , any underlying magma chambers would have experienced a huge drop in confining pressure -- and , according to their numerical simulations , volcanism should have been more prominent back then . <p> The team needed to find several contemporaneous eruptions to back up this idea , and indeed they did : There were 13 eruptions around the Mediterranean at the time , twice the average volcanic activity in that part of the world . <p> Ultimately , this means that climate change has multiple ways of setting off volcanoes -- something we 'd do well to remember considering our prevailing addiction to fossil fuels .
