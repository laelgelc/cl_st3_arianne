<h> MAKING BREXIT WORK FOR THE YORKSHIRE DALES <p> COUNTRYSIDE campaigner and author , Colin Speakman , looks at Brexit , the possible dangers it poses , and how it could be made to work for the Yorkshire Dales . <p> When the Director General of a major conservation charity , the National Trust , also a major landowner in the Yorkshire Dales , warns about the uncertain future of upland farming because of Brexit , it is time to take such warnings seriously . <p> Dame Helen Gosh expresses her concern that farmers , worried about the future , may do everything they can to increase their cash flow , even at a cost of ploughing up " set aside " areas to maximise income . Farmers have every reason to be concerned . Support payments through Common Agricultural Policy will disappear once the UK leaves the EU in 2019 , and whilst the Government has " guaranteed " payments at a similar level until 2022 , five years is an impossibly short tine for any business , especially farming , to plan for the future when most decisions @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ worry is the loss of core markets . It is estimated , for example , that around 75 per cent of British lamb goes to EU countries . Faced with new tariff barriers , and the opening of the British market to cheap imports from highly efficient large-scale producers in New Zealand and elsewhere , the future for small family farms in the Yorkshire Dales could be bleak . Helen Gosh is clear . Echoing proposals by new Environment Minister Michael Grove , Brexit , if handled well , could provide opportunities as well as threats . Rather than paying landowners by acreage , and therefore rewarding the very rich disproportionately , any new support must be based in terms of public benefit . <p> The Trust proposes six core principles upon which future public subsidy for all landowners should be based : <p> 1 . Public money must only pay for public goods . There will need to be a transition to the new world but this basic income support payment should be removed . <p> 2 . It should be unacceptable to harm nature but easy to help @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ any public payment should be conditional on meeting higher standards of wildlife , soil and water stewardship . <p> 3 . Nature should be abundant everywhere . The new system needs to support nature in the lowlands as well as the uplands . <p> 4 . We need to drive better outcomes for nature . Nature needs joined up habitats on a landscape scale with subsidies implemented on a farm-by-farm basis . <p> 5 . Farmers that deliver the most public benefit should get the most . In the future , those farmers and land managers who get the most public money should be those who deliver the best environmental outcomes . <p> 6 . We must invest in science , new technology and new markets that help nature . Public money should help create ways of farming that benefit nature and help develop new markets to reward farmer for storing carbon , preventing floods and promoting biodiversity . <p> The Yorkshire Dales National Park Authority is already working with farmers in the Yorkshire Dales through what is described as High Value Nature Farming which recognises the economic value of @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ , mammals , insect and plant life . The Wensleydale Project , in partnership with the Rivers Trust and the local community , is also seeking new ways of combining farming and environmental protection . Nature-based tourism is not a threat to farming , but a potentially vital second source of income for a farm business . <p> In terms of a National Park there is another core principle that should be added , and that relates to public access to land , whether by the rights of way network or CROW Act Open Countryside . Farmers with busy public paths and areas of land where the public have access should receive direct payments based on both the extent of access over land , but also the degree to which the land is used in terms of numbers of visitors , and therefore pressure of erosion , and disturbance . A serious weakness of the 2000 CROW Act is that it focused on rights rather than responsibilities . Far from it , but they need to recognise that if the market for traditional products -- lamb , beef , wool , or dairy - are changing because of external forces beyond our control . Rather than letting events overtake them , they need to plan , and create new opportunities in partnership with bodies like the National Trust , the National Park and indeed the voluntary conservation movement who are also their key customers . <p> What is needed is some radical new thinking . Is sheep farming the only way of producing income on our hills ? Are there new higher-value crops that can also be produced ? The concept of " marginal lands " where altitude , poor soils and difficult growing conditions - including impacts of climate change - combine to make conventional farming difficult is an interesting one .
