<h> Choices made on the ground are behind the disaster of Hurricane Harvey <h> Nicole Hemmer <p> When the history books talk about Hurricane Harvey , the devastating storm that dropped more than 120 centimetres of water on south-east Texas , they 'll call it a natural disaster . When the insurance companies -- the ones that do n't go bankrupt -- categorise claims from the storm , they file them under " acts of God " . <p> But though the trillions of gallons of water dropped from the sky -- rainfall so immense that meteorologists had to add new colours to their maps , just as Australian meteorologists had to add new colours to capture record high temperatures in the country -- the devastation the storm wrought was as much a result of choices made on the ground . <p> In the past three years , Houston has experienced three 500-year floods . It is a vast slab of concrete covering swampland , marshes , and prairie . Those swamps and marshes once served as ecological sponges , soaking in excess rainwater and filtering it through the region . But those natural floodplains are now blocked by sheets of pavement , the result of development that has allowed the metro area to swell to more than six million residents . <p> As in other coastal cities across the world , overdevelopment has been a major issue in Houston for years , one that has already had significant , and costly , consequences . The city used to rely on groundwater to provide water to the area . But as the boomtown grew , the city sank , bringing it closer to sea level and making the land too compact to rely on local sources of water . Worse : they build new developments on flood-prone sites , backed by federally funded flood insurance . <p> Advertisement <p> In the US , the federal government is the primary provider of flood insurance , because floods are insanely expensive and the uptick in costs has driven nearly every private insurer out of business . But the subsidies are controversial , because they encourage people to build and buy homes in flood-prone areas . <p> And increasingly , more and more of Houston is flood-prone , not only because of all the paving and sinking that 's been happening , but because the world is getting hotter and the storms are getting worse . Policymakers have to confront a fairly straightforward reality : that while we can not perfectly predict storm strength or sea-level rises or average temperature increases , we know these things are happening and that something has to be done to safeguard lives , protect economic investment , and forestall future warming . <p> Yet the politicisation of climate changes means that not only is the US government aggressively silent on the issue , but also that there are a large number of policymakers who make their reputations on opposing any sort of climate-related legislation . Scott Pruitt , head of the Environmental Protection Agency , has suggested that any attempt to link Harvey with climate change is " opportunistic " . There have always been places where people should n't live , at least not in large numbers . The desert , for instance . Or flood plains . Or , increasingly , at sea level . Yet they do , and the choice to subsidise those decisions -- by redirecting water to Phoenix and Los Angeles , by subsidising the housing boom in Miami , by rebuilding New Orleans after Katrina -- simply raises the cost for saving lives in the future . <p> These are hard conversations to have in the wake of a tragedy like Harvey .
