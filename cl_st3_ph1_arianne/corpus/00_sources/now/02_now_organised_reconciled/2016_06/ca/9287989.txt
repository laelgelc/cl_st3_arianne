<h> Sylvain Charlebois : Pricey produce should be public-health issue <p> It is known that we do not eat enough produce . In fact , some surveys suggest that 59 per cent of Canadians do not eat their daily required portions of vegetables and fruits . <p> According to a recent study from two universities , Guelph and Dalhousie , it appears that percentage might have increased in recent months . The main reason is linked to higher retail prices . <p> Most vegetable and fruit prices have increased by more than 10 per cent in 12 months . More than 1,000 Canadians were surveyed on how they have coped with sticker shocks in the produce aisle . Due to higher produce prices , 26 per cent of Canadians have reduced their consumption of produce in the past 12 months . <p> This is an alarming rate as , unlike meats or other products , fresh produce has no substitutes , and more than 45 per cent of Canadians have considered or have purchased frozen produce because they can not afford fresh fruits and vegetables . <p> One @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ majority of lower-income households opted for juices as an alternative to vegetables and fruits . <p> Knowing that not all juices are equal , it raises the question as to what kind of juices consumers are looking for when produce prices are out of reach . <p> Another interesting fact about the study is how more access to data causes consumers to behave differently . The majority of respondents who walk into a store to buy produce without gathering any market data on price points were more likely to have walked away from the infamous cauliflower , the price of which nearly hit double digits . <p> Yet cauliflower prices were far from being the most volatile in recent months . Lettuce , tomatoes and oranges were much more volatile , which might have been recognized by savvier consumers . <p> Survey results suggest that consumers who visit stores more often were more likely to notice more volatile prices . <p> The same for those who use different technologies and sources . For example , it appears that smartphone apps and flyers are making consumers smarter and more rational . <p> @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ highly vulnerable to fluctuating produce prices , more so than other socio-demographic groups . <p> The majority of consumers with lower incomes think that price hikes are unjustified . It appears that climate change and the weak-currency argument identified as factors for higher prices by experts and industry were anything but convincing for those with less means . <p> As well , the less-educated and less-informed feel the same about recent price increases . <p> Most would admit that produce is not the most popular of food groups , but they are essential to good health . Ensuring that Canadians eat properly is critical to the well-being of our communities across the country . <p> A significant group of consumers wo n't hesitate to either walk away from the produce section or find less-than-appropriate alternatives when prices fluctuate . With what we know now in nutrition science , the situation can no longer be sustained . <p> Without intervening in price-fixing at the retail level , the state should proactively encourage consumers to purchase and consume produce regularly . Industry should proactively inform the public on how to cope with price @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ our currency , we are likely to see prices fluctuate hourly . Intelligent pricing that sets prices hourly , based on supply and demand , is almost upon us in Canada , something we see in other parts of the world . For the vulnerable to deal with the higher end of a price cycle , they need access to data . <p> Over the long term , our lack of domestic production should be dealt with . In recent months , financiers in Canada have funded greenhouse projects that have built year-round production capacity . This spells good news for us all . Climate change and a weaker currency only support a stronger case for greenhouses in our country . <p> Our mothers asked us to eat our vegetables because they cared . Now it is time we cared about the less fortunate who can not afford vegetables and fruits in their daily lives . <p> Sylvain Charlebois is dean of the faculty of management and a professor in food distribution and policy at Dalhousie University in Nova Scotia .
