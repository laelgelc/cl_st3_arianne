<p> More than 370,000 observations of seasonal " phenological " events such as flowering , migrations , mating and egg-laying were combined with national temperature and rainfall records <p> The results , published in the journal Nature , showed that species at different levels of the food chain respond to climate change in different ways . <p> ( Heather E Lowther/Centre for Eco ) <p> Those known as " secondary consumers " , such as predatory birds , fish and mammals , were less affected than other groups . <p> In contrast , " primary consumers " in the middle of the food chain were the most sensitive to disruptive changes such as spring arriving earlier . <p> These were species such as seed-eating birds and herbivorous insects whose fate was influenced both by the plant life at the bottom of the food chain and the predators at the top . <p> By 2050 , primary consumers will have brought their seasonal events forward by an average of 6.2 days , the scientists predicted . <p> ( John Giles/PA ) <p> This compared with a shift of 2.5 @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ @ . <p> Lead researcher Dr Stephen Thackeray , from the Centre for Ecology &amp; Hydrology , located at four sites in the UK , said : " This is the largest study of the climatic sensitivity of UK plant and animal seasonal behaviour to date . Our results show the potential for climate change to disrupt the relationships between plants and animals , and now it is crucially important that we try to understand the consequences of these changes . " <p> Co-author Dr Deborah Hemming , from the Met Office , said , " We are lucky in the UK to have a long history of people fascinated with observing and recording events in nature . <p> " By quantifying the relationships between these phenological records and climate data across the UK , we identify many phenological events that are extremely sensitive to climate variations . These provide ideal early indicators , or sentinels , for monitoring and responding to the impacts of climate variability and change on nature . "
